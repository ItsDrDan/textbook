Lecture Connection

Nobody expects you to memorize the ten intermediates. What the lecture does expect is that you can look at any step, in words or as structures, and answer the same questions every time: How many carbons are in my molecule? What type of reaction is occurring? Does it take or release energy, and how can I tell? What type of enzyme is needed? Is a coenzyme needed? This page turns the section above into that table.

In this course

The section above says glycolysis happens in the “cytoplasm”; on exams say cytosol. It also describes step 1 as an SN2 mechanism and step 5 through enol forms; you are not responsible for mechanisms, only for the reaction type and enzyme class. One correction: the thiol that activates the aldehyde in step 6 belongs to the enzyme itself (a cysteine side chain), not to coenzyme A. The coenzyme of step 6 is NAD+; coenzyme A first appears when pyruvate becomes acetyl CoA (Section 13.5).

Professor Tip

Turn your paper sideways and set up a table with four columns: What type of reaction is this? How can I tell? What class of enzyme is needed? What coenzyme is needed? The “how can I tell” column is the chemistry: ATP became ADP (phosphorylation, energy in); same formula in and out (isomerization); NAD+ became NADH and a C=O appeared (oxidation); water left and a C=C appeared (dehydration). The enzyme names give the class away: kinase = transferase, dehydrogenase = oxidoreductase, isomerase or mutase = isomerase, aldolase and enolase = lyase, and in digestion every -ase that uses water is a hydrolase. If you want the intermediates anyway, the lecture mnemonic is “Goodness Gracious, Father Franklin Did Go By Picking Pumpkins (to) Prepare Pies”: Glucose, Glucose-6-phosphate, Fructose-6-phosphate, Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate, Dihydroxyacetone phosphate, Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, 1,3-Bisphosphoglycerate, 3-Phosphoglycerate, 2-Phosphoglycerate, Phosphoenolpyruvate, Pyruvate.

The ten steps as a four-question table

Glycolysis is a 10-step linear pathway: glucose (6 C) → 2 pyruvate (3 C each). Steps 1–5 require energy (2 ATP spent to phosphorylate the sugar twice, then the six-carbon chain is split in two). Steps 6–10 generate energy, and because there are two three-carbon pieces, everything in the payoff phase counts twice: 4 ATP and 2 NADH. Net: 2 ATP + 2 NADH per glucose.

Count the carriers: steps 1 and 3 spend one ATP each (2 spent); steps 7 and 10 each make one ATP, twice (4 made); step 6 makes one NADH, twice. Net 2 ATP and 2 NADH. Substrate-level phosphorylation means ATP is produced from ADP by transferring a phosphate from a high-energy compound (the substrate), with no oxygen or electron transport chain required. In step 6 the oxidizing agent is NAD+: it takes the hydrogen the aldehyde loses. Only three steps (1, 3, and 10) are irreversible under cell conditions, and all three are kinase steps; they are the ones the cell regulates (below) and the ones gluconeogenesis must bypass (Section 13.10).

Practice: Step 9 converts 2-phosphoglycerate to phosphoenolpyruvate. Is this a hydration, dehydration, dehydrogenation, hydrolysis, or condensation? And in step 6, which species is the oxidizing agent: glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, NAD+, H2O, NADH, or 3-phosphoglycerate?

Answer
Step 9 is a dehydration: water is removed and a C=C forms (enolase, a lyase, no coenzyme). In step 6 the oxidizing agent is NAD+; it is the species that gets reduced (to NADH) while the aldehyde is oxidized.

Regulation of glycolysis

The amount of glucose broken down depends on the need for pyruvate and ATP. Glycolysis is regulated at its three irreversible kinase steps:

  • Hexokinase (step 1) is inhibited by high levels of its own product, glucose-6-phosphate. If G6P is not being used, glucose is not trapped in the cell.
  • Phosphofructokinase (step 3) is the main control point and is under allosteric control (Chapter 11): a regulator binds at a site other than the active site. It is inhibited by high levels of ATP and activated by high levels of ADP or AMP. Glycolysis exists to make ATP, so the ATP/AMP ratio is the sensible switch.
  • Pyruvate kinase (step 10) is inhibited by high levels of ATP or acetyl CoA. Plenty of acetyl CoA means Stage 3 already has fuel, so the cell stops making more pyruvate.

In one sentence: when the cell has plenty of energy (ATP, acetyl CoA, unused G6P), glycolysis slows; when it is energy-poor (ADP, AMP), glycolysis speeds up. Hormonal control of the same enzymes is in Section 13.8.

Example 1: Reading enzyme names for enzyme class

Problem. Classify each glycolysis enzyme into one of the six enzyme classes from Chapter 11: hexokinase, aldolase, phosphoglycerate mutase, enolase, pyruvate kinase.

Solution. Hexokinase and pyruvate kinase move a phosphate between ATP/ADP and a substrate: transferases. Aldolase splits a C–C bond without water or oxidation: a lyase. Phosphoglycerate mutase moves a phosphate from one carbon to another within the same molecule: an isomerase. Enolase removes H2O to form a C=C (a dehydration): a lyase.

Check: No hydrolases (no water is used to break a bond; digestion is where the hydrolases live) and no ligases (nothing is joined at the cost of ATP). Glycolysis uses only four of the six classes: transferase, isomerase, lyase, oxidoreductase.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Caries is glycolysis you can see. Streptococcus mutans and its neighbors in the plaque biofilm take up sucrose, glucose, and fructose, run glycolysis, and, being bacteria with no mitochondria and little oxygen under the biofilm, reduce pyruvate to lactic acid, just as your muscles make lactate. The Stephan curve plots what happens next: plaque pH falls within 2–5 minutes of a sugar rinse, dips below the critical pH of about 5.5 at which hydroxyapatite begins to dissolve (demineralization), and takes 20–40 minutes of salivary buffering to recover; frequent snacking or sipping soda means more acid attacks and keeps the curve below 5.5 for hours. Sucrose is doubly bad: besides being fermented, it is the raw material bacteria use to build sticky glucans that glue the biofilm to enamel. Saliva fights back. It is supersaturated with calcium and phosphate, so between attacks it redeposits mineral onto softened enamel (remineralization), and its bicarbonate buffers the plaque. Fluoride speeds remineralization and builds fluorapatite, and it does something else: at low pH it enters bacteria as HF, and inside the cell it inhibits enolase, the step-9 lyase of glycolysis; less phosphoenolpyruvate means less acid and less sugar uptake, so fluoride is also an antibacterial agent. Xylitol, a five-carbon sugar alcohol, is taken up but cannot be fermented, so it feeds no acid; chewing it in sugar-free gum also stimulates saliva flow. Patients with xerostomia (dry mouth), including those on some antidepressants, antihistamines, and beta blockers, lose that protection and need a tighter recall schedule.
