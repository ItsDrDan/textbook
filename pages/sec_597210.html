Learning Objectives

  • Describe how the carbon skeletons of amino acids enter the citric acid cycle, and classify amino acids as glucogenic or ketogenic (removing the nitrogen is covered in Sections 15.8 and 15.9).

Amino acids are the products of stage 1 of protein catabolism. Usually, amino acids are used to synthesize proteins and other substances that need nitrogen, e.g., nucleotides. Only about 10% of our energy is usually derived from amino acids. Still, when there is a shortage of carbohydrates and fats, e.g., during fasting or starvation, amino acids are used as an alternate energy source. Prolonged use of amino acids as an energy source may lead to the destruction of essential tissues.

Degradation of amino acids has two sets of catabolic pathways: one that deals with the processing of \(\ce{N}\) and the other that deals with the processing of the remaining \(\ce{C}\) skeleton of the amino acids.

Processing the Nitrogen

The nitrogen comes off first, because the body cannot oxidize nitrogen-containing compounds for energy. Transamination and oxidative deamination are taught in Section 15.8, and the urea cycle that turns the ammonia into urea in Section 15.9.

The link between the urea cycle and the citric acid cycle

The urea cycle and citric acid cycle are two separate cycles but are linked with each other. Aspartate that provides one \(\ce{N}\) in the urea cycle is produced by oxaloacetate transamination, which is an intermediate in the citric acid cycle. Fumarate is a product of the urea cycle that is an intermediate of the citric acid cycle and returns to it.

processing of \(\ce{C}\) skeleton of amino acids

\(\alpha\)-Ketoacids are left as \(\ce{C}\) skeleton of amino acid after transamination or oxidative deamination. For example, pyruvate is left after transamination of alanine and \(\alpha\)-ketoglutarate is left after oxidative deamination of glutamate, as described in Section 15.8. \(\alpha\)-Ketoacids are either used as precursors for the synthesis of other compounds, or they enter the citric acid cycle to produce \(\ce{CO2}\), \(\ce{H2O}\), and energy. \(\alpha\)-Ketoglutarate and oxaloacetate are \(\alpha\)-keto acids that are intermediates in the citric acid cycle and directly enter the cycle. Other \(\alpha\)-keto acids go through a series of reactions to convert into one of the intermediates in the citric acid cycle, or they convert to pyruvate or acetyl-\(\ce{CoA}\) that enter the citric acid cycle, as illustrated in Figure \(\PageIndex{3}\). Details of the conversion of \(\alpha\)-keto acids into intermediates of the citric acid cycle or pyruvate or acetyl-\(\ce{CoA}\) are not described here.

Amino acids that can degrade to pyruvate or oxaloacetate are called glucogenic because these products can form glucose through the gluconeogenesis pathway. For example, alanine yields pyruvate, and aspartate yields oxaloacetate, as shown in Figure 15.8.1. Amino acids that degrade to acetyl-\(\ce{CoA}\) or acetoacetic acid, which can not form glucose but can be converted into ketone bodies, are called ketogenic. Lysine and leucine are ketogenic amino acids. Several amino acids can catabolize to produce both glucogenic and ketogenic intermediates, and they fall into both classes, as shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{3}\).

The carbon skeletons: 20 amino acids, 7 products

With the nitrogen gone, each α-keto acid is a familiar molecule or becomes one. Each amino acid has its own degradation pathway, in one or several steps, but all of them end at one of seven products: pyruvate, acetyl CoA, acetoacetyl CoA, and the citric acid cycle intermediates α-ketoglutarate, succinyl CoA, fumarate, and oxaloacetate. Skeletons that end up as pyruvate or a citric acid cycle intermediate can be made into glucose by gluconeogenesis (Section 13.8): these amino acids are glucogenic. Skeletons that end up as acetyl CoA or acetoacetyl CoA cannot become glucose (there is no path from acetyl CoA back to pyruvate) but can contribute to fatty acids or ketone bodies: these amino acids are ketogenic. A few appear in both groups, either because they degrade by two pathways or because parts of the skeleton go different ways.

Professor Tip

“L” for ketogenic: Leucine and Lysine are the only two amino acids that are purely ketogenic. Everything else can make at least some glucose.

Practice: Transamination removes alanine’s amino group and leaves pyruvate. Transamination of aspartate leaves oxaloacetate. Leucine is degraded only to acetyl CoA and acetoacetyl CoA. Classify each amino acid as glucogenic, ketogenic, or both, and say why.

Answer
Alanine: glucogenic (pyruvate → glucose by gluconeogenesis). Aspartate: glucogenic (oxaloacetate is a citric acid cycle intermediate and a gluconeogenesis starting point). Leucine: ketogenic only (acetyl CoA and acetoacetyl CoA can become ketone bodies or fat but never a net amount of glucose).

One body, one metabolism: where the pathways overlap

You have now met three chapters of pathways. Step back and notice that they share a handful of molecules. Pyruvate connects glucose, alanine (and other glucogenic amino acids), and glycerol (through DHAP). Acetyl CoA is where glucose, every fatty acid, and the ketogenic amino acids all end up, and it is the starting material for fatty acids, ketone bodies, and cholesterol. Oxaloacetate and α-ketoglutarate are both citric acid cycle intermediates and the keto-acid partners of aspartate and glutamate, so the cycle doubles as the on-ramp for amino acid carbon and the source of carbon for nonessential amino acids. One arrow is missing on purpose: there is no pathway from acetyl CoA back to pyruvate, so fatty acids cannot be turned into glucose. That single fact explains why the brain needs ketone bodies in a fast and why gluconeogenesis must run on glycerol, lactate, and amino acids.

Summary

  • After transamination and oxidative deamination remove the nitrogen, each amino acid’s carbon skeleton becomes pyruvate, acetyl CoA, acetoacetyl CoA or a citric acid cycle intermediate.
  • Glucogenic amino acids give pyruvate or cycle intermediates that can be made into glucose; ketogenic amino acids give acetyl CoA or acetoacetyl CoA, which can be made into fatty acids or ketone bodies but not glucose.
  • The urea cycle and the citric acid cycle are linked through fumarate and aspartate, and the metabolism of carbohydrates, fats and proteins meets at pyruvate, acetyl CoA and the cycle intermediates.
