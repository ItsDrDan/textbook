The carbonyl group of an aldehyde or a ketone is a polar double bond just like the acyl group of a carboxylic acid derivative, i.e., \(\ce{R-\!\!{\overset{\overset{\Huge\enspace\!{\overset{\Large{\delta{-}}}{\ce{O}}}}|\!\!|\enspace}{\overset{\delta{+}}{\ce{C}}}}\!\!-R'}\) where the carbonyl \(\ce{\overset{\delta{+}}{C}}\) is an electrophile. However, this electrophile does not have a leaving group on it. It can add a nucleophile but usually needs either base-promoted or acid catalysis conditions. These two mechanisms are described next.

Base-promoted nucleophilic addition reactions

A nucleophile in its more reactive basic form \(\ce{Nu^{-}}\) in basic medium attacks the electrophilic carbonyl \(\ce{\overset{\delta{+}}{C}}\). Simultaneously, the \(\pi\)-bond breaks, as shown in step#1 of the mechanism below. The \(\ce{O^{-}}\) either re-establishes the \(\pi\)-bond, expelling the nucleophile, or it is neutralized by an acid-base reaction with any acid group present in the medium in step#2 of the mechanism shown below.

Remember, the medium is basic but amphoteric and has some acidic groups. If there is no sufficiently acidic group in the medium, an acid (\(\ce{HB^{+}}\)) is added after mixing the reactants for step#2 to happen. The carbonyl \(\ce{C}\) changes hybridization from sp2 to sp3, and \(\ce{C=O}\) group converts to an alcohol (\(\ce{-OH}\)) in this reaction. It is called nucleophilic addition reaction because the overall result is the addition of a nucleophile (\(\ce{HNu}\)) to a carbonyl (\(\ce{C=O}\)) group.

Cyanohydrin formation

The addition of cyanide (\(\ce{^{-}CN}\)) to aldehydes or ketones is an example of base-promoted nucleophilic addition. It converts the \(\ce{C=O}\) into cyanohydrin, i.e., a \(\ce{C}\) with a cyanide (\(\ce{-CN}\)) and hydroxyl (\(\ce{-OH}\)) groups on it. Two examples of cyanohydrin formation are shown below.

Acid-catalyzed nucleophilic addition reactions

The acid in the medium converts less electrophilic \(\ce{R-\!\!{\overset{\overset{\Huge\enspace\!{\overset{\Large{\delta{-}}}{\ce{O}}}}|\!\!|\enspace}{\overset{\delta{+}}{\ce{C}}}}\!\!-R'}\) to a more electrophilic \(\ce{R-\!\!\!\!\!\!{\overset{\overset{\Huge\enspace{\overset{\Large{+}}{\ce{O}}\ce{H}}}|\!\!\!\!\!\!\!|\enspace\enspace}{\overset{\delta{+}}{\ce{C}}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!-{R'}}\) form by protonating its \(\ce{O}\) in step#1. Neutral nucleophile can exist only in neutral (\(\ce{HNu}\)) form in the acidic medium. The neutral nucleophile (\(\ce{HNu}\)) attacks the carbonyl \(\ce{C}\) making a \(\sigma\)-bond and, simultaneously, the \(\ce\pi\)-bond breaks in step#2. Although the medium is acidic, it is amphoteric and has some basic groups that receive acidic proton on the incoming \(\ce{-\overset{+}{Nu}H}\) in step#3, as illustrated below.

The acidic medium converts bad leaving \(\ce{-OH}\)-group to a good leaving \(\ce{-\overset{+}{O}H2}\)-group in step#4. The \(\ce{-\overset{+}{O}H2}\)-group leaves as \(\ce{H2O}\), pushed by lone pair on \(\ce{-Nu}\) in step#5. Second \(\ce{HNu}\) attacks the electrophilic \(\ce{C}\) in step#6, and the acidic proton on the second incoming \(\ce{-\overset{+}{Nu}H}\) is removed by any base (\(\ce{:\!\!B}\)) in the medium in step#7. All the steps of the mechanism are reversible. Two situations can arise:

  1. if \(\ce{HNu}\) is in limited supply, equilibrium exists between the substrate and tetrahedral intermediate-II of step#3, and
  2. if \(\ce{HNu}\) is in excess, equilibrium exists between the substrate and the product of reaction#7.

The reaction can be made irreversible by removing \(\ce{H2O}\) from the products. The reaction can be reversed by mixing the acid catalyst and excess water with the product.

Hemiacetals and acetals

The addition of alcohol (\(\ce{R-OH}\)) to an aldehyde or a ketone is another example of a nucleophilic addition reaction. If \(\ce{R-OH}\) is in limited supply, the reaction stops after the addition of one \(\ce{R-OH}\) molecule resulting in a product called hemiacetal, as in the following example.

Hemiacetal has an \(\ce{-O-R}\)-group and an \(\ce{-OH}\) bonded to the same \(\ce{C}\). Hemiacetals are usually unstable and exist in small proportion at equilibrium. Exceptions are five-membered and six-membered hemiacetals formed by the reaction of (\(\ce{-OH}\))-group and the \(\ce{C=O}\)-group on the same molecule. For example, more than 99% of glucose molecules exist in six-membered hemiacetal forms in equilibrium with open-chain aldehyde form in a water solution, as shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\).

When alcohol is in excess, a second \(\ce{R-OH}\) adds to the hemiacetal resulting in an acetal. An acetal has two \(\ce{-O-R}\)-groups bonded to the same \(\ce{C}\), as shown in the example below.

The acetal groups in carbohydrates are called glycoside linkages. Glycoside linkages connect monosaccharide units together in polysaccharides like starch and cellulose and in disaccharides, e.g., lactose, maltose, and sucrose, as shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\).

Lecture Connection

Of everything in this section, the course needs one reaction, run forward and backward: an alcohol adds to a C=O to give a hemiacetal, a second alcohol turns it into an acetal, and water plus acid takes it all apart again. Learn to spot the "goggle-eyed" carbon with two single-bonded oxygens; every sugar ring in Chapter 8 has one.

In this course

The section text explains these reactions with curved-arrow mechanisms, hydrates and the addition of cyanide (cyanohydrins). None of that is assessed in CHEM 3B. You are responsible for: recognizing and drawing hemiacetals and acetals (including cyclic ones), predicting the products of adding one or two alcohols to an aldehyde or ketone, and predicting the products of hydrolyzing an acetal or hemiacetal. Some books distinguish hemiketals and ketals (from ketones); the exam terms "hemiacetal" and "acetal" cover both.

Hemiacetal, then acetal: two separate steps

Both steps are reversible and both are catalyzed by a trace of acid (H+). Learn them as a pair.

  1. Step 1 – addition. The O–H of an alcohol adds across the C=O. The H goes to the carbonyl oxygen (making an OH) and the OR group goes to the carbonyl carbon. Product: a hemiacetal, one carbon holding 1 OH and 1 OR. Nothing is lost; it is a simple addition reaction. ("Hemi" means half, as in hemisphere.)
  2. Step 2 – condensation (substitution of OH by OR). A second alcohol replaces the hemiacetal OH, and a water molecule leaves. Product: an acetal, one carbon holding 2 OR groups, plus H2O.

The OH and OR groups must be attached to the same carbon; two oxygens on different carbons make a diol or a hydroxy ether, not a hemiacetal.

Because every arrow is reversible, the reaction direction depends on conditions (Le Châtelier’s principle, Chapter 4). Excess alcohol and removal of water push toward the acetal; excess water and acid push back toward the aldehyde.

Professor Tip

Look for the "goggle eyes": one carbon with two oxygens attached by single bonds. If one O has an H, it is a hemiacetal. If both oxygens are bonded to carbons, it is an acetal. That carbon used to be the C=O carbon, and it tells you where the molecule came from: if the goggle-eyed carbon also has an H, it was an aldehyde (a hemiacetal/acetal); if it has two carbons, it was a ketone (a hemiketal/ketal). Learn to spot it now, because every sugar ring in Chapter 8 is a hemiacetal.

Practice (lecture style). Draw the condensed structural formula of the acetal formed when butanal reacts with methanol. Two methanol molecules add to the aldehyde carbon, which keeps its H and its propyl group: CH3CH2CH2CH(OCH3)2, plus one H2O. Stop after one methanol and you have the hemiacetal, CH3CH2CH2CH(OH)OCH3.

Hydrolysis of acetals: run the steps backward

Acetal formation is reversible. With water and an acid catalyst, an acetal splits back into the aldehyde or ketone and two alcohol molecules. Hemiacetals revert even more easily. This matters in Chapter 8: the glycosidic bonds that link sugars are acetals (the source text calls them glycoside linkages), and digestion hydrolyzes them. To predict the products, find the acetal carbon (the one with two single-bonded oxygens), then:

  • Each –OR group leaves as a separate alcohol, R–OH. Two OCH3 groups give two methanol molecules; two OCH2CH3 groups give two ethanol molecules.
  • The acetal carbon becomes the C=O carbon again. If it still holds an H, the product is an aldehyde; if it holds two carbons, the product is a ketone.
  • Count: acetal + H2O → carbonyl compound + 2 alcohol. A hemiacetal needs no water; it simply splits into the carbonyl compound + 1 alcohol.

Example: CH3CH2CH(OCH3)2 + H2O (H+) → propanal + 2 CH3OH. Acetals are stable in base and in neutral water, so they survive the small intestine's near-neutral pH until an enzyme does the hydrolysis instead of acid.

Example: Hydrolysis of an acetal

Problem. CH3C(OCH2CH3)2CH2CH3 is heated with water and a drop of acid. Give the products and name them.

Solution. Find the carbon with two single-bonded oxygens: it is C2, bonded to CH3, CH2CH3 and two OCH2CH3 groups, so this is an acetal from a ketone. Each OCH2CH3 leaves as ethanol (two molecules). C2 becomes C=O again, holding CH3 and CH2CH3: a ketone. Products: butanone (2-butanone) + 2 ethanol.

Check. Run it forward: butanone + 2 ethanol (H+) → the acetal + H2O. Atoms balance, and the water consumed in hydrolysis is the water released in formation.

Cyclic hemiacetals: a preview of sugars

A single molecule that contains both a C=O and an OH can react with itself. If the OH sits on C4 or C5 (counting the carbonyl carbon as C1), the chain can curl around so the OH adds to the C=O, giving a five- or six-membered ring that contains the oxygen. The product is a cyclic hemiacetal: the old carbonyl carbon sits in the ring holding one OH (outside the ring) and one O that is now part of the ring. Take 5-hydroxypentanal as the simplest case.

Rings of five or six atoms form easily and, because hemiacetal formation is reversible, the open-chain and ring forms exist together in solution, with the ring strongly favored; the section's glucose figure shows exactly this. Glucose is a six-carbon aldehyde with an OH on C5 that closes to a six-membered cyclic hemiacetal, and more than 99% of it sits in the ring form in water. When a cyclic hemiacetal then reacts with a second alcohol (another sugar's OH, for instance) it becomes a cyclic acetal, which is what the glycosidic bond in sucrose, lactose and starch is. Hydrolysis of those acetal links is digestion. You will draw all of this in Chapter 8; for now, make sure you can spot the hemiacetal carbon in a ring.
