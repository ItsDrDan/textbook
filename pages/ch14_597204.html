Learning Objectives

  • List where every ATP, NADH and FADH2 comes from when one glucose is completely oxidized to CO2 and H2O.
  • Explain why cytosolic NADH needs a shuttle, and compare the malate–aspartate shuttle with the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle.
  • Calculate the net ATP from one glucose (32 or 30), from two pyruvates, and from one acetyl CoA, and explain why older books say 36–38.
  • Estimate the efficiency of glucose catabolism and compare the aerobic yield with the anaerobic yield (2 ATP).

When a patient in shock has a lactate of 6 mmol/L, or when a tooth's pulp dies after its blood supply is cut, what has really gone wrong is arithmetic: the cell needs more ATP than it can make without oxygen. This section does the accounting for one glucose molecule, from the first phosphorylation in the cytosol to the last water molecule formed at Complex IV, so that you can see exactly how big the difference between aerobic and anaerobic metabolism is and where every ATP comes from.

The bookkeeping method

Only three things count toward the final total: ATP (or GTP) made directly by substrate-level phosphorylation, NADH, and FADH2. The reduced coenzymes are not ATP yet; they are converted to ATP only in oxidative phosphorylation, at the rates this book uses throughout: 2.5 ATP per NADH and 1.5 ATP per FADH2. So the method is: list each stage, count its direct ATP, NADH and FADH2, multiply, and add.

  1. Glycolysis (cytosol). One glucose → 2 pyruvate. Two ATP are spent in the first half and four are made in the second half: 2 ATP net. The glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase step runs twice and gives 2 NADH, but these are made in the cytosol, which turns out to matter.
  2. Pyruvate → acetyl CoA (matrix). The pyruvate dehydrogenase complex runs twice: 2 NADH and 2 CO2. No ATP.
  3. Citric acid cycle (matrix), two turns. Each turn gives 3 NADH, 1 FADH2, 1 GTP and 2 CO2; doubled: 6 NADH, 2 FADH2, 2 GTP (counted as 2 ATP) and 4 CO2.
  4. Oxidative phosphorylation (inner membrane). Every NADH and FADH2 from steps 1–3 is cashed in here. Nothing new is made here except water: the 12 electron pairs carried by 10 NADH and 2 FADH2 reduce 6 O2 to water.

Add the carbon dioxide up as a check: 0 + 2 + 4 = 6 CO2, one for each carbon of glucose.

The shuttle problem

The inner mitochondrial membrane must be nearly leak-proof to hold the proton gradient, and one consequence is that NADH cannot cross it. The 2 NADH from glycolysis are stuck in the cytosol, and if nothing were done about them glycolysis would stop for lack of NAD+. Cells solve this by moving the electrons across instead of the molecule, on small metabolites that do have carriers. Two systems exist, and they give different ATP yields.

Malate–aspartate shuttle (heart, liver, kidney)

In the cytosol, malate dehydrogenase uses the glycolytic NADH to reduce oxaloacetate to malate (the reverse of cycle step 8), regenerating cytosolic NAD+. Malate has a carrier and enters the matrix. There, the matrix malate dehydrogenase re-oxidizes it to oxaloacetate and makes matrix NADH, which goes to Complex I for the full 2.5 ATP. The loop has to be closed: oxaloacetate has no carrier, so a transaminase swaps its ketone for an amino group (from glutamate, which becomes α-ketoglutarate) to make aspartate, which does have a carrier. Aspartate returns to the cytosol and is transaminated back to oxaloacetate, ready for the next NADH. Nothing is consumed; the shuttle simply carries two electrons inward.

Glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle (skeletal muscle, brain)

Here the cytosolic NADH reduces dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP), a glycolysis intermediate you met in Chapter 13, to glycerol-3-phosphate. A second glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase, sitting on the outer face of the inner membrane, re-oxidizes it to DHAP, but this enzyme uses FAD, not NAD+. The FADH2 passes its electrons directly to coenzyme Q, skipping Complex I and its proton pumps, so each cytosolic NADH is worth only 1.5 ATP. The shuttle is simpler and fast, which suits tissues that need quick bursts, but it costs 1 ATP per NADH, or 2 ATP per glucose.

Professor Tip

When a problem says "in muscle" or "in brain," use the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle and count the two glycolytic NADH as 1.5 ATP each (total 30). When it says "in liver," "in heart," or does not say, use the malate–aspartate shuttle and count them as 2.5 each (total 32). Everything after glycolysis is the same in every tissue, so the only line that changes is the glycolysis NADH line.

Of the 32, only 4 come from substrate-level phosphorylation (2 in glycolysis, 2 GTP in the cycle); the other 28 come from oxidative phosphorylation. That is why losing oxygen is so catastrophic: it removes nearly 90% of the yield.

Why older textbooks say 36 or 38. For decades the standard values were 3 ATP per NADH and 2 per FADH2, which give 38 (malate–aspartate) or 36 (glycerol-3-phosphate). Those round numbers assumed that each proton-pumping complex made exactly one ATP. Measurements of protons pumped per NADH (about 10) and protons needed per ATP (about 4, counting the transport of ADP, Pi and ATP across the membrane) give the values 2.5 and 1.5 used here. If you see 36–38 elsewhere, it is the same chemistry with older arithmetic.

How efficient is it?

Burning one mole of glucose in a calorimeter releases about 686 kcal. Hydrolysis of ATP to ADP under standard conditions releases about 7.3 kcal/mol. Capturing 32 mol ATP per mol glucose therefore stores 32 × 7.3 = 234 kcal, an efficiency of 234/686 ≈ 34%. The remaining two-thirds leave as heat, which is most of the heat that keeps you at 37 °C. (Inside a real cell, where ATP hydrolysis releases more energy than the standard value, the efficiency is somewhat higher, but 34% is the figure usually quoted.)

Aerobic versus anaerobic

Without oxygen, electron transport stops, the proton gradient collapses, and the matrix NAD+ and FAD are no longer regenerated, so pyruvate dehydrogenase and the cycle stop too. The only stage that can keep running is glycolysis, and even it needs NAD+. The cell gets it by using NADH to reduce pyruvate to lactate (lactate dehydrogenase, Chapter 13). Lactate fermentation makes no ATP of its own; its whole purpose is to recycle NAD+ so the 2 ATP of glycolysis can keep coming. The anaerobic yield is therefore 2 ATP per glucose, one-sixteenth of the aerobic yield. A cell can survive on this only if its ATP demand is low or it can consume glucose very fast, which is why a sprinting muscle uses up much of its glycogen within a minute.

Example 1: Muscle versus liver

Problem. One glucose is completely oxidized (a) in a skeletal muscle cell and (b) in a liver cell. How many ATP does each yield, and where does the difference come from?

Solution. The stages after glycolysis are identical: 2 NADH (pyruvate dehydrogenase) × 2.5 = 5; cycle 6 NADH × 2.5 = 15, 2 FADH2 × 1.5 = 3, 2 GTP = 2; plus 2 ATP from glycolysis. That is 27 ATP before the glycolytic NADH are counted. (a) Muscle uses the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle: 2 NADH × 1.5 = 3; total 30 ATP. (b) Liver uses the malate–aspartate shuttle: 2 NADH × 2.5 = 5; total 32 ATP.

Check. The difference is exactly 2 ATP, one per cytosolic NADH, because each enters at CoQ (1.5) instead of Complex I (2.5). Nothing else in the tally depends on the tissue.

Example 2: Starting partway through

Problem. How many ATP are produced from (a) one acetyl CoA, and (b) the two pyruvates made from one glucose, assuming complete oxidation in the mitochondrion?

Solution. (a) One turn of the cycle: 3 NADH × 2.5 = 7.5; 1 FADH2 × 1.5 = 1.5; 1 GTP = 1. Total 10 ATP. (b) Each pyruvate gives 1 NADH at pyruvate dehydrogenase (2.5) plus one acetyl CoA (10), so 12.5 per pyruvate and 25 ATP for two.

Check. 25 (from two pyruvates) + 7 (glycolysis: 2 ATP + 2 NADH at 2.5) = 32, the full malate–aspartate total. Because everything in (a) and (b) happens inside the matrix, no shuttle is involved and the tissue does not matter.

Example 3: One mole of glucose with and without oxygen

Problem. A tissue receives 1.0 mol of glucose. Compare the ATP it can make (a) with adequate oxygen (liver) and (b) with no oxygen, and explain why the anaerobic cell must make lactate.

Solution. (a) Aerobic: 32 mol ATP, storing about 32 × 7.3 = 234 kcal. (b) Anaerobic: glycolysis only, 2 mol ATP, about 15 kcal. The ratio is 32/2 = 16. In (b), glycolysis still makes 2 NADH per glucose, but electron transport cannot re-oxidize them. If the NADH were not recycled the cell would run out of NAD+ within seconds and glycolysis itself would stop, yielding zero. Lactate dehydrogenase transfers the hydride from NADH to pyruvate, giving lactate and NAD+, so the 2 ATP per glucose can continue. The lactate is the price of keeping glycolysis alive.

Check. To make the same 32 ATP anaerobically, the cell would need 16 glucose molecules and would release 32 lactate molecules and the acid that goes with them, which is why hypoxic tissue becomes acidotic so quickly.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Shock is any state in which tissues do not receive enough oxygenated blood: hemorrhagic, cardiogenic, septic. At the cellular level every type looks the same: oxidative phosphorylation fails, each glucose gives 2 ATP instead of 32, and the cells pour out lactate to keep glycolysis running. Rising blood lactate and the resulting metabolic acidosis (low pH, low bicarbonate) are therefore early warning signs, and a falling lactate is one sign that resuscitation is working. The brain suffers first: it runs almost entirely on glucose and oxygen, uses about 20% of the body's energy, and stores neither, so a few minutes without flow kills neurons whose ion pumps need ATP faster than glycolysis can supply it. The same arithmetic applies to heart muscle during a myocardial infarction. Looking ahead to Chapter 15: fat releases 9 kcal/g against 4 kcal/g for carbohydrate because fatty acid carbons are more reduced and feed more NADH and FADH2 into the chain, but fat can only be burned with oxygen, so in shock it is useless as fuel.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

A deep periodontal pocket is an oxygen-poor environment, and the bacteria that thrive there (Porphyromonas gingivalis and other obligate anaerobes) make their ATP without using oxygen, mostly by fermentation. That is why the biofilm shifts from the streptococci of supragingival plaque to anaerobic species as a pocket deepens and oxygen falls, and why disrupting the biofilm and reducing pocket depth, which lets oxygen back in, shifts the community toward less harmful organisms. Healing itself is oxygen-hungry: fibroblasts laying down new collagen and the cells rebuilding bone need the full 32-ATP yield, which is why tissues with poor blood supply (a smoker's gingiva, an irradiated jaw) heal slowly and why clinicians watch surgical sites in patients with compromised circulation.

Summary

  • Only direct ATP/GTP, NADH and FADH2 count; convert NADH at 2.5 ATP and FADH2 at 1.5 ATP.
  • One glucose: glycolysis 2 ATP + 2 NADH (cytosol); pyruvate dehydrogenase 2 NADH; two cycle turns 6 NADH + 2 FADH2 + 2 GTP.
  • Cytosolic NADH cannot cross the inner membrane. The malate–aspartate shuttle (liver, heart, kidney) delivers its electrons as matrix NADH (2.5 ATP each); the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle (muscle, brain) delivers them to CoQ as FADH2 (1.5 ATP each).
  • Totals: 32 ATP (malate–aspartate) or 30 ATP (glycerol-3-phosphate). Older texts using 3 and 2 ATP per carrier give 38 or 36.
  • About 34% of the 686 kcal/mol in glucose is captured as ATP; the rest is heat.
  • Without oxygen only glycolysis runs: 2 ATP per glucose, and pyruvate must be reduced to lactate to regenerate NAD+.

Exercises

Exercise 1

Using the tally table, how many ATP per glucose come from oxidative phosphorylation alone (malate–aspartate shuttle)? What fraction of the total is that?

Answer
Total 32 minus 4 substrate-level (2 ATP in glycolysis + 2 GTP) = 28 ATP from oxidative phosphorylation, which is 28/32 = 87.5% of the total.

Exercise 2

A brain cell oxidizes one glucose completely. Show the tally and give the total.

Answer
Brain uses the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle. Glycolysis: 2 ATP + 2 NADH × 1.5 = 3 → 5. Pyruvate dehydrogenase: 2 NADH × 2.5 = 5. Cycle: 6 NADH × 2.5 = 15, 2 FADH2 × 1.5 = 3, 2 GTP = 2 → 20. Total 5 + 5 + 20 = 30 ATP.

Exercise 3

How many ATP would one glucose yield if a cell had a working malate–aspartate shuttle but its Complex II were completely inhibited (assume the cycle could still finish by another route and the FADH2 was simply wasted)?

Answer
Remove the 2 FADH2 × 1.5 = 3 ATP from the 32 total: 29 ATP. Everything else (direct ATP, all 10 NADH) is unchanged.

Exercise 4

Using the older values (3 ATP per NADH, 2 per FADH2), recompute the total for a liver cell and show that it comes to 38.

Answer
Glycolysis: 2 ATP + 2 NADH × 3 = 6 → 8. Pyruvate dehydrogenase: 2 NADH × 3 = 6. Cycle: 6 NADH × 3 = 18, 2 FADH2 × 2 = 4, 2 GTP = 2 → 24. Total 8 + 6 + 24 = 38. With the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle the first line becomes 2 + 4 = 6 and the total is 36.

Exercise 5

Three acetyl CoA molecules enter the citric acid cycle. How many ATP result, and how many CO2 are released?

Answer
3 × 10 = 30 ATP (each turn: 3 NADH × 2.5 + 1.5 + 1 = 10); 3 × 2 = 6 CO2.

Exercise 6

If 32 mol ATP are made per mol glucose and ATP stores 7.3 kcal/mol, how many kilocalories per mole of glucose are captured, and what percentage of the 686 kcal/mol is lost as heat?

Answer
32 × 7.3 = 234 kcal captured; 686 − 234 = 452 kcal lost as heat, which is 452/686 = 66% (efficiency about 34%).

Exercise 7

A muscle working anaerobically needs 60 ATP per second from glucose. How many glucose molecules per second must it consume, and how many lactate molecules per second does it release? How many glucose per second would it need aerobically (glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle)?

Answer
Anaerobic: 60 ATP ÷ 2 ATP per glucose = 30 glucose per second, releasing 2 lactate each = 60 lactate per second. Aerobic: 60 ÷ 30 = 2 glucose per second, and no lactate. The anaerobic muscle burns 15 times more glucose and acidifies itself in the process.
