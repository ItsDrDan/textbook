Learning Objectives
  • To recognize the structures of common fatty acids and classify them as saturated, monounsaturated, or polyunsaturated.

Fatty acids are carboxylic acids that are structural components of fats, oils, and all other categories of lipids, except steroids. More than 70 have been identified in nature. They usually contain an even number of carbon atoms (typically 12–20), are generally unbranched, and can be classified by the presence and number of carbon-to-carbon double bonds. Thus, saturated fatty acids contain no carbon-to-carbon double bonds, monounsaturated fatty acids contain one carbon-to-carbon double bond, and polyunsaturated fatty acids contain two or more carbon-to-carbon double bonds.

Two polyunsaturated fatty acids—linoleic and α-linolenic acids—are termed essential fatty acids because humans must obtain them from their diets. Both substances are required for normal growth and development, but the human body does not synthesize them. The body uses linoleic acid to synthesize many of the other unsaturated fatty acids, such as arachidonic acid, a precursor for the synthesis of prostaglandins. In addition, the essential fatty acids are necessary for the efficient transport and metabolism of cholesterol. The average daily diet should contain about 4–6 g of the essential fatty acids.

To Your Health: Prostaglandins

Prostaglandins are chemical messengers synthesized in the cells in which their physiological activity is expressed. They are unsaturated fatty acids containing 20 carbon atoms and are synthesized from arachidonic acid—a polyunsaturated fatty acid—when needed by a particular cell. They are called prostaglandins because they were originally isolated from semen found in the prostate gland. It is now known that they are synthesized in nearly all mammalian tissues and affect almost all organs in the body. The five major classes of prostaglandins are designated as PGA, PGB, PGE, PGF, and PGI. Subscripts are attached at the end of these abbreviations to denote the number of double bonds outside the five-carbon ring in a given prostaglandin.

The prostaglandins are among the most potent biological substances known. Slight structural differences give them highly distinct biological effects; however, all prostaglandins exhibit some ability to induce smooth muscle contraction, lower blood pressure, and contribute to the inflammatory response. Aspirin and other nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory agents, such as ibuprofen, obstruct the synthesis of prostaglandins by inhibiting cyclooxygenase, the enzyme needed for the initial step in the conversion of arachidonic acid to prostaglandins.

Their wide range of physiological activity has led to the synthesis of hundreds of prostaglandins and their analogs. Derivatives of PGE2 are now used in the United States to induce labor. Other prostaglandins have been employed clinically to lower or increase blood pressure, inhibit stomach secretions, relieve nasal congestion, relieve asthma, and prevent the formation of blood clots, which are associated with heart attacks and strokes.

Although we often draw the carbon atoms in a straight line, they actually have more of a zigzag configuration (Figure \(\PageIndex{2a}\)). Viewed as a whole, however, the saturated fatty acid molecule is relatively straight (Figure \(\PageIndex{2b}\)). Such molecules pack closely together into a crystal lattice, maximizing the strength of dispersion forces and causing fatty acids and the fats derived from them to have relatively high melting points. In contrast, each cis carbon-to-carbon double bond in an unsaturated fatty acid produces a pronounced bend in the molecule, so that these molecules do not stack neatly. As a result, the intermolecular attractions of unsaturated fatty acids (and unsaturated fats) are weaker, causing these substances to have lower melting points. Most are liquids at room temperature.

Waxes are esters formed from long-chain fatty acids and long-chain alcohols. Most natural waxes are mixtures of such esters. Plant waxes on the surfaces of leaves, stems, flowers, and fruits protect the plant from dehydration and invasion by harmful microorganisms. Carnauba wax, used extensively in floor waxes, automobile waxes, and furniture polish, is largely myricyl cerotate, obtained from the leaves of certain Brazilian palm trees. Animals also produce waxes that serve as protective coatings, keeping the surfaces of feathers, skin, and hair pliable and water repellent. In fact, if the waxy coating on the feathers of a water bird is dissolved as a result of the bird swimming in an oil slick, the feathers become wet and heavy, and the bird, unable to maintain its buoyancy, drowns.

Summary

Fatty acids are carboxylic acids that are the structural components of many lipids. They may be saturated or unsaturated. Most fatty acids are unbranched and contain an even number of carbon atoms. Unsaturated fatty acids have lower melting points than saturated fatty acids containing the same number of carbon atoms.

Lecture Connection

In lecture, lipids are the one family defined by a physical property, not a functional group: they dissolve in nonpolar solvents and not in water. The first sort is hydrolyzable (contains an ester or amide you can split with water) versus non-hydrolyzable; every hydrolyzable lipid is built from the fatty acids of this section, so the skills here (saturation, cis/trans, omega numbering, melting-point ranking) carry through the whole chapter.

The map of the chapter: hydrolyzable or not

  • Hydrolyzable lipids can be converted into smaller molecules by hydrolysis because they contain ester (or, in sphingolipids, amide) linkages that water can split: waxes, triacylglycerols, glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids. All are derived from fatty acids.
  • Non-hydrolyzable lipids cannot be cleaved into smaller molecules by aqueous hydrolysis because they contain no ester linkages: steroids, eicosanoids, and the fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K).

Hydrolyzable lipids are built by condensation (esterification or amide formation) and can be taken apart by hydrolysis, which is how digestion and saponification work. Non-hydrolyzable lipids have no such link, so they survive boiling with acid or base; the body changes them by oxidation and other reactions instead.

Fatty acids: saturation, packing, and melting point

  • Fatty acids are carboxylic acids (RCOOH) with long, unbranched carbon chains, usually an even number of carbons (12–20). The long hydrocarbon tail is nonpolar; only the COOH end is polar.
  • Saturated (no C=C; "saturated with hydrogen," the maximum number of H's): straight chains pack tightly, giving many dispersion-force (van der Waals) contacts and high melting points. Saturated fatty acids are solids at room temperature (butter, lard, coconut oil).
  • Unsaturated (one or more C=C, generally cis): monounsaturated means one C=C, polyunsaturated means two or more. Each cis double bond puts a kink in the chain, so the molecules pack poorly, there are fewer dispersion-force contacts, and the melting point drops with every added cis double bond. Unsaturated fatty acids are liquids at room temperature (oils). A trans C=C does not kink the chain, so a trans fatty acid packs and melts almost like a saturated one (elaidic acid, the trans isomer of oleic acid, melts at about 45 °C).
  • Carbon labels: the carbon next to the COOH is α (C2), the next is β (C3), and the last carbon (the CH3) is ω. "Omega-3" means the first C=C starts 3 carbons in from the ω end.

Shorthand notation. A fatty acid is written as carbons : double bonds. Stearic acid is 18:0 (18 carbons, saturated); oleic acid is 18:1; linoleic acid is 18:2; arachidonic acid is 20:4. Double-bond positions are counted from the COOH carbon (C1) when a chemist draws the molecule, but the omega number counts from the CH3 end: it is the position of the first double-bonded carbon you reach starting from the ω carbon as number 1. A quick way to get it: omega number = total carbons − the C1-numbered position of the last double bond. For oleic acid the only C=C is C9=C10, so 18 − 9 = 9: omega-9.

Essential fatty acids. Human enzymes can add double bonds only within the first nine carbons from the COOH end, so we cannot make a C=C at the omega-6 or omega-3 position. Linoleic acid (18:2, omega-6) and α-linolenic acid (18:3, omega-3) must therefore come from the diet; they are essential fatty acids. From them the body builds the longer ones: arachidonic acid from linoleic acid, and EPA and DHA (slowly and inefficiently) from α-linolenic acid.

Professor Tip

Two rules decide every melting-point ranking in this chapter. (1) Among saturated chains, longer chain = more dispersion forces = higher melting point. (2) Among chains of equal length, each cis double bond lowers the melting point a lot. When the two rules pull in opposite directions, a cis double bond usually wins over a few extra carbons: arachidonic acid (20:4) melts far below lauric acid (12:0). Practice: which has the lowest melting point, CH3(CH2)14COOH, CH3(CH2)16COOH, cis CH3(CH2)7CH=CH(CH2)7COOH, or all-cis CH3(CH2)4CH=CHCH2CH=CH(CH2)7COOH? The last one: two cis kinks.

In this course

The melting points in the section's table differ by a few degrees from the ones in lecture (stearic 70 vs. 69 °C, oleic 16 vs. 13 °C, myristic 58 vs. 54 °C); sources vary. Exams ask you to rank and explain with intermolecular forces, never to quote a value. The section text does not use the omega system or the 18:1 shorthand; both are required in this course.

Two more things an exam drawing can ask of a fatty acid chain. (1) cis or trans? Look at the two hydrogens (or the two chain segments) on the C=C: on the same side of the double bond is cis, which bends the chain; on opposite sides is trans, which keeps it straight. Natural unsaturated fatty acids are cis. (2) Which carbon is α, β, or ω? Number from the COOH carbon as C1: α is C2, β is C3; the ω carbon is the CH3 at the far end, however long the chain is, and the omega number counts back from it to the first C=C.

Example 1: Melting points

Problem. Stearic acid (18:0) melts at 69 °C, and oleic acid (18:1, cis) melts at about 13 °C. Explain.

Solution

Both have 18 carbons. The cis double bond in oleic acid kinks the chain, so the molecules cannot line up closely. Fewer dispersion-force contacts mean a lower melting point, so oleic acid is a liquid at room temperature.

Check. Same chain length, so only the double-bond rule applies; one cis C=C should drop the melting point by tens of degrees, and it does (56 °C).

Example 2: Omega numbering

Problem. Linoleic acid is 18 carbons with cis double bonds starting at C9 and C12 (numbered from the COOH carbon). Is it an omega-3 or omega-6?

Solution

The double bond farthest from the COOH starts at C12, i.e., between C12 and C13. Counting from the ω end, C18 is ω1, so C13 is ω6. Omega-6.

Check. Shortcut: 18 − 12 = 6. Try it on α-linolenic acid (18:3; C9, C12, C15): 18 − 15 = 3, omega-3. And on arachidonic acid (20:4; C5, C8, C11, C14): 20 − 14 = 6, omega-6. All three agree with the table above.

Example 3: Label a fatty acid and decide cis or trans

Problem. Palmitoleic acid is CH3(CH2)5CH=CH(CH2)7COOH, and in the natural acid the two H atoms on the double bond are on the same side. (a) Classify it (saturated, monounsaturated, polyunsaturated) and give its shorthand notation. (b) Identify the α, β, and ω carbons. (c) Is the double bond cis or trans, and what does that do to the chain? (d) Give the omega class.

Solution. (a) One C=C: monounsaturated. Carbons: 1 + 5 + 2 + 7 + 1 = 16, so 16:1. (b) Number from the COOH carbon as C1: the α carbon is C2 (the first CH2 next to COOH), the β carbon is C3, and the ω carbon is C16, the CH3 at the far end. (c) Two hydrogens on the same side of the C=C is cis; the chain bends at the double bond, so the molecules pack poorly and the melting point is low (about 0 °C; it is an oil). (d) The C=C is between C9 and C10 counting from COOH, so counting from the ω end it starts at carbon 16 − 9 = 7: an omega-7 fatty acid.

Check. From the CH3 end: CH3 is ω1, then five CH2 (ω2–ω6), then the first double-bonded carbon at ω7. Both counting methods agree.

Waxes: one ester, two long tails

A wax is an ester of a long-chain fatty acid and a long-chain alcohol, so it has one ester group and two long nonpolar tails. The main ester in beeswax, for example, joins palmitic acid (16 carbons) to a 30-carbon alcohol: CH3(CH2)14COO(CH2)29CH3. Hydrolysis of a wax gives back the fatty acid and the alcohol, one molecule of each, which is the easy way to tell a wax from a fat. The long nonpolar chains make waxes water-repellent and give them a firm, moldable texture: beeswax (candles, cosmetics), carnauba wax (car polish, candy coatings), and lanolin, the wax secreted by sheep's skin that coats wool. Lanolin is a mixture of long-chain esters, including esters of steroid alcohols, and because it softens and seals skin it is the base of many lip balms, nipple creams, and hand creams. Plants coat leaves and fruit with wax (the bloom on a plum) to hold in water. "Condensation: molecules join, a small molecule (H2O) is lost. Hydrolysis: water splits the molecule."

Example 4: Draw a wax from its parts

Problem. Write the condensed structural formula of the wax formed from palmitic acid, CH3(CH2)14COOH, and the 26-carbon alcohol CH3(CH2)24CH2OH. Name the reaction and the small molecule released, then write the products of hydrolyzing the wax with aqueous acid.

Solution. A wax is an ester, so this is a condensation (esterification): the acid's –OH and the alcohol's H leave as water, and the C=O carbon bonds to the alcohol's oxygen.

CH3(CH2)14COOH + HOCH2(CH2)24CH3 → CH3(CH2)14COO(CH2)25CH3 + H2O

Acid hydrolysis is the reverse: water splits the ester back into palmitic acid and the 26-carbon alcohol, one molecule of each.

Check. Count carbons: 16 from the acid + 26 from the alcohol = 42 in the ester (1 + 14 + 1 + 25 + 1 = 42). The C=O side of the ester came from the acid, the single-bonded O from the alcohol, the same rule as for every ester in Chapter 6. One ester and two long chains, no glycerol: a wax, not a fat.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Waxes are tools of the trade: baseplate, bite-registration, and orthodontic relief waxes (paraffin hydrocarbons blended with beeswax esters) soften near mouth temperature, and lanolin lip balm protects patients' lips during long appointments.

Eicosanoids: what the body makes from arachidonic acid

The section's prostaglandins are one of three families of eicosanoids ("eicosa" = twenty), all derived from 20-carbon unsaturated fatty acids, chiefly arachidonic acid (20:4, omega-6), which is first cut out of a membrane phospholipid by the enzyme phospholipase. They are not stored in cells but synthesized in response to a stimulus, and unlike hormones they act within seconds on the cells right around them and are destroyed almost immediately, so they are short-lived "local hormones." Two enzyme routes give three families, and each family has a shape you can recognize in a drawing:

  • Prostaglandins (made by the COX, or cyclooxygenase, enzymes, COX-1 and COX-2; the chain is folded into a five-membered carbon ring, and the members differ in the groups on that ring): responsible for inflammation, pain, and fever; also protect the stomach lining by stimulating mucus and bicarbonate, help the kidney keep blood flowing, and trigger uterine contractions in labor.
  • Thromboxanes (also from COX, in platelets; the ring is six-membered and contains an ether oxygen): promote platelet aggregation (the first step of a clot) and vasoconstriction.
  • Leukotrienes (made by lipoxygenase in leukocytes, white blood cells; no ring, but three conjugated C=C bonds, alternating double and single, hence "triene"): responsible for the inflammatory response, and in asthma they constrict the smooth muscle of the airways and cause mucus secretion and swelling; newer asthma drugs block leukotriene synthesis or receptors, treating the cause rather than only the symptoms.

How NSAIDs work (concept only). Aspirin, ibuprofen, and naproxen are nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). They block the COX enzymes, so less arachidonic acid is turned into prostaglandins (less pain, fever, and swelling) and thromboxanes (less clotting). The same block removes the stomach-protecting prostaglandins, which is why long-term NSAID use can cause gastric ulcers and why they are taken with food. Corticosteroids such as cortisol and prednisone act one step earlier: they suppress the release of arachidonic acid itself, so they lower all three eicosanoid families, including leukotrienes. That is the chemical reason an inhaled corticosteroid controls asthma while an NSAID does not.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Low-dose aspirin is antiplatelet because it blocks COX in platelets, which cannot make new enzyme, so thromboxane stays low for the platelet's 7–10 day life. In dentistry the same chemistry has two faces: NSAIDs such as ibuprofen reduce pain and swelling after extractions and periodontal surgery by blocking prostaglandin synthesis, and patients on aspirin may bleed more during procedures because thromboxane is blocked too.
