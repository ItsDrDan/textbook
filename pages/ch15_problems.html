Problem 15.13

Why do ketone bodies build up in uncontrolled diabetes, and what danger does this pose?

Problem 15.42

Which enzyme of β-oxidation is a lyase?

  1. acyl CoA dehydrogenase
  2. enoyl CoA hydratase
  3. L-3-hydroxyacyl CoA dehydrogenase
  4. thiolase
Answer
B. Enoyl CoA hydratase adds water across the C=C without ATP or a redox change, the definition of a lyase. Both dehydrogenases are oxidoreductases (FAD and NAD+), and thiolase transfers the acyl group to coenzyme A, making it a transferase.

Problem 15.43

The coenzyme required by ALT and AST is

  1. biotin
  2. FAD
  3. pyridoxal phosphate (vitamin B6)
  4. coenzyme A
Answer
C. Aminotransferases carry the amino group on pyridoxal phosphate between the two halves of the reaction. Biotin carries CO2 (acetyl CoA carboxylase), FAD carries electrons, and coenzyme A carries acyl groups.

Problem 15.44

In the urea cycle, which step is catalyzed by a hydrolase?

  1. NH4+ + HCO3− → carbamoyl phosphate
  2. citrulline + aspartate → argininosuccinate
  3. argininosuccinate → arginine + fumarate
  4. arginine + H2O → urea + ornithine
Answer
D. Arginase uses water to split arginine, so it is a hydrolase (step 4, hydrolysis). A is the entry step, a ligase (carbamoyl phosphate synthetase, 2 ATP); B is step 2, a condensation by a ligase (argininosuccinate synthetase, ATP → AMP); C is step 3, a cleavage by a lyase (argininosuccinate lyase, no water or ATP).

Problem 15.45

Which statement about fatty acid synthesis (lipogenesis) is correct?

  1. It occurs in the mitochondria and uses NAD+.
  2. Acetyl CoA carboxylase, a biotin-dependent ligase, makes malonyl CoA and is activated by insulin.
  3. Glucagon activates acetyl CoA carboxylase.
  4. The growing chain is carried on coenzyme A.
Answer
B. Lipogenesis runs in the cytosol with NADPH and carries the chain on acyl carrier protein (ACP), so A and D are wrong. Insulin activates acetyl CoA carboxylase; glucagon inhibits it by phosphorylation, so C is wrong.

Problem 15.46

Myristic acid (14 C, saturated) and myristoleic acid (14 C, one cis C=C) are both oxidized completely. Build the full tally (acetyl CoA, FADH2, NADH, activation) for each and find the net ATP. Then explain in one sentence why the two numbers differ.

Answer
Both: 14/2 = 7 acetyl CoA, 6 cycles, 6 NADH. Myristic: 6 FADH2; ATP = 7(10) + 6(2.5) + 6(1.5) − 2 = 70 + 15 + 9 − 2 = 92. Myristoleic: 5 FADH2; ATP = 70 + 15 + 7.5 − 2 = 90.5. The unsaturated acid already has one C=C, so the FAD-dependent acyl CoA dehydrogenase step is skipped once (an isomerase moves the bond into position instead), costing 1.5 ATP.

Problem 15.47

Compare and contrast glycogen and triacylglycerol as energy stores: energy per gram, water content, and how long each lasts in a fast. Why does the body not simply store all its energy as glycogen?

Answer
Glycogen gives 4 kcal/g, is stored hydrated (2–3 g water per gram), and the ~400–500 g supply lasts less than a day. Triacylglycerol gives 9 kcal/g, is stored without water, and the ~12 kg supply lasts weeks. Fat is more reduced (more to oxidize) and nonpolar (no water), so it holds about six to seven times more energy per gram of tissue; storing 100,000 kcal as glycogen would mean carrying roughly 75 kg of hydrated tissue instead of 12 kg of fat. Glycogen is kept because it is fast to mobilize and can give glucose, which fat cannot.

Problem 15.48

Predict what happens in a fat cell during a 24-hour fast compared with the fed state: which hormones are high, whether the fat-mobilizing lipase is on or off, whether the hydrolysis of stored triacylglycerol is complete or incomplete, what the two products are, and how each product travels in the blood and where it goes.

Answer
Glucagon (and, with stress, epinephrine) is high and insulin is low. The hormones activate the adipose lipase, so lipolysis is on. The hydrolysis is complete: each triacylglycerol gives glycerol + 3 fatty acids. The fatty acids are insoluble, so they bind the plasma protein albumin and travel to muscle, heart, and liver for β-oxidation (in the liver, excess acetyl CoA becomes ketone bodies). Glycerol dissolves in plasma and goes to the liver, where it is phosphorylated and oxidized to DHAP for gluconeogenesis. In the fed state insulin would switch the lipase off and fat would be stored instead.

More Practice: Clinical and Integrative

Problem 15.51

A nurse admits a 19-year-old with type 1 diabetes who has vomiting, deep rapid breathing, fruity breath, blood glucose 480 mg/dL, pH 7.12, and low bicarbonate. (a) Name the condition. (b) Explain, starting from the missing hormone, why ketone bodies are being made. (c) Why is a blood β-hydroxybutyrate level more informative than a urine ketone strip here? (d) Why might the urine strip read higher as the patient improves?

Answer
(a) Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). (b) Without insulin, hormone-sensitive lipase in adipose tissue is not switched off, so lipolysis runs unopposed; fatty acids flood the liver, where β-oxidation makes acetyl CoA faster than the citric acid cycle can use it (oxaloacetate is being drawn into gluconeogenesis), and the excess is converted to acetoacetate, β-hydroxybutyrate, and acetone. The two acids consume bicarbonate and lower pH. (c) In DKA most of the ketone body is β-hydroxybutyrate, which the strip does not detect. (d) As insulin treatment works, β-hydroxybutyrate is oxidized back to acetoacetate, which the strip does detect, so urine ketones can rise briefly while blood ketones are actually falling.

Problem 15.52

A patient with cirrhosis (severe liver scarring) becomes confused and has a blood ammonia level three times normal. His ALT and AST were very high two years ago but are now near normal. (a) Which pathway has failed to cause the confusion, and what is the condition called? (b) Why does a failing liver that once had very high ALT and AST now show near-normal values? (c) The patient is given lactulose. Using acid–base chemistry, explain how it lowers blood ammonia.

Answer
(a) The urea cycle, which runs only in the liver, can no longer convert NH4+ to urea; the result is hyperammonemia with hepatic encephalopathy. (b) ALT and AST are enzymes inside liver cells that leak into the blood when cells are being damaged; once most of the cells have been destroyed and replaced by scar, there are few left to leak, so values fall even though function is poor. (c) Colon bacteria ferment lactulose to organic acids, lowering the pH of the gut contents; NH3 that diffuses in is protonated to NH4+, which cannot diffuse back across the membrane and is excreted in stool.

Problem 15.53

During a dental hygiene appointment, a patient mentions she has been on a very-low-carbohydrate diet for three weeks. You notice a faint fruity breath odor. Her last blood glucose was normal and she feels well. A second patient has the same odor but is also very thirsty, urinating often, and has lost 5 kg without trying. Explain the chemistry behind the odor in both, and decide which patient needs an urgent referral and why.

Answer
In both, the liver is making ketone bodies from excess acetyl CoA, and acetone (formed when acetoacetate loses CO2) is volatile and exhaled. Patient 1 is in nutritional ketosis: low carbohydrate, low insulin, modest controlled lipolysis, normal glucose, buffers coping. Patient 2 shows signs of undiagnosed diabetes: unopposed lipolysis (weight loss) and hyperglycemia with glucose and water lost in urine (thirst, urination), so ketone production may progress to ketoacidosis. Patient 2 needs same-day referral for blood glucose testing; patient 1 needs only routine advice.

Problem 15.54

A child with a urea cycle disorder (ornithine transcarbamoylase deficiency) is kept on a protein-restricted diet, and a child with phenylketonuria (PKU) is kept on a phenylalanine-restricted diet. Explain the biochemical reason for each restriction, name the molecule that accumulates in each, and explain why the PKU child must still receive some phenylalanine and extra tyrosine.

Answer
OTC deficiency: the urea cycle cannot convert carbamoyl phosphate to citrulline, so NH4+ from amino acid breakdown accumulates (hyperammonemia); limiting protein limits the nitrogen that must be disposed of. PKU: phenylalanine hydroxylase is missing, so phenylalanine cannot become tyrosine and accumulates (with phenylketones). Phenylalanine is an essential amino acid needed for protein synthesis, so a small amount must be given; tyrosine, normally nonessential because it is made from phenylalanine, becomes essential and must be supplied.

Problem 15.55

Integrative. A patient has not eaten for three days. Trace the fate of (a) a glucose unit from the last of the liver glycogen, (b) a palmitic acid from adipose tissue, and (c) an alanine from muscle protein, naming the hub molecule each passes through and whether it can supply glucose to the brain. Problem 15.60

Compare fuels. Palmitic acid (16 C) and glucose are both oxidized completely. (a) How many glucose molecules have the same number of carbons as one palmitic acid? (b) Compare the ATP from that many glucose molecules with the ATP from one palmitic acid (106). (c) Which fuel gives more ATP per carbon, and why?

Answer
(a) 16 ÷ 6 is not a whole number, so compare 3 glucose (18 C) with palmitate (16 C), or just compute per carbon. (b) 3 glucose = 96 ATP for 18 carbons (5.3 per carbon); palmitate = 106 ATP for 16 carbons (6.6 per carbon). (c) The fatty acid, because its carbons are more reduced (CH2 groups with no oxygen) and so have more hydrogen to hand to NAD+ and FAD; glucose carbons each already carry an oxygen. Problem 15.61

A ketogenesis step is described: “Acetoacetyl CoA reacts with a third acetyl CoA and water to form HMG-CoA, which is then cleaved to acetoacetate and acetyl CoA.” (a) Name the two reaction types in order. (b) How can you tell each one? (c) What happens to acetoacetate next, under two different circumstances, and what type of reaction is each?

Answer
(a) Condensation, then cleavage. (b) In the condensation two molecules (4 C + 2 C) join into a larger 6-carbon one; in the cleavage the 6-carbon HMG-CoA is split into two smaller pieces (4 C acetoacetate + 2 C acetyl CoA) by a lyase, without water or oxidation. (c) Acetoacetate is reduced (hydrogenated) by NADH to β-hydroxybutyrate, a 2° alcohol (dehydrogenase, oxidoreductase), or it loses CO2 spontaneously (decarboxylation) to give acetone, which is exhaled.

Problem 15.62

Multiple choice. The first committed step of lipogenesis converts acetyl CoA (2 C) to malonyl CoA (3 C) while ATP becomes ADP. This reaction is a

  1. decarboxylation by a lyase
  2. carboxylation by a ligase
  3. hydrogenation by an oxidoreductase
  4. hydrolysis by a hydrolase
Answer
B. The carbon count rises from 2 to 3 because CO2 is added (carboxylation), and ATP is spent to join the pieces, which is what a ligase does (acetyl CoA carboxylase). Malonyl CoA then becomes malonyl ACP, the two-carbon donor for each round of chain elongation.

Problem 15.63

Compare and contrast. Step 4 of lipogenesis reduces a C=C to a C–C single bond with NADPH; step 1 of β-oxidation oxidizes a C–C single bond to a C=C with FAD. Set up the table for both and explain why the cell uses different coenzymes, different compartments, and different hormones for the two directions.

Answer
Lipogenesis step 4: hydrogenation (reduction); two H added across the double bond and NADPH → NADP+; enoyl ACP reductase, an oxidoreductase; coenzyme NADPH; cytosol; switched on by insulin. β-Oxidation step 1: dehydrogenation (oxidation); two H removed and FAD → FADH2; acyl CoA dehydrogenase, an oxidoreductase; coenzyme FAD; mitochondrial matrix; switched on by glucagon and epinephrine. Keeping the pathways in different compartments with different coenzymes and opposite hormonal signals lets the cell build fat after a meal and burn it during fasting without the two running at once and wasting energy.

Problem 15.67

Twenty amino acids are degraded to only seven different products. (a) List the seven. (b) Which of them make an amino acid glucogenic, and which make it ketogenic? Problem 15.68

Fat digestion in the small intestine is an incomplete hydrolysis, while fat mobilization from adipose tissue is a complete hydrolysis. Name the enzyme and the products in each case, state the reaction type and enzyme class, and explain what the bile salts do that no enzyme does.

Answer
Intestine: pancreatic lipase hydrolyzes two of the three ester bonds, giving two fatty acids and a monoacylglycerol, which are repackaged into triacylglycerol inside the intestinal cell and shipped in chylomicrons. Adipose tissue: a hormone-activated lipase hydrolyzes all three ester bonds, giving glycerol and three fatty acids that leave for the liver (glycerol) and the tissues (fatty acids on albumin). Both are hydrolysis reactions by hydrolases, with no coenzyme. Bile salts are not enzymes; they act like soaps, emulsifying the insoluble fat into small micelles so that the water-soluble lipase can reach the ester bonds.

  2. 9 acetyl CoA; 9 turns; 9 NADH and 9 FADH2
  3. 18 acetyl CoA; 18 turns; 18 NADH and 18 FADH2
  4. 6 acetyl CoA; 6 turns; 6 NADH and 6 FADH2
Answer
A. Each turn removes two carbons as acetyl CoA, so 18 carbons give 9 acetyl CoA, but the last turn produces two at once, so only 8 turns are needed, each making 1 NADH and 1 FADH2. Each turn repeats the three-reaction sequence from the citric acid cycle (Chapter 14): oxidize to a trans alkene (FAD, Chapter 3), hydrate (Chapter 3), oxidize the 2° alcohol to a ketone (NAD+, Chapters 4 and 5), then split off acetyl CoA. ATP: 9 × 10 + 8 × 2.5 + 8 × 1.5 − 2 (activation) = about 120 ATP, nearly four times the yield of one glucose, which is why fat is the body’s long-term fuel.

Cumulative Problem 15.2 (Chapters 4, 5, 6 and 15)

The three ketone bodies are acetoacetate, CH3COCH2COO−; β-hydroxybutyrate, CH3CH(OH)CH2COO−; and acetone. (a) Name the functional groups in each. (b) Which one is not actually a ketone? (c) How is β-hydroxybutyrate made from acetoacetate, and how is acetone made from it? (d) Why do large amounts of ketone bodies lower blood pH, and which one is exhaled?

Answer
(a) Acetoacetate: a ketone and a carboxylate. β-Hydroxybutyrate: a 2° alcohol and a carboxylate. Acetone: a ketone only. (b) β-Hydroxybutyrate; it keeps the name only because it is made from a ketone body. (c) Reduction of acetoacetate’s ketone to a 2° alcohol by NADH (Chapter 5); decarboxylation (loss of CO2, spontaneous in blood) of acetoacetate gives acetone. (d) Acetoacetate and β-hydroxybutyrate are carboxylic acids (Chapter 6) released as H+ plus anion; in starvation or uncontrolled diabetes they overwhelm the bicarbonate buffer, causing ketoacidosis. Acetone is volatile and cannot be metabolized, so it leaves in the breath, giving the fruity odor a dental hygienist may notice first.

Cumulative Problem 15.3 (Chapters 5, 7, 11 and 15)

Alanine + α-ketoglutarate → pyruvate + glutamate. (a) What type of reaction is this, and what group moves? (b) Identify the functional groups that are exchanged, in Chapter 5 and 7 terms. (c) Which vitamin supplies the coenzyme, and what is the class of the enzyme?

Answer
(a) A transamination: the amino group of alanine is transferred to the keto acid α-ketoglutarate. (b) Alanine’s amine (Chapter 7) becomes a ketone (pyruvate, Chapter 5), while α-ketoglutarate’s ketone becomes an amine (glutamate). The carbon skeletons swap functional groups; nothing is lost. (c) Vitamin B6 (pyridoxal phosphate, PLP) is the coenzyme that carries the amino group; the enzyme is a transferase (an aminotransferase, Chapter 11). The blood test for liver damage, ALT (alanine aminotransferase), measures this very enzyme leaking from injured liver cells.

Cumulative Problem 15.4 (Chapters 6, 7 and 15)

Ammonium ion released by deamination is toxic and is converted in the liver to urea, H2NCONH2. (a) Describe urea in functional-group terms. (b) Why is urea a safe molecule to carry in blood while NH4+ is not? (c) The blood test “BUN” measures urea. What two organs does it report on, and how?

Answer
(a) Urea is a diamide: a carbonyl carbon bonded to two –NH2 groups, the double amide of carbonic acid (Chapter 7). (b) Urea is neutral, very water soluble, and unreactive; ammonia/ammonium is a base (Chapter 7) that disturbs the pH and the nitrogen balance inside cells, especially brain cells. Packing two nitrogens into one harmless molecule also saves water during excretion. (c) The liver makes urea and the kidneys excrete it, so a high BUN usually means the kidneys are not clearing it (or protein breakdown is excessive), while a very low BUN can mean the liver is too damaged to make it.

Cumulative Problem 15.5 (Chapters 9, 13, 14 and 15)

During a fast, the liver makes new glucose for the brain. (a) Alanine can be converted to glucose; leucine cannot. Give the terms for these two kinds of amino acids and explain what alanine is converted to first. (b) Fatty acids cannot be converted to glucose at all, even though they yield plenty of acetyl CoA. Why not? (c) What part of a fat molecule can become glucose?

Answer
(a) Alanine is glucogenic: transamination turns it into pyruvate, which gluconeogenesis (Chapter 13) converts to glucose. Leucine is ketogenic: its carbons end up only as acetyl CoA and acetoacetate. (b) Acetyl CoA enters the citric acid cycle (Chapter 14) by joining oxaloacetate, but the cycle then releases both acetyl carbons as CO2 before any oxaloacetate is regenerated, so there is no net gain of the carbon skeleton gluconeogenesis needs. Animals have no pathway from acetyl CoA to glucose. (c) The glycerol backbone (Chapter 9): hydrolysis of the triacylglycerol releases it, and the liver converts glycerol to a glycolytic intermediate and then to glucose, a small but real contribution.

Cumulative Problem 15.6 (Chapters 6, 8, 9, 10, 13, 14 and 15)

Putting the whole course together. A patient has eaten nothing for 24 hours before oral surgery. Trace what the body is doing for fuel, naming the chapter chemistry at each step: (a) the first few hours, (b) after liver glycogen is gone, (c) what the brain uses after a day or two of fasting, and (d) why a long fast makes the breath smell and the blood more acidic.

Answer
(a) Blood glucose is held up by glycogenolysis: glucagon signals the liver to hydrolyze glycogen’s α-glycosidic (acetal) bonds (Chapter 8) and release glucose (Chapter 13). (b) With glycogen gone (about 12–18 hours), hormone-sensitive lipase in fat tissue hydrolyzes the ester bonds of triacylglycerols (Chapters 6 and 9), releasing fatty acids and glycerol. Muscle and liver burn the fatty acids by β-oxidation (Chapter 15) through the citric acid cycle and electron transport (Chapter 14); the liver makes new glucose from glycerol and from amino acids freed by breaking down muscle protein (peptide-bond hydrolysis, Chapter 10; transamination, Chapter 15). (c) The brain cannot use fatty acids, so the liver converts excess acetyl CoA into ketone bodies, which the brain burns instead of glucose, sparing muscle protein. (d) Acetoacetate and β-hydroxybutyrate are carboxylic acids (Chapter 6) and lower the blood pH; acetone, formed by decarboxylation, is exhaled, giving a fruity odor. A patient told to fast before surgery is, chemically, a textbook of Chapters 6 through 15 in one person.
