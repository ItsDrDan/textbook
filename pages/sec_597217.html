Learning Objectives

  • Tell essential from nonessential amino acids and explain why some amino acids are conditionally essential.
  • Describe how nonessential amino acids are made from metabolic intermediates, mainly by transamination.
  • Explain the link between phenylalanine, tyrosine and phenylketonuria (PKU).

In humans, only half of the standard amino acids (Glu, Gln, Pro, Asp, Asn, Ala, Gly, Ser, Tyr, Cys) can be synthesized (Figure \(\PageIndex{12}\) and 13), and are thus classified the nonessential amino acids. Within this group, the first three, glutamate, glutamine, and proline, have a shared anabolic pathway. It begins with glutamate dehydrogenase, which adds ammonia to α-ketoglutarate in the presence of NADPH to form glutamate. This is a key reaction for all amino acid synthesis: glutamate is a nitrogen (amino group) donor for the production of all the other amino acids.

Glutamine synthetase catalyzes the formation of glutamine from glutamate and ammonia. This is an important biochemical reaction for a completely different reason: it is the primary route for ammonia detoxification.

Proline is synthesized from glutamate in a two-step process that begins with the reduction of glutamate to a semialdehyde form that spontaneously cyclizes to D-pyrroline-5-carboxylate. This is reduced by pyrroline carboxylate reductase to proline.

Alanine and Aspartate are the products of glutamate-based transamination of pyruvate and oxaloacetate, respectively.

Asparagine is synthesized through one of two known pathways. In bacteria, an asparagine synthetase combines aspartate and ammonia. However, in mammals, the aspartate gets its amino group from glutamine.

The synthesis of serine begins with the metabolic intermediate 3-phosphoglycerate (glycolysis). Phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase oxidizes it to 3-phosphohydroxypyruvate. An amino group is donated by glutamate in a reaction catalyzed by phosphoserine transaminase, forming 3-phosphoserine, and finally the phosphate is removed by phosphoserine phosphatase to produce serine.

Serine is the immediate precursor to glycine, which is formed by serine hydroxymethyltransferase. This enzyme requires the coenzyme tetrahydrofolate (THF), which is a derivative of vitamin B9 (folic acid).

Serine is also a precursor for cysteine, although the synthesis of cysteine actually begins with the essential amino acid methionine. Methionine is converted to S-adenosylmethionine by methionine adenosyltransferase. This is then converted to S-adenosylhomocysteine by a member of the SAM-dependent methylase family. The sugar is removed by adenosylhomocysteinase, and the resultant homocysteine is connected by cystathionine synthase to the serine molecule to form cystathionine. Finally, cystathionine-g-lyase catalyzes the production of cysteine.

Tyrosine is another amino acid that depends on an essential amino acid as a precursor. In this case, the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase oxidizes phenylalanine to tyrosine, using O2 to add an –OH group to the aromatic ring. It is the enzyme missing in phenylketonuria (PKU).

In general, the synthesis of essential amino acids, usually in microorganisms, is much more complex than for the nonessential amino acids and is best left to a full-fledged biochemistry course.

Lecture Connection

The lecture’s version of this section is short. The body makes the nonessential amino acids from glycolysis and citric acid cycle intermediates, adding nitrogen by transamination. It cannot make the essential ones, because it cannot build their carbon skeletons. Those must come from food. Three nonessential amino acids are made in a single transamination step; tyrosine is made from an essential one; the rest take several reactions. That, plus the list of essential amino acids, is what you are responsible for.

Professor Tip

This course counts ten nonessential and ten essential amino acids (arginine counted as essential; see below). The pathway details here (glutamine synthetase, S-adenosylmethionine, cystathionine, tetrahydrofolate) are background.

Essential versus nonessential

The body builds the carbon skeletons of the nonessential amino acids from glycolysis and citric acid cycle intermediates. It then adds nitrogen by transamination (Section 15.2). These routes are shorter than the routes to the essential amino acids. Nine are essential for all adults; the mnemonic PVT TIM HaLL lists them: Phenylalanine, Valine, Threonine, Tryptophan, Isoleucine, Methionine, Histidine, Leucine, Lysine. The lower-case “a” is arginine. The urea cycle makes it (Section 15.10), but not fast enough in infants or during severe illness. So many GOB textbooks and the lecture slides count it as a tenth essential amino acid, which leaves ten nonessential. Tyrosine and cysteine are also conditionally essential, for a different reason. Tyrosine is made from phenylalanine, and cysteine’s sulfur comes from methionine. The body can make them only when it has enough of those two essential amino acids. Three nonessential amino acids (alanine, aspartate, and glutamate) are made in one step: transamination of the matching α-keto acid (pyruvate, oxaloacetate, or α-ketoglutarate). These are the ALT and AST reactions of Section 15.2 running in reverse. The other nonessential amino acids take several more reactions.

Practice: Oxaloacetate accepts an amino group from glutamate. Name the amino acid formed, the reaction type, the enzyme class, and the coenzyme, and state whether the product is essential or nonessential.

Answer
Aspartate. Transamination (amino-group transfer), by an aminotransferase (AST), a transferase, with pyridoxal phosphate (vitamin B6). Nonessential: its carbon skeleton is a citric acid cycle intermediate the body already has.

Health Career Connection

PKU and sugar-free products. People with phenylketonuria (PKU) lack phenylalanine hydroxylase, so they cannot convert phenylalanine to tyrosine. Phenylalanine and its by-products build up and damage the developing brain, which is why every newborn is screened and why the diet restricts phenylalanine while supplying tyrosine, which has become essential for them. The sweetener aspartame is the methyl ester of a dipeptide made of aspartate and phenylalanine. Digestion releases the phenylalanine. That is why sugar-free gums, diet sodas, and some mouth products carry the warning “Phenylketonurics: contains phenylalanine.” For a dental hygienist recommending sugar-free gum to a patient with PKU, xylitol-sweetened products are the alternative (Section 13.5). For a nurse, the lesson is the same one this section teaches: an amino acid is “nonessential” only as long as the enzyme that makes it works.

Summary

  • The body can make the 10 nonessential amino acids from metabolic intermediates, mostly by transamination; the 10 essential amino acids (arginine counted as essential in this course) must come from the diet.
  • Tyrosine is made by oxidizing phenylalanine (phenylalanine hydroxylase); people with phenylketonuria (PKU) lack this enzyme, so tyrosine becomes essential for them.
