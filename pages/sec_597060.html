Learning Objectives

  • Read and draw molecular, Lewis, condensed, and structural formulae of simple organic compounds.
  • Understand the mixed versions of and slight variations in drawing the formulae of simple organic compounds.

The molecular formula, Lewis formula, condensed formula, skeletal formula, or a combination of these can represent an organic compound. These ways of representing organic compounds and what they mean are described below.

Molecular formula

The molecular formula tells the symbols of the elements that compose the compound, and the subscript to the element symbol denotes how many atoms of that element are in the molecule.

For example, (\(\ce{CH4}\)) is the molecular formula of methane which means there is one carbon and four hydrogen atoms in a methane molecule. \(\ce{C2H6}\) is the molecular formula of ethane, which means the ethane molecule has two carbon and six hydrogen atoms. Molecular formulas do not tell about the molecule's bonds and shapes.

Lewis formula

The Lewis structure or Lewis formula shows all the bonding electron pairs as lines (bonds) and lone pairs (non-bonding electron pairs) as pairs of dots around each atom in a molecule.

For example, Lewis formula of ethane (\(\ce{C2H6}\)) is: \(\ce{\small{\ce{H}}-\overset{\overset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\underset{\underset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}}-\overset{\overset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\underset{\underset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}}-H}\) that shows each carbon is bonded with one \(\ce{C}\) and three \(\ce{H}'s\) by single bonds. Similarly, Lewis formula of formaldehyde (\(\ce{CH2O}\)) is: \(\ce{\small{\ce{H}}-{\underset{\underset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}}=\overset{\huge{\cdot\cdot}}{O}\!\!\Large:}\) that shows \(\ce{C}\) is bonded with two \(\ce{H}'s\) by single bonds and with one \(\ce{O}\) by a double bond and \(\ce{O}\) has two lone pairs on it. The lone pairs are usually omitted from Lewis structures except when needed to emphasize their presence. For example, Lewis formula for methanol (\(\ce{CH4O}\)) with lone pairs is: \(\ce{\small{\ce{H}}-\overset{\overset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\underset{\underset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}}-\overset{\huge{\cdot\cdot}}{\underset{\huge{\cdot\cdot}}{O}}-H}\), but it can also be shown as \(\ce{\small{\ce{H}}-\overset{\overset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\underset{\underset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}}-{O}-H}\) where the lone pairs are not shown on \(\ce{O}\) but it is understood that two lone pairs are there.

Condensed formula

The Lewis formulas become complicated and time-consuming for larger organic compounds.

Condensed formula simplifies the Lewis formula by writing each \(\ce{C}\) followed by \(\ce{H's}\) attached with it. Subscripts are used to show more than one \(\ce{H's}\). If there is a heteroatom, i.e., any atom other than \(\ce{C}\) or \(\ce{H}\) in the chain, it is condensed like \(\ce{C}\), except for halogens which are condensed like \(\ce{H}\).

For example, the Lewis formula of ethane \(\ce{\small{\ce{H}}-\overset{\overset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\underset{\underset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}}-\overset{\overset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\underset{\underset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}}-H}\) is condensed as \(\ce{CH3CH3}\). Lewis formula for methanol \(\ce{\small{\ce{H}}-\overset{\overset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\underset{\underset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}}-{O}-H}\) is condensed as \(\ce{CH3OH}\). Lewis formula for ethanamine \(\ce{\small{\ce{H}}-\overset{\overset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\underset{\underset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}}-\overset{\overset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\underset{\underset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}}-{\underset{\underset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{N}}}-H}\) is condensed as \(\ce{CH3CH2NH2}\). Lewis formula for 2-chloropropane \(\ce{\small{\ce{H}}-\overset{\overset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\underset{\underset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}}-\overset{\overset{\huge{\ce{Cl}}}|}{\underset{\underset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}}-\overset{\overset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\underset{\underset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}}-H}\), is condensed as \(\ce{CH3CHClCH3}\).

Skeletal formula

The Lewis and condensed formulas do not show the geometry of the organic compounds. Further, the Lewis and condensed formulas become complicated and time-consuming for large organic compounds.

Skeletal formulas or line-angle formulas overcome these drawbacks by simplifying the representation of organic compounds by omitting \(\ce{C's}\) and \(\ce{H's}\) in the formula and showing only the skeleton of the compound by \(\ce{C}\)-to-\(\ce{C}\) bonds as lines in a geometry that is closer to the actual geometry. Any heteroatom and \(\ce{H's}\) attached to the heteroatom are shown in the skeletal formula.

Some terms related to the primary classification of organic compounds

  • Before learning skeletal formulas, it is essential to understand the following terms related to the primary type of organic compounds.
  • Organic compounds containing only \(\ce{C's}\) and \(\ce{H's}\) are called hydrocarbons. For example, ethane (\(\ce{CH3CH3}\)), ethene (\(\ce{CH2=CH2}\)), and ethyne (\(\ce{HC#CH}\)) described in previous sections are hydrocarbons.
  • The hydrocarbons that contain only single bonds are called alkanes. For example, methane (\(\ce{CH4}\)) and ethane (\(\ce{CH3CH3}\)) are alkanes.
    • Alkanes containing a chain of \(\ce{C's}\) where \(\ce{C's}\) are connected with either one or two \(\ce{C's}\) are called straight chain alkanes or normal-chain alkanes (n-alkanes). For example, n-alkane having four carbons is n-butane (\(\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH3}\)), five carbons is n-pentane (\(\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2CH3}\)), six carbons is n-hexane (\(\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2CH2CH3}\)), and so on.
  • Alkanes in which at least one \(\ce{C}\) is connected to three or four other \(\ce{C's}\) are called branched-chain alkanes. For example, isobutane (2-methylpropane) \(\ce{\small{\ce{H}}-\overset{\overset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\underset{\underset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}}-\!-\!-\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\overset{\overset{\huge{\ce{H}-\overset{\overset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}-\ce{H}}}|}{\underset{\underset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!-\!-\!-\overset{\overset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\underset{\underset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}}-H}\) is a branched-chain alkane.
  • The hydrocarbons containing at least one double bond are called alkenes. For example, ethene

    \(\ce{\small{\ce{H}}-{\underset{\underset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}}={\underset{\underset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}}-H}\) is an alkene.

  • The hydrocarbons containing at least one triple-bond are called alkynes. For example, ethyne \(\ce{\small{\ce{H-C≡C-H}}}\) is an alkyne.
  • Hydrocarbons that contain a benzene ring, a flat ring of six carbons drawn with three alternating double bonds, are a special class called aromatic hydrocarbons (Section 3.7).
  • Organic compounds containing at least one heteroatom, i.e., \(\ce{O}\), \(\ce{N}\), \(\ce{S}\), \(\ce{P}\), etc. are not hydrocarbons. For example, methanol \(\ce{CH3OH}\) is not a hydrocarbon. Several classes of organic compounds are not hydrocarbons, which will be described later.

Replacing any \(\ce{H}\) with another tetrahedral \(\ce{C}\) results in ethane (\(\ce{CH3CH3}\)) as shown in row two of Table 1. A line drawn to represent \(\ce{C-C}\) bond in ethane is the skeletal formula of ethane, as shown in row two of Table 1. When 2nd \(\ce{H}\) of methane is also replaced with another tetrahedral \(\ce{C}\), it results in propane (\(\ce{CH3CH2CH3}\)), as shown in row three of Table 1. Two \(\ce{H's}\) of methane in the plane of the page have been replaced with \(\ce{C's}\) in this case resulting in \(\ce{C-C-C}\) bonds in an inverted V-shape in the plane of the page. The skeletal formula representing a propane molecule is two lines connected in an inverted V-shape, as shown in row three of Table 1.

Replacing any \(\ce{H}\) of a terminal \(\ce{C}\) of a propane with another tetrahedral \(\ce{C}\) results in butane (\(\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH3}\)) as shown in 4th row of Table 1. Remember rotation around \(\ce{C-C}\) single bond happens. Therefore, the 4th \(\ce{C}\) of butane can be placed at any angle relative to the plane defined by the other three \(\ce{C's}\). However, placing all four \(\ce{C's}\) of butane in the same plane is the most stable arrangement because the terminal \(\ce{C's}\), which are the bulkiest groups attached to the internal \(\ce{C's}\), are farthest apart in this arrangement. Three lines connected by zigzag is the skeletal formula representing butane, as shown in row 4th of Table 1. The zigzag lines representing a chain of 4 \(\ce{C's}\) can be extended to represent a chain of 5 \(\ce{C's}\) by adding one line, a chain of 6 \(\ce{C's}\) by adding two lines, and so on, as shown in Table 1 for the cases of n-alkanes having a chain of 2 to 12 \(\ce{C's}\).

In summary, the skeletal formula of n-alkane is a line or lines connected zigzag representing \(\ce{C-C}\) bonds. It is understood that:

  • the terminals (end) and corners (bends) of the lines are \(\ce{C's}\), and
  • each \(\ce{C}\) has four bonds, so the bonds which are not shown by the lines are the bonds to \(\ce{H's}\).

With this knowledge, it is clear that a line or lines connected zigzag way are the structural formulas that represent structures of n-alkanes without showing \(\ce{C's}\) and \(\ce{H's}\) in the formula.

Stem names of organic compounds

Names of n-alkanes without the last syllable, i.e., without -ane, are the stem names that represent the number of \(\ce{C's}\) in the organic compound. For example, meth- from methane represents one \(\ce{C}\), eth- from ethane represents two \(\ce{C's}\), prop- from propane represents three \(\ce{C's}\), and so on. These stem names will be described later in reference to name organic compounds.

Homologous series

It is evident from comparing the molecular formulas of n-alkanes shown in Table 1 that n-alkanes differ from each other by a \(\ce{CH2}\) or a multiple of \(\ce{CH2}\) units. For example, add a \(\ce{CH2}\) to methane (\(\ce{CH4}\)) to convert it to ethane (\(\ce{C2H6}\)), and two \(\ce{CH2}\) units to convert it to propane (\(\ce{C3H8}\)) and so on. Series of organic compounds that differ from each other by a \(\ce{CH2}\) or multiple of \(\ce{CH2}\)'s are homologous series. All of the n-Alkanes shown in Table 1 are members of a homologous series.

General formula of n-alkanes

The general formula of n-alkanes is \(\ce{C_{n}H_{2n + 2}}\) where n is a counting number, i.e., 1, 2, 3,... For example, when n = 1, it is methane \(\ce{C_{1}H_{2\times{1} + 2}}\) = \(\ce{CH4}\) (Recall: that when the subscript to the element symbol in the formula is 1, it is not written.). When n = 2, it is ethane \(\ce{C_{2}H_{2\times{2} + 2}}\) = \(\ce{C2H6}\), and so on

Variations in the structural formula to represent different conformations

The structural formulas in Table 1 represent the linear conformation of n-alkanes which is the most stable conformation. Remember: rotation is possible around any single bond. For example, n-hexane shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\)a is rotated around the middle \(\ce{C-C}\) bond to acquire a new conformation shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\)b and rotated further along the 2nd \(\ce{C-C}\) from left to acquire another conformation shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\)c. All three structural formulas shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\)a, b, and c represent the same molecule. Remember: different shapes of the same molecule obtained by rotation around a single bond are different conformations of the same molecule.

Condensed and skeletal formulas of branched-alkanes

Replacing one or both \(\ce{H's}\) of a non-terminal carbon of straight chain alkane (n-alkane) with a \(\ce{C}\) or a \(\ce{C}\) chain results in a branched-alkane. Replacing hydrogen on the middle carbon of propane ( \(\ce{\small{\ce{H}}-\overset{\overset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\underset{\underset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}}-\overset{\overset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\underset{\underset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}}-\overset{\overset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\underset{\underset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}}-H}\) ) with a \(\ce{C}\) results in isobutane, or 2-methylpropane (\(\ce{\small{\ce{H}}-\overset{\overset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\underset{\underset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}}-\!-\!-\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\overset{\overset{\huge{\ce{H}-\overset{\overset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}-\ce{H}}}|}{\underset{\underset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!-\!-\!-\overset{\overset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\underset{\underset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}}-H}\)) which is a branched chain alkane.

There are two ways to show the condensed formula of the branched alkane:

  • show the condensed formula of the branch within small brackets next to the carbon it is attached to, e.g., \(\ce{CH3CH(CH3)CH3}\) is the condensed formula of isobutane (2-methylpropane);
  • show the condensed formula of the branch hanging above or below the carbon it is attached, e.g., \(\ce{\small{\ce{CH3}}-\overset{\overset{\huge{\!\!\!\!\ce{CH}_{\Large 3}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!}}|}{\ce{C}}H-CH3}\) is the condensed formula of isobutane (2-methylpropane).

The skeletal formula of the branched alkane shows the skeletal formula of the branch with the terminal connected to the carbon in the main branch to which it is attached. For example, is the skeletal formula of isobutane (2-methylpropane). Figure \(\PageIndex{3}\) shows another example of branched alkane and its condensed and skeletal formulas.

Skeletal formulas of alkenes

Hydrocarbons containing at least one double bond (\(\ce{C=C}\) bond) are called alkenes.

Since the two \(\ce{C's}\) at the double bond have trigonal planar geometry with bond angles 120o, the double bond between them fits in the zigzag skeletal structure, like alkanes, except that two lines are drawn where there is a double bond in the chain.

Skeletal formulas of alkynes

Hydrocarbons containing at least one triple bond (\(\ce{C≡C}\)) are called alkynes.

Since the two \(\ce{C's}\) at the triple bond have linear geometry, three lines show the triple bond and the two single bonds to the other atoms attached to them are drawn in line with the triple bond. The zigzag skeletal structure shows the rest of the structure as usual.

  • its symbol in the skeletal formula shows it and
  • any \(\ce{H}\) present on the heteroatom is also shown next to the heteroatom as in the condensed formulas.

Variations in the formulas representing organic compounds

The condensed and the skeletal formulas are usually presented in organic chemistry, as described in the previous sections. However, they may be varied slightly according to the need.

If needed, mixed Lewis, condensed, and skeletal structures emphasize a particular part of the molecule.

For example, the \(\ce{H's}\) are usually written to the right of \(\ce{C}\) or to the right of heteroatoms in the condensed formula, but sometimes this order may be reversed. For example, Lewis formula for methanol \(\ce{\small{\ce{H}}-\overset{\overset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\underset{\underset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}}-{O}\!-H}\) is presented in condensed form as \(\ce{CH3OH}\), but it may be condensed as \(\ce{H3COH}\), or as \(\ce{H3C-OH}\) to emphasize the \(\ce{C-O}\)-bond. Similarly, Lewis formula of acetone \(\ce{\small{\ce{H}}-\overset{\overset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\underset{\underset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}}-\overset{\overset{\huge{\ce{O}\!}}||}{\ce{C}}-\overset{\overset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\underset{\underset{\huge{\ce{H}}}|}{\ce{C}}}-H}\) is changed to skeletal formula as , but it may also be shown as to emphasize the \(\ce{C-H}\) bond.

Sometimes the formula is further condensed by placing the repeating units within a bracket and subscript outside the bracket to show the number of repeat units. For example, the condensed formula of isobutane (2-methylpropane) is \(\ce{\small{\ce{CH3}}-\overset{\overset{\huge{\!\!\!\!\ce{CH}_{\Large 3}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!}}|}{\ce{C}}H-CH3}\) that has three \(\ce{CH3}\) units attached to the central \(\ce{C}\). It can be further condensed as \(\ce{(CH3)3CH}\). Similarly, the condensed formula of decane \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2CH2CH2CH2CH2CH2CH3}\) contains eight \(\ce{CH2}\) units and can be condensed further as \(\ce{CH3(CH2)8CH3}\).

One molecule, four ways to draw it

Molecules are three-dimensional objects, and how the atoms are connected is what distinguishes one molecule from another. Three kinds of 3-D model show that: a space-filling model (color-coded blobs whose merged electron clouds give the most realistic picture), a ball-and-stick model (color-coded balls with sticks for bonds), and a wedge–dash drawing (element symbols, with plain lines for bonds in the plane of the paper, wedges for bonds coming toward you, and dashes for bonds going behind the page). The 2-D structural formulas below give up the shape and keep only the connectivity.

Organic chemists draw one molecule four different ways, depending on how much detail they need. Reading a condensed formula

A condensed formula reads like a book, left to right: each carbon is followed by the atoms bonded to it, C–H bonds are understood, and bonds to a heteroatom (any atom that is not C or H) can be drawn or implied, so CH3–COOH, CH3C(=O)OH, and CH3COOH are all the same acetic acid. Identical groups in a row can be condensed further into parentheses with a subscript: CH3CH2CH2CH2OH and CH3(CH2)3OH are both correct. The one thing you may not do is condense until the connectivity is unclear: "(CH2)5CHOH" for cyclohexanol hides the ring, so a ring is drawn as a polygon instead. It is fine to write a group in reverse at the left end of a formula, such as HO– in HOCH2CH3, when that shows the bonds more clearly.

Reading a line structure

Line (skeletal) structures are the most stripped-down representation and the one organic chemists use most, because they are fast to draw. Three rules let you read any line structure:

  1. Every corner and every end of a line is a carbon atom.
  2. Each carbon has as many hidden hydrogens as it needs to reach four bonds. A carbon with two lines touching it has two hidden H's; a carbon at the end of a line has three.
  3. Nitrogen, oxygen, sulfur, and the halogens are always written, and so is any hydrogen bonded to them (the OH above). Those are the hydrogens that matter for hydrogen bonding, so they stay visible.

Professor Tip

To get a molecular formula from a line structure, count carbons first, then add hydrogens carbon by carbon until each has four bonds. Writing the condensed formula as an in-between step catches most mistakes. And when a question gives you only a molecular formula, remember that one formula can belong to several different molecules (C2H6O is both ethanol and dimethyl ether): always draw the structure.

Professor Tip

Three vocabulary notes. (1) The shapes a molecule takes on by rotating about its single bonds are conformations (Chapter 2), not configurations; configuration is reserved for arrangements that rotation cannot interconvert, such as cis/trans alkenes (Chapter 3) and chiral centers (Chapter 5). (2) If you see the name "isopropane" online, it is an error, not a nickname: CH3CH(CH3)CH3 is 2-methylpropane (common name isobutane). (3) "Lewis formula" and "skeletal formula" in this section mean the same as Lewis structure and line (skeletal) structure on exams; an expanded structural formula is a Lewis structure with the lone pairs left off.

Example 1: Writing a condensed formula from a line structure

Problem

Write the condensed structural formula of each molecule, condensing repeated CH2 groups where you can. (a) The seven-carbon chain below, 3-methylheptane, a component of gasoline. (b) Cyclopentanol, the five-membered ring with one OH.

Solution

(a) Read the chain from left to right, writing each carbon with its hidden hydrogens. The first carbon is an end (CH3), the second has two lines (CH2), the third has three lines because of the branch (CH), and the branch itself is a CH3 written in parentheses right after the carbon that holds it. Then come four more chain carbons: three CH2 and a final CH3. Written out: CH3CH2CH(CH3)CH2CH2CH2CH3. The three identical CH2 groups in a row can be collected: CH3CH2CH(CH3)(CH2)3CH3. Both versions are correct and say the same thing.

(b) The ring has five carbons: one CHOH and four CH2. A string such as "(CH2)4CHOH" is not acceptable, because it does not show that the first and last carbons are bonded to each other. For a ring, draw the polygon and write the groups on it, or draw the ring of CH2 units with bond lines closing the loop.

Check

Count hydrogens in (a): 3 + 2 + 1 + 3 + 3(2) + 3 = 18, and C8H18 fits CnH2n+2 for an eight-carbon alkane. In (b) the point of a structural formula is to show how the atoms are connected; if a condensed formula loses the ring, it has lost the structure. ✓

Summary

  • Organic molecules are drawn four ways: Lewis structure, expanded structural formula, condensed structural formula, and line (skeletal) structure. You must translate among them on sight.
  • In a condensed formula, each C is written with the H atoms attached to it; heteroatoms are condensed like C, and halogens like H.
  • In a line structure, every corner and line end is a carbon, each C has enough H atoms to make four bonds, and heteroatoms and the H atoms on them are always drawn.
  • A molecular formula alone does not tell you the structure; draw it to see which isomer you have.
