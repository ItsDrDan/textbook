Learning Objectives

  • Describe how dietary fats and proteins are digested and absorbed, naming the enzymes and their classes.
  • Know that glycerol is phosphorylated and oxidized to dihydroxyacetone phosphate, which can enter glycolysis or gluconeogenesis, and explain how stored fat is mobilized.
  • Know that fatty acids are metabolized by β-oxidation; describe activation, the carnitine shuttle, and the four reactions of each repetition (reaction type, how you can tell, enzyme class, coenzyme).
  • Given the length of a fatty acid chain, predict the number of acetyl CoA formed and the number of repeated β-oxidation cycles needed, and calculate the NADH, FADH2, and ATP.
  • Know the initial reactant and final product of ketogenesis (acetyl CoA → acetoacetate → β-hydroxybutyrate or acetone), lipogenesis (acetyl CoA → palmitic acid), and the urea cycle (NH4+ → urea); classify their steps.
  • Explain when and why ketone bodies form, and the difference between ketosis and ketoacidosis.
  • Contrast fatty acid synthesis (lipogenesis) with β-oxidation.
  • Describe transamination, oxidative deamination, and the urea cycle, including enzyme classes and coenzymes.
  • Classify amino acid carbon skeletons as glucogenic or ketogenic, list the essential amino acids, and explain nitrogen balance.
  • Draw the "hub" molecules where carbohydrate, lipid, and protein metabolism meet.

Chapter Summary

The big ideas of the chapter, one paragraph each. Each paragraph ends with the section where it is taught in full.

1. Digesting fats

About 98% of dietary lipids are triacylglycerols (TAG); excess energy from any food is stored as TAG in adipocytes. Salivary enzymes do little, gastric lipase hydrolyzes only about 10%, so the job falls to the small intestine. Bile salts emulsify fat globules into small droplets (a physical change, like soap) and later help form micelles that carry the digestion products to the intestinal cell; pancreatic lipase (a hydrolase) performs an incomplete ester hydrolysis to a monoacylglycerol plus two fatty acids; inside the intestinal cell the TAG is rebuilt and packaged into chylomicrons, which travel through the lymph to the blood; lipoprotein lipase at the tissues hydrolyzes it again. When carbohydrate runs short, glucagon and epinephrine activate an adipose lipase that completely hydrolyzes stored TAG to glycerol + 3 fatty acids; fatty acids travel bound to albumin. Details and the figure: Sections 15.1, 15.3, 15.5, and 15.6.

2. β-Oxidation (in the mitochondria)

Three stages: activation (acyl CoA synthetase, a ligase; ATP → AMP, so 2 ATP-equivalents, once), transport into the matrix by the carnitine shuttle, and the fatty acid spiral. Each repetition is four reactions, the citric acid cycle pattern plus a cleavage: (1) dehydrogenation, C–C → trans C=C, FAD → FADH2, oxidoreductase; (2) hydration to a 2° alcohol at the β carbon, lyase; (3) dehydrogenation, alcohol → ketone, NAD+ → NADH, oxidoreductase; (4) thiolysis by CoA–SH (thiolase, a transferase), releasing acetyl CoA and an acyl CoA two carbons shorter. For n carbons: acetyl CoA = n/2, repetitions = n/2 − 1 (the last cut gives two pieces). ATP = (acetyl CoA × 10) + (NADH × 2.5) + (FADH2 × 1.5) − 2. Stearic acid (18 C): 120 ATP; palmitic (16 C): 106. Fat beats carbohydrate per carbon (and about 2.4× the ATP per gram; 9 vs 4 kcal/g) because its carbons are more reduced. Table, figure, and the slicing tip: Section 15.3.

3. Ketone bodies

When the liver has more acetyl CoA than a citric acid cycle short of oxaloacetate can handle (fasting, low-carb diets, uncontrolled diabetes), ketogenesis makes acetoacetate (condensation of 2 acetyl CoA by thiolase in reverse, condensation with a third to HMG-CoA, then cleavage), β-hydroxybutyrate (hydrogenation of the ketone, NADH → NAD+, oxidoreductase), and acetone (spontaneous decarboxylation; leaves in the breath). Heart, muscle, and after a few days the brain burn them. Ketosis is accumulation the buffers can handle; ketoacidosis lowers blood pH (heavy breathing, dehydration, coma); in DKA insulin does not work and ketogenesis runs unchecked. Table, structures, and the clinical reading: Section 15.7.

4. Amino acid metabolism

Amino acids are not stored; the nitrogen comes off first, because the body cannot oxidize N-containing compounds. Transamination (aminotransferase, a transferase; coenzyme PLP from vitamin B6) moves the amino group to α-ketoglutarate, making glutamate and a new α-keto acid: ALT, alanine → pyruvate; AST, aspartate → oxaloacetate. Oxidative deamination of glutamate (glutamate dehydrogenase, an oxidoreductase, NAD+) releases toxic NH4+ and regenerates α-ketoglutarate. The urea cycle (liver; NH4+ → urea) has an entry step (carbamoyl phosphate synthetase, ligase, 2 ATP) and four steps: transfer (ornithine transcarbamoylase) → condensation with aspartate (ligase, ATP) → cleavage to arginine + fumarate (lyase) → hydrolysis to urea + ornithine (arginase, hydrolase); step 1 in the matrix, 2–4 in the cytosol; one N from NH4+, one from aspartate; cost 3 ATP. Carbon skeletons: 20 amino acids give 7 products; glucogenic skeletons reach pyruvate or a cycle intermediate, ketogenic ones reach acetyl CoA or acetoacetyl CoA (only Leucine and Lysine are purely ketogenic). Details, figures, and tables: Sections 15.2, 15.4, and 15.10.

5. Fat mobilization and glycerol

Glucagon and epinephrine activate hormone-sensitive lipase in adipose tissue (covalent modification; insulin does the opposite), releasing fatty acids (on albumin) and glycerol into the blood. Glycerol, the only part of a fat that can become glucose, goes to the liver and becomes DHAP in two steps: phosphorylation of the 1° OH (glycerol kinase, transferase, ATP → ADP) to glycerol 3-phosphate, then oxidation of the 2° alcohol to a ketone (glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase, oxidoreductase, NAD+ → NADH). DHAP enters glycolysis or gluconeogenesis; two glycerols make one glucose. Mobilization: Section 15.6; glycerol steps and figure: Section 15.3.

6. Getting fatty acids into the mitochondria

A carboxylic acid is unreactive, so acyl CoA synthetase (ligase) first makes the thioester fatty acyl CoA, splitting ATP to AMP + PPi (2 ATP-equivalents). Acyl CoA cannot cross the inner membrane, so carnitine acyltransferase moves the acyl group onto carnitine, acyl-carnitine crosses, and the acyl group is handed back to a matrix CoA (both transferase reactions). Malonyl CoA blocks the shuttle, so a cell building fat does not burn it. Details and the carnitine clinical note: Section 15.1.

7. Unsaturated fatty acids

An existing C=C means that repetition skips the FAD step and starts at step 2: one fewer FADH2 (1.5 fewer ATP) per double bond; a cis–trans isomerase first converts the natural cis bond to the trans-(2,3) bond hydration needs. Acetyl CoA and repetition counts do not change; oleic acid gives 120 − 1.5 = 118.5 ATP. Section 15.3.

8. Lipogenesis vs. β-oxidation

Lipogenesis (acetyl CoA → palmitic acid, 16 C) runs when intake exceeds need, in the cytosol, with NADPH (from the pentose phosphate pathway), on acyl carrier protein (ACP), under insulin. Acetyl CoA reaches the cytosol as citrate; acetyl CoA carboxylase (ligase, biotin, ATP) makes the 3-carbon malonyl CoA, the two-carbon donor that loses CO2 as it adds. Elongation: condensation, hydrogenation (NADPH), dehydration, hydrogenation (NADPH); steps 2–4 are the reverse of β-oxidation steps 3–1. Seven rounds, 7 malonyl CoA, 14 NADPH per palmitate. Full tables: Section 15.8.

9. Protein digestion, nitrogen balance, and essential amino acids

Proteins supply only about 10% of the body’s energy. Digestion is peptide-bond hydrolysis by hydrolases secreted as zymogens: none in the mouth; in the stomach HCl denatures proteins and activates pepsinogen to pepsin; in the small intestine trypsin (cuts after Lys, Arg), chymotrypsin (after Tyr, Trp, Phe, Leu), carboxypeptidase (C-terminus), and aminopeptidase (N-terminus); amino acids are absorbed by active transport. The amino acid pool has three sources: diet, protein turnover, and liver biosynthesis (nonessential only). Nitrogen balance: intake = excretion; negative in starvation, protein-poor diets, wasting illness, burns; positive in growth, pregnancy, convalescence. Essential amino acids (PVT TIM HaLL: Phe, Val, Thr, Trp, Ile, Met, His, Leu, Lys, plus arginine in the lecture’s count) must come from food; the nonessential ones are made from glycolysis and cycle intermediates, three of them (Ala, Asp, Glu) by a single transamination, tyrosine by oxidation of phenylalanine. Sections 15.9 and 15.11.

10. One body, one metabolism: where the pathways overlap

The pathways share a handful of hub molecules. Pyruvate connects glucose, alanine, lactate, and glycerol (via DHAP). Acetyl CoA is where glucose, every fatty acid, and the ketogenic amino acids end up, and it starts fatty acids, ketone bodies, and cholesterol. Oxaloacetate and α-ketoglutarate are cycle intermediates and the keto-acid partners of aspartate and glutamate. The one-way street pyruvate → acetyl CoA means fatty acids cannot become glucose. The hub figure and table: Section 15.4.

Health Career Connection

  • Nursing – diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA): fruity acetone breath, deep rapid Kussmaul breathing, frequent urination, dehydration; a medical emergency that can show up in a dental chair too (Section 15.7).
  • Nursing – lab values: ALT and AST are the transamination enzymes, so high blood levels suggest liver damage (Section 15.2); blood urea nitrogen (BUN) reflects the urea cycle and kidney clearance, and high ammonia in liver failure causes hepatic encephalopathy (Section 15.10).
  • Dental hygiene – PKU and sugar-free products: aspartame contains phenylalanine, hence the warning label; xylitol products are the alternative (Section 15.11).

Worked Examples

Example 1: β-Oxidation of lauric acid (12 C)

Problem. Find the number of β-oxidation cycles, acetyl CoA, NADH, FADH2, and the total ATP.

Solution.

  • Cycles = 12/2 − 1 = 5, so 5 NADH and 5 FADH2.
  • Acetyl CoA = 12/2 = 6.
  • ATP = 6(10) + 5(2.5) + 5(1.5) − 2 = 60 + 12.5 + 7.5 − 2 = 78 ATP.

Check: Each extra pair of carbons on a saturated chain is worth 14 ATP, so 78 + 14 + 14 = 106 for palmitate (Section 15.3). The numbers agree.

Example 2: Comparing fuels

Problem. A 6-carbon fatty acid (hexanoic acid) and glucose both have 6 carbons. Which yields more ATP?

Solution. Hexanoic acid: 2 cycles, 3 acetyl CoA → 3(10) + 2(2.5) + 2(1.5) − 2 = 36 ATP. Glucose = 32 ATP. The fatty acid wins because its carbons are more reduced (fewer oxygens), so there is more to oxidize. This is the same reason fat gives 9 kcal/g.

Check: C6H12O2 versus C6H12O6: four fewer oxygens means four more C–H bonds to hand to NAD+ and FAD.

Example 3: Set up the table for ketogenesis

Problem. Two steps are described in words. (a) “Two acetyl CoA molecules combine to form acetoacetyl CoA, releasing CoA–SH.” (b) “Acetoacetate is converted to β-hydroxybutyrate while NADH becomes NAD+.” For each, give the reaction type, how you can tell, the enzyme class, and the coenzyme. Then name the pathway and its initial reactant and final products.

Solution. (a) Condensation: two molecules came together to form a larger one (2 C + 2 C → 4 C) and a small molecule, CoA–SH, was lost. The enzyme is thiolase, a transferase, running in reverse of the last step of β-oxidation; no coenzyme is consumed. (b) Hydrogenation, that is, a reduction: the ketone C=O became a 2° alcohol C–OH, and NADH gave up hydrogen (it was oxidized), so the substrate was reduced. The enzyme is a dehydrogenase, an oxidoreductase, and the coenzyme is NADH → NAD+. The pathway is ketogenesis: initial reactant acetyl CoA; products acetoacetate, then β-hydroxybutyrate (by reduction) or acetone (by decarboxylation).

Check. Count carbons: 2 + 2 = 4 in (a); 4 → 4 in (b), since only the oxidation state changed. If the question had said “acetoacetate loses CO2,” the carbon count would drop to 3 and the answer would be decarboxylation to acetone.

Example 4: Lipogenesis is not β-oxidation in reverse

Problem. In fatty acid synthesis, the β-keto group of acetoacetyl ACP is reduced to a 2° alcohol by NADPH. (a) Classify the reaction and the enzyme. (b) Which β-oxidation step is this the reverse of, and what coenzyme does that step use? (c) Name three other ways lipogenesis differs from β-oxidation.

Solution. (a) A hydrogenation (reduction): C=O → C–OH, with NADPH as the reducing agent, which is oxidized to NADP+. The enzyme (3-ketoacyl ACP reductase) is an oxidoreductase. (b) It reverses step 3 of β-oxidation, in which the 2° alcohol is oxidized to a ketone by NAD+. Different coenzyme, different direction. (c) Lipogenesis runs in the cytosol (β-oxidation in the mitochondrial matrix); the chain is carried on acyl carrier protein, not coenzyme A; the two-carbon units are delivered by the three-carbon malonyl group, which loses CO2; and it is switched on by insulin rather than by glucagon and epinephrine.

Check. Line the two pathways up: lipogenesis steps 2, 3, 4 (reduce, dehydrate, reduce) undo β-oxidation steps 3, 2, 1 (oxidize, hydrate, oxidize). Only the first step of each, condensation versus thiolysis, is not a mirror image, because lipogenesis adds carbons from malonyl ACP while thiolysis removes them with CoA.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Setting the number of cycles equal to the number of acetyl CoA. Cycles = acetyl CoA − 1.
  • Forgetting to subtract the 2 ATP activation cost.
  • Thinking ketone bodies are always harmful. Mild ketosis is a normal response to fasting; ketoacidosis is the dangerous extreme.
  • Calling thiolase a hydrolase. Step 4 of β-oxidation uses coenzyme A, not water, to break the carbon–carbon bond; it is a transferase. (Pancreatic lipase and arginase, which do use water, are examples of hydrolases in this chapter.)
  • Saying that fatty acids can be made into glucose. Acetyl CoA cannot go back to pyruvate; only the glycerol part of a fat is glucogenic.
  • Mixing up the two aminotransferase reactions. ALT: alanine ↔ pyruvate. AST: aspartate ↔ oxaloacetate. In both, the partner is α-ketoglutarate ↔ glutamate.
  • Thinking both urea nitrogens come from NH4+. One comes from ammonium (via carbamoyl phosphate); the other comes from aspartate.
  • Confusing ketosis with ketoacidosis on a lab report. Ketones alone are not a diagnosis; look at blood glucose, pH, and bicarbonate.
  • Saying glycerol 3-phosphate is reduced to dihydroxyacetone phosphate. A C–OH becoming a C=O is an oxidation; NAD+ is the oxidizing agent and is itself reduced.
  • Treating lipogenesis as β-oxidation run backward. Different compartment (cytosol), different coenzyme (NADPH), different carrier (ACP), and a three-carbon malonyl donor; only the chemistry of steps 2–4 mirrors steps 3–1.
  • Counting one too many repetitions for an unsaturated fatty acid. The number of acetyl CoA and the number of repetitions do not change; only the FADH2 count drops, by one per existing double bond.

Clinical Update: Rosa’s Story, Part 15 of 15

A sick day, and one year later

In the winter, Rosa catches a stomach virus and cannot keep food down for two days. Following the written sick-day plan from her care team, she sips fluids, checks her glucose more often and tests her urine with a ketone strip, which shows moderate ketones. Her nurse practitioner adjusts her medicines until she can eat again. Her daughter notices a fruity smell on her breath.

At a follow-up visit, her lab report includes BUN (blood urea nitrogen) and creatinine to check her kidneys. One year after her deep cleaning, Rosa’s gums bleed much less and her A1c is 7.0%. Studies suggest that treating gum disease can modestly improve blood glucose control, along with the medicines, diet and exercise you have followed through this book.

Case Question 15.1

Why does a person who cannot eat for a couple of days start making ketone bodies?

Answer
With little glucose coming in, the body breaks down stored fat. β-Oxidation of fatty acids produces more acetyl CoA than the citric acid cycle can use (oxaloacetate is being diverted to gluconeogenesis), so the liver converts the excess acetyl CoA into ketone bodies.

Case Question 15.2

Which ketone body causes the fruity breath, and how is it formed?

Answer
Acetone. It forms when acetoacetate loses CO2 (decarboxylation), and because it is volatile it is exhaled.

Case Question 15.3

What does BUN measure, and which pathway produces it?

Answer
BUN measures urea nitrogen in the blood. Urea is made in the liver by the urea cycle, which converts toxic ammonium ion from amino acid breakdown into urea that the kidneys excrete. When the kidneys are not working well, BUN rises.

Practice

Exercise 1

For stearic acid (18 C), find the cycles, acetyl CoA, and total ATP.

Answer
8 cycles, 9 acetyl CoA. ATP = 90 + 8(2.5) + 8(1.5) − 2 = 90 + 20 + 12 − 2 = 120 ATP.

Exercise 2

For myristic acid (14 C), how many FADH2 are produced?

Answer
6 (one per cycle; 14/2 − 1 = 6 cycles)

Exercise 3

Name the three ketone bodies. Which one is exhaled?

Answer
Acetoacetate, β-hydroxybutyrate, and acetone. Acetone is volatile and exhaled.

Exercise 4

Why does the body convert ammonium ions to urea instead of excreting NH4+ directly?

Answer
Ammonia/ammonium is toxic, especially to the brain. Urea is non-toxic, water soluble, and safely excreted by the kidneys.

Exercise 5

How many ATP does oleic acid (18 carbons, one C=C) yield? Compare with stearic acid (120 ATP).

Answer
118.5 ATP: the same as stearic acid minus one FADH2 (1.5 ATP) for the existing double bond.

Exercise 6

A patient recovering from major burns is losing more nitrogen than they take in. What is this called, and why does it matter?

Answer
Negative nitrogen balance. Body protein is being broken down faster than it is replaced, which slows healing, so extra dietary protein is needed.

Exercise 7

Classify each enzyme by class: pancreatic lipase, acyl CoA synthetase, enoyl CoA hydratase, glutamate dehydrogenase, acetyl CoA carboxylase.

Answer
Pancreatic lipase: hydrolase (ester hydrolysis). Acyl CoA synthetase: ligase (joins fatty acid to CoA using ATP). Enoyl CoA hydratase: lyase (adds water across a C=C without ATP or oxidation). Glutamate dehydrogenase: oxidoreductase (NAD+). Acetyl CoA carboxylase: ligase (adds CO2 using ATP; biotin coenzyme).

Exercise 8

Which hub molecule do these all pass through on their way to the citric acid cycle: glucose, palmitic acid, and leucine? Which of the three could instead be used to make glucose?

Answer
All three become acetyl CoA. Only glucose itself (through pyruvate and oxaloacetate) can be made into glucose; palmitate and leucine (purely ketogenic) cannot, because acetyl CoA has no path back to pyruvate.

Exercise 9

Give the initial reactant and final product of (a) ketogenesis, (b) lipogenesis, and (c) the urea cycle, and the compartment where each runs.

Answer
(a) Acetyl CoA → acetoacetate, then β-hydroxybutyrate or acetone; liver mitochondria. (b) Acetyl CoA (via malonyl CoA) → palmitic acid (16 C); cytosol. (c) NH4+ (as carbamoyl phosphate) + aspartate → urea; liver, first step in the mitochondrial matrix and the rest in the cytosol.

Exercise 10

Indicate at which step of β-oxidation each event occurs: (a) a C–C single bond becomes a C=C; (b) NAD+ is reduced to NADH; (c) water is added; (d) an acetyl CoA is released.

Answer
(a) Step 1 (dehydrogenation, FAD). (b) Step 3 (dehydrogenation of the 2° alcohol). (c) Step 2 (hydration). (d) Step 4 (thiolysis).
