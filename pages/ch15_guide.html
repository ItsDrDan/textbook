Learning Objectives

  • Describe how dietary fats and proteins are digested and absorbed, naming the enzymes and their classes.
  • Know that glycerol is phosphorylated and oxidized to dihydroxyacetone phosphate, which can enter glycolysis or gluconeogenesis, and explain how stored fat is mobilized.
  • Know that fatty acids are metabolized by β-oxidation; describe activation, the carnitine shuttle, and the four reactions of each repetition (reaction type, how you can tell, enzyme class, coenzyme).
  • Given the length of a fatty acid chain, predict the number of acetyl CoA formed and the number of repeated β-oxidation cycles needed, and calculate the NADH, FADH2, and ATP.
  • Know the initial reactant and final product of ketogenesis (acetyl CoA → acetoacetate → β-hydroxybutyrate or acetone), lipogenesis (acetyl CoA → palmitic acid), and the urea cycle (NH4+ → urea); classify their steps.
  • Explain when and why ketone bodies form, and the difference between ketosis and ketoacidosis.
  • Contrast fatty acid synthesis (lipogenesis) with β-oxidation.
  • Describe transamination, oxidative deamination, and the urea cycle, including enzyme classes and coenzymes.
  • Classify amino acid carbon skeletons as glucogenic or ketogenic, list the essential amino acids, and explain nitrogen balance.
  • Draw the "hub" molecules where carbohydrate, lipid, and protein metabolism meet.

Key Ideas

1. Digesting fats

About 98% of dietary lipids are triacylglycerols (TAG), the triesters of glycerol and three fatty acids you met in Chapter 9. Excess energy from carbohydrates and proteins is also stored as TAG in adipocytes (fat cells), to be used later. TAGs are insoluble in water, so the water-soluble enzymes of saliva have little effect on them, and only about 10% are hydrolyzed by gastric lipase in the stomach. Digesting the rest is a two-part problem: the fat globules must be broken into small particles so that a water-soluble enzyme can reach them, and then the ester bonds must be hydrolyzed.

  • Emulsification. Bile salts, made in the liver from cholesterol and secreted from the gallbladder into the small intestine, act like soaps: a steroid ring that dissolves in fat plus an ionic group that dissolves in water. They break the fat globules into smaller particles called micelles, making the fat “soluble” in water. No bonds are broken yet; this is a physical change.
  • Incomplete hydrolysis. Pancreatic lipase (a hydrolase) hydrolyzes the ester linkages between glycerol and the fatty acid units. In the intestine this hydrolysis is incomplete: it removes the two outer fatty acids and leaves a monoacylglycerol. Set up the table: reaction type, hydrolysis (water splits an ester); how can you tell, an ester became a carboxylic acid plus an alcohol; enzyme class, hydrolase; coenzyme, none. Compare saponification in Chapter 9: same ester hydrolysis, but at body pH with an enzyme instead of hot NaOH.
  • Repackaging and absorption. Micelles of free fatty acids and monoacylglycerols pass through the membranes of intestinal cells. Inside, the fatty acids recombine with the monoacylglycerols to re-form TAG, which is coated with phospholipids and proteins to make chylomicrons, water-soluble particles that carry TAG through the lymphatic system into the bloodstream.
  • Delivery and storage. At capillaries in muscle and adipose tissue, lipoprotein lipase, another hydrolase, hydrolyzes the chylomicron TAG again so that fatty acids can enter the cells to be burned or stored. Stored TAG sits in adipocytes, whose cytoplasm is mostly one large fat droplet, beneath the skin and around the organs.
  • Complete hydrolysis. When carbohydrate cannot meet energy needs, the hormones glucagon and epinephrine activate a lipase in adipose tissue that completely hydrolyzes stored TAG to glycerol + 3 fatty acids. The fatty acids bind the plasma protein albumin for transport to the tissues; glycerol dissolves in blood and goes to the liver (Key Idea 5).

2. β-Oxidation (in the mitochondria)

Fatty acids are broken down for energy in three stages: (1) the fatty acid is activated by binding to coenzyme A; (2) it is transported into the mitochondrial matrix; (3) it is repeatedly oxidized, in what lecture calls the fatty acid spiral, to produce acetyl CoA, FADH2, and NADH. Stages 1 and 2 are in Key Idea 6; here is stage 3. β-Oxidation is a sequence of four reactions that repeatedly cleaves two-carbon units from the carboxyl end of the acyl CoA. The name comes from the β carbon, the second carbon from the carboxyl end (the α carbon is the first): it is the carbon that gets oxidized, from CH2 to C=O, before the bond between the α and β carbons is broken.

  • Activation: the fatty acid is attached to CoA. This costs the equivalent of 2 ATP (one time per fatty acid).
  • Each repetition removes 2 carbons as acetyl CoA and produces 1 FADH2 + 1 NADH, leaving an acyl CoA two carbons shorter.
  • For a fatty acid with n carbons (even): repetitions = n/2 − 1, acetyl CoA = n/2. The last repetition splits a 4-carbon piece into two acetyl CoA (the “extra” acetyl CoA), which is why there is one fewer repetition than acetyl CoA. A C18 fatty acid needs 8 repetitions to give 9 acetyl CoA; a C14 fatty acid needs 6 to give 7.
  • ATP total = (acetyl CoA × 10) + (NADH × 2.5) + (FADH2 × 1.5) − 2

“Pathway” is a better word than “cycle” here: it is a linear set of four reactions that repeats over and over, and each repetition gives a different (shorter) product rather than regenerating the starting material. The four reactions follow a pattern you have seen in the citric acid cycle (succinate → fumarate → malate → oxaloacetate): dehydrogenate to a C=C, hydrate it, dehydrogenate the new alcohol to a ketone, then a fourth step unique to β-oxidation, thiolysis. Why does the arithmetic work? Each acetyl CoA that enters the citric acid cycle yields about 10 ATP (3 NADH, 1 FADH2, 1 GTP, Chapter 14). Each repetition's FADH2 and NADH go straight to electron transport for 1.5 and 2.5 ATP. The only cost is the activation step, paid once. For stearic acid (18:0): 9 acetyl CoA (90) + 8 NADH (20) + 8 FADH2 (12) = 122, minus 2 for activation = 120 ATP.

Fats versus carbs. Compare on equal carbons: three glucose molecules (3 × 6 C = 18 C) give 3 × 32 = 96 ATP, while one stearic acid (18 C) gives 120 ATP. The fatty acid wins per carbon because its carbons are more reduced (almost all CH2, few oxygens), so there is more to oxidize. On an equal-mass basis fatty acids give about 2.4 times as much energy per gram, which is why 1 g of fat is 9 kcal while 1 g of carbohydrate is 4 kcal (Chapter 14).

Do the counting before you touch any ATP numbers, and remember that the final cut gives two pieces at once, which is why pieces = cuts + 1.

3. Ketone bodies

When carbohydrate is scarce (fasting, very low-carb diets) or cannot be used (uncontrolled diabetes), the liver makes more acetyl CoA than the citric acid cycle can handle. It converts the extra into ketone bodies: acetoacetate, β-hydroxybutyrate, and acetone. The heart, muscles, and (after a few days) the brain can burn them, but acetoacetate and β-hydroxybutyrate are acids. In large amounts they lower blood pH: ketoacidosis.

Here is the chemistry behind that paragraph. β-Oxidation of fatty acids releases a large number of acetyl CoA molecules, and if the citric acid cycle cannot process them all (during fasting or insulin deficiency the liver is pulling oxaloacetate out of the cycle to make glucose), the excess acetyl CoA is diverted in the liver into the three ketone bodies through ketogenesis (initial reactant acetyl CoA; products acetoacetate, then β-hydroxybutyrate or acetone). Technically the middle ketone body, β-hydroxybutyrate, is not a ketone (it has a 2° alcohol instead), but it is made from one and can be oxidized back to one, so it keeps the name. Acetone is more of a by-product than a product of the pathway; it is volatile and leaves in the breath. Ketone bodies are produced in the liver and transported to the heart, brain, and muscles.

  • Who uses them? The liver makes ketone bodies but lacks the enzyme to activate them, so it exports them. Heart and skeletal muscle use them readily; after two to three days of fasting the brain, which cannot burn fatty acids, gets most of its energy from ketone bodies, sparing muscle protein that would otherwise be broken down for gluconeogenesis.
  • Ketosis vs. ketoacidosis. Ketosis is the buildup of ketone bodies in blood and urine from starvation, vigorous dieting, alcoholism, or uncontrolled diabetes; the mild form brings headache, dry mouth, and sometimes acetone-smelling breath, and the blood buffers handle it. Ketoacidosis is the runaway version, in which the acidic ketone bodies actually lower blood pH: heavy breathing and increased urine output that can lead to dehydration, and ultimately coma and death. In diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) insulin does not function, glucose cannot be used for energy, fats are broken down to acetyl CoA, and ketogenesis runs unchecked; the ketone bodies are soluble in blood and urine, and acetone is detectable in the breath.

4. Amino acid metabolism

  • Transamination: the amino group moves from an amino acid to α-ketoglutarate, making glutamate and a new α-keto acid (alanine → pyruvate). The enzymes involved are ALT and AST.
  • Oxidative deamination: glutamate releases its nitrogen as NH4+ (toxic) and regenerates α-ketoglutarate.
  • Urea cycle (liver): converts NH4+ and CO2 into urea, which the kidneys excrete.
  • Leftover carbon skeletons are glucogenic (can become glucose via pyruvate or cycle intermediates) or ketogenic (become acetyl CoA or ketone bodies).

Unlike glucose and fat, amino acids are not stored. Whatever the body does not need for protein synthesis is broken down in two parts: the nitrogen (the amino group) is removed and disposed of as urea, and the carbon skeleton is burned or converted to glucose or fat. Let's follow each.

Step 1: Transamination. An aminotransferase (also called a transaminase; enzyme class transferase) swaps the amino group of an amino acid with the keto oxygen of an α-keto acid. The usual acceptor is α-ketoglutarate, the citric acid cycle intermediate, which becomes glutamate. The coenzyme is pyridoxal phosphate (PLP), the active form of vitamin B6, which holds the amino group between the two halves of the reaction. The reaction is reversible, which is why the same enzymes also make nonessential amino acids (Key Idea 9).

The two aminotransferases you will see on lab reports are ALT (alanine aminotransferase: alanine + α-ketoglutarate → pyruvate + glutamate) and AST (aspartate aminotransferase: aspartate + α-ketoglutarate → oxaloacetate + glutamate). Transamination funnels the nitrogen of almost every amino acid into one molecule, glutamate.

Step 2: Oxidative deamination. In the mitochondria, glutamate dehydrogenase (an oxidoreductase, using NAD+ or NADP+) oxidizes glutamate and releases its nitrogen as free ammonium ion, NH4+, regenerating α-ketoglutarate for another round of transamination:

glutamate + NAD+ + H2O → α-ketoglutarate + NH4+ + NADH + H+

Ammonia is toxic, especially to the brain, so it cannot be allowed to build up in the blood. The liver converts it to urea.

Step 3: The urea cycle (initial reactant NH4+, final product urea). The cycle runs only in the liver; it has four steps, the first in the mitochondrial matrix and the other three in the cytosol. It takes in two nitrogen atoms, one from NH4+ and one from aspartate, plus one carbon from CO2 (as bicarbonate), and releases urea, (NH2)2C=O, a small, neutral, very water-soluble molecule that the kidneys excrete in urine. Three amino acids serve as intermediates: ornithine and citrulline, which are nonstandard amino acids not found in proteins, and arginine, a standard one; all three are built on the same five-carbon chain. Ornithine is regenerated each turn, like oxaloacetate in the citric acid cycle. The ammonium ion does not enter the cycle directly: it is first converted to carbamoyl phosphate by carbamoyl phosphate synthetase (a ligase, using 2 ATP). The cost of one urea is the equivalent of 3 ATP molecules (two at the carbamoyl phosphate step and one, split all the way to AMP, in step 2; counting that split as two high-energy bonds gives 4 ATP-equivalents, the number some texts quote). Step 4: The carbon skeletons. With the nitrogen gone, each α-keto acid is a familiar molecule or becomes one. The body cannot oxidize nitrogen-containing compounds for energy, which is why the nitrogen had to come off first. Each amino acid has its own degradation pathway, in one or several steps, but 20 amino acids give only 7 degradation products: pyruvate, acetyl CoA, acetoacetyl CoA, and the citric acid cycle intermediates α-ketoglutarate, succinyl CoA, fumarate, and oxaloacetate. Skeletons that end up as pyruvate or a citric acid cycle intermediate can be made into glucose by gluconeogenesis: these amino acids are glucogenic. Skeletons that end up as acetyl CoA or acetoacetyl CoA cannot become glucose (there is no path from acetyl CoA back to pyruvate) but can contribute to fatty acids or ketone bodies: these amino acids are ketogenic. A few appear in both groups, either because they degrade by two pathways or because parts of the skeleton go different ways.

5. Fat mobilization and glycerol

When energy is needed, glucagon and epinephrine activate hormone-sensitive lipase in fat tissue, which releases fatty acids and glycerol into the blood. Glycerol goes to the liver, where it becomes dihydroxyacetone phosphate, an intermediate that can enter glycolysis or gluconeogenesis.

The hormones work the same way glycogen breakdown is switched on in Chapter 13: they activate the lipase by covalent modification (Chapter 11), and insulin does the opposite. The fatty acids are not water-soluble, so they travel in the blood bound to the plasma protein albumin; the glycerol, a small triol, simply dissolves. Section 15.6 covers storage and mobilization in detail.

Glycerol is worth a closer look because it is the only part of a fat that can become glucose. It travels to the liver (fat cells lack the first enzyme), where it is converted to dihydroxyacetone phosphate in two steps:

  1. Phosphorylation of the 1° hydroxyl group. Glycerol kinase (a kinase, so a transferase; ATP → ADP) phosphorylates an end carbon to make glycerol 3-phosphate. How can you tell? A phosphate was added and ATP became ADP.
  2. Oxidation of the 2° alcohol to a ketone. Glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (an oxidoreductase; NAD+ → NADH) oxidizes the middle carbon, giving dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP). How can you tell? A C–OH became a C=O, and NAD+, the oxidizing agent, was reduced. (Glycerol 3-phosphate is oxidized, not reduced, a favorite true-or-false item.)

DHAP is a glycolysis intermediate, so it can go "downhill" to pyruvate when energy is needed or "uphill" to glucose during fasting. Two glycerols make one glucose. The amount is small (glycerol is about one-tenth of a fat by mass), but during a long fast it is a steady contribution to blood glucose.

6. Getting fatty acids into the mitochondria

After activation (fatty acyl CoA), the fatty acid cannot cross the inner mitochondrial membrane directly. The carnitine shuttle carries it across, and it is reattached to CoA inside.

Activation. A carboxylic acid is unreactive, so the first job is to convert it into a thioester. Acyl CoA synthetase (a ligase) on the outer mitochondrial membrane joins the fatty acid to coenzyme A:

R–COO− + CoA–SH + ATP → R–CO–S–CoA + AMP + PPi

ATP is split to AMP, not ADP, and the pyrophosphate (PPi) is then hydrolyzed to two phosphates. Two high-energy bonds are spent, so we count the cost as 2 ATP-equivalents per fatty acid, no matter how long the chain.

The carnitine shuttle. Acyl CoA is too large to pass through the inner mitochondrial membrane to the matrix, so it needs a shuttle. Carnitine acyltransferase catalyzes both acyl transfers: on the outside it moves the acyl group from CoA onto carnitine, a small carrier molecule; acyl-carnitine crosses the membrane; and on the inside the same kind of enzyme gives the acyl group back to a mitochondrial CoA. Both transfers are transferase reactions. The shuttle is also a control point: malonyl CoA, the three-carbon unit used to build fatty acids (Key Idea 8), blocks the transport of acyl groups into the matrix, so a cell that is making fat does not burn it at the same time. (Medium-chain fatty acids can enter without the shuttle; see the Nursing box.)

7. Unsaturated fatty acids

A double bond that is already present means the FAD step is skipped for that repetition: the pathway starts at step 2, so each C=C gives one fewer FADH2 (1.5 fewer ATP). Less energy is released from an unsaturated fatty acid than from the saturated one of the same length.

There is a wrinkle: the double bonds in natural fatty acids are cis, and the hydration step adds water only to a trans double bond between the α and β carbons. A cis–trans isomerase (an isomerase) converts the cis bond, which is usually one carbon too far down the chain, into the trans-(2,3) bond that step 2 needs. At the level of this course, count one fewer FADH2 per pre-existing double bond; oleic acid (18 C, one C=C) gives 120 − 1.5 = 118.5 ATP.

8. Lipogenesis vs. β-oxidation

When dietary intake provides more nutrients than energy requirements need, the liver turns the extra acetyl CoA into fatty acids (lipogenesis, or fatty acid synthesis; initial reactant acetyl CoA, final product the 16-carbon palmitic acid) and ships them to adipose tissue as VLDL. Lipogenesis is not the reverse of β-oxidation. It occurs in the cytosol, uses the coenzyme NADPH, carries the growing chain on a different carrier, and is controlled by the opposite hormone, which lets the cell run one direction without the other. Because acetyl CoA is made in the mitochondria, it must first be carried to the cytosol as citrate (the citrate–malate shuttle).

Getting started. The chain is built on acyl carrier protein (ACP), whose business end is the same pantothenic acid–thiol unit found in coenzyme A. Two starting complexes are needed. Acetyl ACP is made by transferring the acetyl group from acetyl CoA to ACP (a transferase). Malonyl ACP takes two steps: first acetyl CoA is carboxylated to the three-carbon malonyl CoA by acetyl CoA carboxylase (a ligase, using ATP and a biotin cofactor; how can you tell? 2 C → 3 C and ATP → ADP), then the malonyl group is transferred to ACP. Malonyl CoA is the two-carbon donor for every round of chain elongation: it brings three carbons and loses one as CO2.

Chain elongation is four reactions, repeated. Steps 2, 3, and 4 are the reverse of steps 3, 2, and 1 of β-oxidation. Set up the table:

9. Protein digestion, nitrogen balance, and essential amino acids

  • Pepsin (stomach) and trypsin and chymotrypsin (small intestine) hydrolyze proteins into amino acids, which join the body's amino acid pool along with amino acids from normal protein turnover.
  • Positive nitrogen balance (more N taken in than lost): growth, pregnancy, wound healing. Negative: starvation, severe burns, serious illness.
  • Nine amino acids are essential (must come from food): His, Ile, Leu, Lys, Met, Phe, Thr, Trp, Val. The rest can be made, largely by transamination.
  • Everything connects: acetyl CoA, pyruvate, and citric acid cycle intermediates link carbohydrate, fat, and protein metabolism.

Digestion, step by step. Protein digestion is peptide-bond hydrolysis (Chapter 10), done by proteases (all hydrolases) in stages:

  • Mouth. Saliva contains no enzyme that hydrolyzes proteins, so protein digestion starts in the stomach.
  • Stomach. Hydrochloric acid (pH about 2) denatures the protein, unfolding it so the enzymes can reach the backbone, and activates the inactive pepsinogen into pepsin, which hydrolyzes proteins into polypeptides.
  • Small intestine. The pancreas secretes trypsin, chymotrypsin, and carboxypeptidase as inactive zymogens (Chapter 11) so that they do not digest the pancreas itself. Each has its own specificity: trypsin cleaves at the carboxyl side of lysine and arginine (the amino acids with a 1° amine in the side chain); chymotrypsin cleaves at the carboxyl side of the large hydrophobic residues tyrosine, tryptophan, phenylalanine, and leucine; carboxypeptidase removes amino acids one at a time from the C-terminus; and aminopeptidase removes them from the N-terminus.
  • Absorption. The free amino acids move into the bloodstream by active transport through the intestinal wall (Chapter 9) and travel in the portal vein to the liver.

Proteins supply only about 10% of the body’s energy; their main value is as a source of amino acids for protein synthesis and for the other nitrogen-containing molecules the cell needs.

The amino acid pool and turnover. The amino acid pool is the total supply of free amino acids available for use in the body. It has three sources: dietary proteins; protein turnover, the repetitive process in which body proteins are degraded and resynthesized; and biosynthesis of amino acids in the liver (nonessential amino acids only). It is drained by protein synthesis, by the making of nitrogen-containing molecules such as heme, nucleotides, and neurotransmitters, and by degradation for energy. Amino acids cannot be stored in the body or directly excreted, so those in excess are degraded to substances that can be processed through the common metabolic pathways.

Nitrogen balance. Nitrogen balance occurs when the amount of nitrogen taken in as protein equals the amount excreted in waste. Two kinds of imbalance occur. Negative nitrogen balance: protein degradation exceeds protein synthesis and tissue is wasted; causes are protein-poor diets, starvation, and wasting illnesses (and, in the hospital, severe burns, sepsis, major surgery, immobilization, and corticosteroid treatment). Positive nitrogen balance: protein synthesis exceeds degradation and large amounts of tissue are built; causes are growth, pregnancy, and convalescence from an emaciating illness.

Essential vs. nonessential. The body can make the carbon skeletons of the nonessential amino acids from glycolysis and citric acid cycle intermediates and then add nitrogen by transamination; these need fewer reaction steps than the essential ones. The essential amino acids cannot be made, because the body lacks the correct carbon skeleton, so they must come from the diet. Nine are essential for all adults; the mnemonic PVT TIM HaLL lists them: Phenylalanine, Valine, Threonine, Tryptophan, Isoleucine, Methionine, Histidine, Leucine, Lysine. The lower-case “a” is arginine: the urea cycle makes it, but not fast enough in infants or during severe illness, so many GOB textbooks (and the lecture slides) count it as a tenth essential amino acid, leaving ten nonessential. Tyrosine and cysteine are conditionally essential for the same reason: tyrosine is made from phenylalanine and cysteine’s sulfur comes from methionine. Three nonessential amino acids, alanine, aspartate, and glutamate, are made in a single step by transamination of the matching α-keto acid (pyruvate, oxaloacetate, α-ketoglutarate); the others take several reactions in addition to transamination.

10. One body, one metabolism: where the pathways overlap

You have now met three chapters of pathways. Step back and notice that they share a handful of molecules. Pyruvate connects glucose, alanine (and other glucogenic amino acids), and glycerol (through DHAP). Acetyl CoA is where glucose, every fatty acid, and the ketogenic amino acids all end up, and it is the starting material for fatty acids, ketone bodies, and cholesterol. Oxaloacetate and α-ketoglutarate are both citric acid cycle intermediates and the keto-acid partners of aspartate and glutamate, so the cycle doubles as the on-ramp for amino acid carbon and the source of carbon for nonessential amino acids. One arrow is missing on purpose: there is no pathway from acetyl CoA back to pyruvate, so fatty acids cannot be turned into glucose. That single fact explains why the brain needs ketone bodies in a fast and why gluconeogenesis must run on glycerol, lactate, and amino acids.

Health Career Connection

  • Nursing – diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA): Without insulin, cells cannot use glucose and fat breakdown runs unchecked. Signs include fruity (acetone) breath, deep rapid Kussmaul breathing (the body blowing off CO2 to raise pH), frequent urination, and dehydration. DKA is a medical emergency, and patients in a dental chair can show these signs too.
  • Nursing – lab values: ALT and AST are the transamination enzymes, so high blood levels suggest liver damage. Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) reflects the urea cycle and kidney clearance. High ammonia in liver failure can cause confusion (hepatic encephalopathy).
  • Dental hygiene – PKU and sugar-free products: People with phenylketonuria (PKU) cannot convert phenylalanine to tyrosine. The sweetener aspartame contains phenylalanine, which is why sugar-free gums and some mouth products carry a "Phenylketonurics: contains phenylalanine" warning. Xylitol-sweetened products are an alternative.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Ketones. Urine ketone strips detect acetoacetate, but in DKA most of the acid is β-hydroxybutyrate, so a blood β-hydroxybutyrate level is the better measure; as a patient recovers and β-hydroxybutyrate is oxidized back to acetoacetate, the urine strip can briefly look worse while the patient is getting better. Starvation ketosis gives modest ketones with normal glucose; DKA gives high ketones and hyperglycemia (high blood glucose) with a low bicarbonate. Carnitine. Patients with carnitine deficiency cannot move long-chain fatty acids into the mitochondria and become hypoglycemic and weak when fasting; medium-chain triglyceride (MCT) oil helps because 8- and 10-carbon fatty acids enter without the shuttle. Liver and kidney markers. ALT and AST are intracellular enzymes; they appear in blood only when liver cells (or, for AST, heart and muscle cells) are damaged and leak. Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) rises when the kidneys cannot clear urea. In liver failure the urea cycle fails and NH4+ rises (hyperammonemia), causing hepatic encephalopathy; inherited urea cycle disorders produce the same chemistry in infants, which is why both are managed partly by limiting dietary protein. Protein needs. Burns, sepsis, and ICU stays put patients in negative nitrogen balance; dietitians raise protein intake and, when the gut cannot be used, supply amino acids by total parenteral nutrition (TPN), an IV infusion of amino acids, glucose, and lipid.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Breath tells a story. A sweet, fruity, nail-polish-remover odor is acetone: ketone bodies are being made in quantity. In a patient who is also thirsty, urinating often, breathing deeply, or confused, it may signal DKA and needs urgent referral; in a fasting or low-carb patient with no other signs, it is simple ketosis. A urine-like or ammonia odor (uremic fetor) suggests kidney failure: urea accumulates in saliva, and bacterial urease converts it to ammonia. Diabetes and the periodontium. Poorly controlled diabetes (high HbA1c) worsens periodontitis and slows healing after scaling and root planing, and periodontal inflammation in turn raises blood glucose, so your care is part of diabetes care. Liver disease. The liver makes the clotting proteins and metabolizes amide local anesthetics, so patients with hepatic disease may bleed more after instrumentation and clear lidocaine or articaine more slowly; the dentist may limit the dose. Nutrition and the oral tissues. Protein malnutrition (negative nitrogen balance) means poor collagen synthesis and poor healing of the gingiva after periodontal therapy. Deficiencies of the transamination coenzyme vitamin B6, or of B12 or folate, show up early in fast-dividing oral tissues as glossitis (smooth, sore, red tongue) and angular cheilitis (cracks at the corners of the mouth), so the mouth can be the first place a nutritional problem is seen.

Worked Examples

Example 1: β-Oxidation of lauric acid (12 C)

Find the number of β-oxidation cycles, acetyl CoA, NADH, FADH2, and the total ATP.

Solution

  • Cycles = 12/2 − 1 = 5, so 5 NADH and 5 FADH2.
  • Acetyl CoA = 12/2 = 6.
  • ATP = 6(10) + 5(2.5) + 5(1.5) − 2 = 60 + 12.5 + 7.5 − 2 = 78 ATP.

Check: Each extra pair of carbons on a saturated chain is worth 14 ATP, so 78 + 14 + 14 = 106 for palmitate (Example 4). The numbers agree.

Example 2: Transamination

Write the products when alanine transfers its amino group to α-ketoglutarate.

Solution

Alanine + α-ketoglutarate → pyruvate + glutamate (catalyzed by ALT). Pyruvate can become glucose, so alanine is glucogenic.

Check: The amino acid lost its NH2 and gained a C=O; the keto acid did the reverse. Nothing else changed, and no nitrogen was released.

Example 3: Comparing fuels

A 6-carbon fatty acid (hexanoic acid) and glucose both have 6 carbons. Which yields more ATP?

Solution

Hexanoic acid: 2 cycles, 3 acetyl CoA → 3(10) + 2(2.5) + 2(1.5) − 2 = 36 ATP. Glucose = 32 ATP. The fatty acid wins because its carbons are more reduced (fewer oxygens), so there is more to oxidize. This is the same reason fat gives 9 kcal/g.

Check: C6H12O2 versus C6H12O6: four fewer oxygens means four more C–H bonds to hand to NAD+ and FAD.

Example 4: The full ATP tally for palmitic acid (16 C)

Problem. Palmitic acid is the most common saturated fatty acid in body fat. Build a complete table of its β-oxidation products and the ATP from each, then find the net ATP. Then, without redoing the table, state the net ATP for stearic acid (18 C).

Solution. Count first: 16 carbons → 16/2 = 8 acetyl CoA and 8 − 1 = 7 cycles, so 7 FADH2 and 7 NADH.

Example 5: Classify the reactions of the urea cycle

Problem. For the entry step and each of the four urea-cycle steps, name the reaction type and the enzyme class, and say which of the two nitrogen atoms of urea enters at that step, if any.

Solution. Entry: carbamoyl phosphate synthetase joins NH4+ and bicarbonate at the cost of 2 ATP: a ligase; the first nitrogen enters here. (1) Ornithine transcarbamoylase transfers the carbamoyl group onto ornithine to make citrulline: a transferase; no new nitrogen. (2) Argininosuccinate synthetase condenses citrulline with aspartate using ATP: a ligase; the second nitrogen (aspartate's amino group) enters here. (3) Argininosuccinate lyase cleaves off fumarate without water or oxidation: a lyase. (4) Arginase hydrolyzes arginine into urea and ornithine: a hydrolase.

Check. Three ATP molecules are spent (2 at the entry step, 1 at step 2, the latter split to AMP), the “3 ATP” cost quoted in lecture. Two nitrogens in, one urea out, and ornithine returned, so the cycle balances.

Example 6: Predict the nitrogen balance

Problem. A 24-year-old with a 30% body-surface burn is in the ICU, on a high-protein feeding plan. A 7-year-old is healthy and growing. A 60-year-old is on bed rest after a hip fracture and is taking a corticosteroid. For each, predict the nitrogen balance and name the metabolic process that explains it.

Solution. Burn patient: negative. Stress hormones (cortisol, glucagon, epinephrine) drive muscle protein breakdown to supply amino acids for gluconeogenesis and tissue repair; transamination and the urea cycle run hard, so urinary urea exceeds dietary nitrogen even with extra protein. Growing child: positive; new tissue is being built, so more nitrogen is retained than excreted. Bed-rest patient on a corticosteroid: negative. Immobilized muscle is not maintained, and corticosteroids stimulate protein breakdown and gluconeogenesis.

Check. Ask "is net body protein going up or down?" Up means positive; down means negative. The direction of the urea cycle output follows the opposite way: more urea, more negative.

Example 7: Set up the table for ketogenesis

Problem. Two steps are described in words. (a) “Two acetyl CoA molecules combine to form acetoacetyl CoA, releasing CoA–SH.” (b) “Acetoacetate is converted to β-hydroxybutyrate while NADH becomes NAD+.” For each, give the reaction type, how you can tell, the enzyme class, and the coenzyme. Then name the pathway and its initial reactant and final products.

Solution. (a) Condensation: two molecules came together to form a larger one (2 C + 2 C → 4 C) and a small molecule, CoA–SH, was lost. The enzyme is thiolase, a transferase, running in reverse of the last step of β-oxidation; no coenzyme is consumed. (b) Hydrogenation, that is, a reduction: the ketone C=O became a 2° alcohol C–OH, and NADH gave up hydrogen (it was oxidized), so the substrate was reduced. The enzyme is a dehydrogenase, an oxidoreductase, and the coenzyme is NADH → NAD+. The pathway is ketogenesis: initial reactant acetyl CoA; products acetoacetate, then β-hydroxybutyrate (by reduction) or acetone (by decarboxylation).

Check. Count carbons: 2 + 2 = 4 in (a); 4 → 4 in (b), since only the oxidation state changed. If the question had said “acetoacetate loses CO2,” the carbon count would drop to 3 and the answer would be decarboxylation to acetone.

Example 8: Lipogenesis is not β-oxidation in reverse

Problem. In fatty acid synthesis, the β-keto group of acetoacetyl ACP is reduced to a 2° alcohol by NADPH. (a) Classify the reaction and the enzyme. (b) Which β-oxidation step is this the reverse of, and what coenzyme does that step use? (c) Name three other ways lipogenesis differs from β-oxidation.

Solution. (a) A hydrogenation (reduction): C=O → C–OH, with NADPH as the reducing agent, which is oxidized to NADP+. The enzyme (3-ketoacyl ACP reductase) is an oxidoreductase. (b) It reverses step 3 of β-oxidation, in which the 2° alcohol is oxidized to a ketone by NAD+. Different coenzyme, different direction. (c) Lipogenesis runs in the cytosol (β-oxidation in the mitochondrial matrix); the chain is carried on acyl carrier protein, not coenzyme A; the two-carbon units are delivered by the three-carbon malonyl group, which loses CO2; and it is switched on by insulin rather than by glucagon and epinephrine.

Check. Line the two pathways up: lipogenesis steps 2, 3, 4 (reduce, dehydrate, reduce) undo β-oxidation steps 3, 2, 1 (oxidize, hydrate, oxidize). Only the first step of each, condensation versus thiolysis, is not a mirror image, because lipogenesis adds carbons from malonyl ACP while thiolysis removes them with CoA.

Example 9: Carbs versus fat, carbon for carbon

Problem. How many glucose molecules contain the same number of carbons as one stearic acid (18:0)? Compare the ATP from those glucose molecules with the ATP from the stearic acid, and explain the result in terms of oxidation state.

Solution. Glucose has 6 carbons, so 3 glucose = 18 carbons. ATP from 3 glucose = 3 × 32 = 96 ATP. ATP from stearic acid = 9 acetyl CoA (90) + 8 NADH (20) + 8 FADH2 (12) − 2 (activation) = 120 ATP. The fatty acid gives more ATP per carbon because its carbons are mostly –CH2– groups with no oxygen, so each one has more hydrogens (electrons) to give to NAD+ and FAD; glucose carbons each already carry an oxygen and are partly oxidized before metabolism begins.

Check. Per carbon: 120/18 ≈ 6.7 ATP for stearic acid versus 96/18 ≈ 5.3 for glucose. On a mass basis the gap widens to about 2.4 times, which is the 9 kcal/g versus 4 kcal/g you see on nutrition labels.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Setting the number of cycles equal to the number of acetyl CoA. Cycles = acetyl CoA − 1.
  • Forgetting to subtract the 2 ATP activation cost.
  • Thinking ketone bodies are always harmful. Mild ketosis is a normal response to fasting; ketoacidosis is the dangerous extreme.
  • Calling thiolase a hydrolase. Step 4 of β-oxidation uses coenzyme A, not water, to break the carbon–carbon bond; it is a transferase. (Pancreatic lipase and arginase, which do use water, are the hydrolases in this chapter.)
  • Saying that fatty acids can be made into glucose. Acetyl CoA cannot go back to pyruvate; only the glycerol part of a fat is glucogenic.
  • Mixing up the two aminotransferase reactions. ALT: alanine ↔ pyruvate. AST: aspartate ↔ oxaloacetate. In both, the partner is α-ketoglutarate ↔ glutamate.
  • Thinking both urea nitrogens come from NH4+. One comes from ammonium (via carbamoyl phosphate); the other comes from aspartate.
  • Confusing ketosis with ketoacidosis on a lab report. Ketones alone are not a diagnosis; look at blood glucose, pH, and bicarbonate.
  • Saying glycerol 3-phosphate is reduced to dihydroxyacetone phosphate. A C–OH becoming a C=O is an oxidation; NAD+ is the oxidizing agent and is itself reduced.
  • Treating lipogenesis as β-oxidation run backward. Different compartment (cytosol), different coenzyme (NADPH), different carrier (ACP), and a three-carbon malonyl donor; only the chemistry of steps 2–4 mirrors steps 3–1.
  • Counting one too many repetitions for an unsaturated fatty acid. The number of acetyl CoA and the number of repetitions do not change; only the FADH2 count drops, by one per existing double bond.

Practice

Exercise 1

For stearic acid (18 C), find the cycles, acetyl CoA, and total ATP.

Answer
8 cycles, 9 acetyl CoA. ATP = 90 + 8(2.5) + 8(1.5) − 2 = 90 + 20 + 12 − 2 = 120 ATP.

Exercise 2

For myristic acid (14 C), how many FADH2 are produced?

Answer
6 (one per cycle; 14/2 − 1 = 6 cycles)

Exercise 3

Name the three ketone bodies. Which one is exhaled?

Answer
Acetoacetate, β-hydroxybutyrate, and acetone. Acetone is volatile and exhaled.

Exercise 4

Why does the body convert ammonium ions to urea instead of excreting NH4+ directly?

Answer
Ammonia/ammonium is toxic, especially to the brain. Urea is non-toxic, water soluble, and safely excreted by the kidneys.

Exercise 5

How many ATP does oleic acid (18 carbons, one C=C) yield? Compare with stearic acid (120 ATP).

Answer
118.5 ATP: the same as stearic acid minus one FADH2 (1.5 ATP) for the existing double bond.

Exercise 6

A patient recovering from major burns is losing more nitrogen than they take in. What is this called, and why does it matter?

Answer
Negative nitrogen balance. Body protein is being broken down faster than it is replaced, which slows healing, so extra dietary protein is needed.

Exercise 7

Classify each enzyme by class: pancreatic lipase, acyl CoA synthetase, enoyl CoA hydratase, glutamate dehydrogenase, acetyl CoA carboxylase.

Answer
Pancreatic lipase: hydrolase (ester hydrolysis). Acyl CoA synthetase: ligase (joins fatty acid to CoA using ATP). Enoyl CoA hydratase: lyase (adds water across a C=C without ATP or oxidation). Glutamate dehydrogenase: oxidoreductase (NAD+). Acetyl CoA carboxylase: ligase (adds CO2 using ATP; biotin coenzyme).

Exercise 8

Which hub molecule do these all pass through on their way to the citric acid cycle: glucose, palmitic acid, and leucine? Which of the three could instead be used to make glucose?

Answer
All three become acetyl CoA. Only glucose itself (through pyruvate and oxaloacetate) can be made into glucose; palmitate and leucine (purely ketogenic) cannot, because acetyl CoA has no path back to pyruvate.

Exercise 9

Give the initial reactant and final product of (a) ketogenesis, (b) lipogenesis, and (c) the urea cycle, and the compartment where each runs.

Answer
(a) Acetyl CoA → acetoacetate, then β-hydroxybutyrate or acetone; liver mitochondria. (b) Acetyl CoA (via malonyl CoA) → palmitic acid (16 C); cytosol. (c) NH4+ (as carbamoyl phosphate) + aspartate → urea; liver, first step in the mitochondrial matrix and the rest in the cytosol.

Exercise 10

Indicate at which step of β-oxidation each event occurs: (a) a C–C single bond becomes a C=C; (b) NAD+ is reduced to NADH; (c) water is added; (d) an acetyl CoA is released.

Answer
(a) Step 1 (dehydrogenation, FAD). (b) Step 3 (dehydrogenation of the 2° alcohol). (c) Step 2 (hydration). (d) Step 4 (thiolysis).
