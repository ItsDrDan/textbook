Learning Objectives

  • Explain why the body must make glucose during fasting, name the tissues that do it, and list the precursors that can and cannot become glucose.
  • Identify the three irreversible steps of glycolysis and the four enzymes that bypass them in gluconeogenesis, with their enzyme classes, coenzymes, and energy input.
  • Account for the cost of gluconeogenesis (6 ATP-equivalents and 2 NADH per glucose) and explain why the two pathways cannot run at once.
  • Describe the reciprocal regulation of glycolysis and gluconeogenesis by AMP, citrate, fructose 2,6-bisphosphate, acetyl-CoA, insulin, and glucagon.
  • Trace the Cori cycle and the glucose–alanine cycle between muscle and liver, and apply them to alcohol-related hypoglycemia, lactate in sepsis, and stress hyperglycemia.

Your brain burns about 120 g of glucose a day and your red blood cells live on it entirely, yet liver glycogen is exhausted within a day of fasting. The reason a healthy person can skip meals, sleep through the night, or survive a long illness without the brain running out of fuel is gluconeogenesis: the liver's ability to build new glucose from molecules that are not carbohydrates. For a nurse, this is the chemistry behind fasting hypoglycemia, the lactate level in sepsis, and the blood glucose that climbs during stress or steroid therapy.

Why the body makes glucose, and from what

Gluconeogenesis (literally "new glucose birth") is the synthesis of glucose from noncarbohydrate precursors. It runs mainly in the liver, which can export the product because it has glucose-6-phosphatase (Section 13.8). In prolonged fasting the kidney cortex takes on a growing share, up to about 40% after several days. Red blood cells have no mitochondria and depend on glucose; the brain can adapt partly to ketone bodies (Chapter 15) but still needs a steady glucose supply. When glycogen runs out after roughly 24 hours, gluconeogenesis is the only source left.

The raw materials all share one feature: each can be turned into pyruvate, oxaloacetate, or a triose phosphate, the entry points into the pathway.

  • Lactate from working muscle and red blood cells (Section 13.5). Lactate dehydrogenase oxidizes it back to pyruvate.
  • Glycerol released when stored triacylglycerols are hydrolyzed (Chapter 15). The liver phosphorylates and oxidizes it to dihydroxyacetone phosphate, a triose phosphate.
  • Glucogenic amino acids, especially alanine and glutamine released from muscle protein. Transamination (Chapter 15) converts alanine to pyruvate and glutamine, through glutamate, to the citric acid cycle intermediate α-ketoglutarate and then oxaloacetate.

What is not on the list is just as important: fatty acids cannot become glucose. Most fatty acids break down to two-carbon acetyl-CoA (Chapter 15). The reaction that makes acetyl-CoA from pyruvate, catalyzed by pyruvate dehydrogenase (Section 13.5 and Chapter 14), releases CO2 and is irreversible, so there is no road back from acetyl-CoA to pyruvate. And when acetyl-CoA enters the citric acid cycle, its two carbons leave as two CO2 before oxaloacetate is regenerated, so no net oxaloacetate is gained. The fat on your body can supply energy and glycerol during fasting, but the glucose your brain needs must come from lactate, glycerol, and protein.

Running glycolysis in reverse, almost

Gluconeogenesis looks like glycolysis run backward, and seven of its ten steps literally are: the enzymes of glycolysis that catalyze near-equilibrium reactions (the isomerases, aldolase, the dehydrogenase, enolase, and the kinase of step 7) simply run in the reverse direction when product is removed. But glycolysis has three steps that release so much energy they are effectively one-way: the two ATP-spending kinase steps (hexokinase, phosphofructokinase-1) and the ATP-making pyruvate kinase step. A one-way street cannot be driven backward, so gluconeogenesis uses four bypass enzymes to go around them. Table 1 pairs each irreversible glycolytic step with its bypass.

Two patterns are worth noticing. First, the bypasses of the two kinase steps are hydrolyses: the phosphate that glycolysis installed using ATP is removed with water, and the ATP is not recovered. Second, going from pyruvate back up to PEP takes two enzymes and two nucleotides. Pyruvate carboxylase first attaches CO2 (a carboxylation, joining two molecules with ATP, which makes it a ligase) to make the four-carbon oxaloacetate, and PEP carboxykinase then removes that same CO2 (a decarboxylation, splitting a molecule without water, which makes it a lyase) while attaching a phosphate from GTP. The carbon is borrowed and returned; its purpose is to make the energetically expensive formation of PEP possible. Oxaloacetate cannot cross the mitochondrial membrane, so it leaves as malate (or aspartate) and is converted back in the cytosol.

What it costs

Making one glucose requires two pyruvates, so every step below PEP is counted twice. The energy inputs are:

  • pyruvate carboxylase: 2 ATP;
  • PEP carboxykinase: 2 GTP;
  • phosphoglycerate kinase running in reverse (3-phosphoglycerate → 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate): 2 ATP;
  • glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase running in reverse: 2 NADH.

Total: 4 ATP + 2 GTP = 6 ATP-equivalents, plus 2 NADH, per glucose made. Glycolysis of that glucose would return only 2 ATP and 2 NADH. The difference, 4 ATP-equivalents, is the price of pushing the reaction uphill. It also explains why the cell must never run both pathways at full speed in the same cell at the same time: glucose → 2 pyruvate → glucose would spin in place, a futile cycle that burns 4 ATP per turn and produces nothing but heat. Preventing that waste is the job of regulation.

Reciprocal regulation

Because glycolysis and gluconeogenesis use different enzymes at the three key points, each of those points can be a switch. A molecule that activates one direction inhibits the other. Table 2 collects the signals; read it as a pair of opposing columns, with the fed/high-energy signals pushing glycolysis and the fasting/low-energy signals pushing gluconeogenesis.

Professor Tip

Seven reactions reverse, three do not, and the three that do not are all kinase steps. Kinases use or make ATP, and if you need ATP one way, you don’t need it the other way: that is why each kinase step needs a different enzyme for the return trip. When a question asks whether an enzyme belongs to glycolysis or gluconeogenesis, look at the name's ending. A kinase adds phosphate from ATP and belongs to the energy-releasing direction (hexokinase, PFK-1, pyruvate kinase). A phosphatase removes phosphate with water and belongs to the synthetic direction (fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase, glucose-6-phosphatase). The two odd ones, pyruvate carboxylase and PEP carboxykinase, are the detour around pyruvate kinase; remember them as a pair, "carboxylase puts CO2 on, carboxykinase takes it off."

The Cori cycle and the glucose–alanine cycle

Gluconeogenesis connects tissues. A sprinting muscle or an oxygen-poor tissue reduces pyruvate to lactate to regenerate NAD+ (Section 13.5). Muscle cannot turn lactate back into glucose, so the lactate rides the blood to the liver, which oxidizes it to pyruvate and spends 6 ATP-equivalents to make glucose. The glucose returns to the blood, and the muscle can burn it again. This loop is the Cori cycle. Its arithmetic is unfavorable for the body as a whole: each turn gains the muscle 2 ATP and costs the liver 6, a net loss of 4 ATP. Its value is that it moves the energy burden from the working muscle to the resting liver, lets the muscle keep glycolysis running without oxygen, and clears lactate before it causes acidosis.

During fasting a parallel loop, the glucose–alanine cycle, carries nitrogen along with carbon. Muscle breaks down protein, and the amino groups released are transferred onto pyruvate (made by glycolysis) to form alanine (a transamination, Chapter 15, in which an amino group is swapped onto pyruvate's carbonyl carbon). Alanine travels to the liver, where the reverse transamination gives back pyruvate for gluconeogenesis and hands the nitrogen to the urea cycle for safe disposal. The liver returns glucose to the muscle. The cycle explains why long fasting or severe illness costs muscle mass: the body is quite literally converting muscle protein into blood glucose.

Worked examples

Example 1: Which step does this enzyme bypass?

Problem. For each enzyme, name the irreversible glycolytic step it bypasses, give its enzyme class, and state any coenzyme or nucleotide it uses: (a) fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase, (b) pyruvate carboxylase, (c) PEP carboxykinase.

Solution. (a) Bypasses PFK-1 (F6P → F1,6BP). It is a hydrolase: water removes the phosphate from carbon 1 of fructose 1,6-bisphosphate. No ATP is involved. (b) Together with PEPCK, bypasses pyruvate kinase (PEP → pyruvate). It is a ligase: it joins pyruvate and CO2 using ATP, with biotin as the CO2 carrier, and it works in the mitochondrion. (c) Completes the same bypass. It is a lyase: it removes CO2 from oxaloacetate while transferring a phosphate from GTP to form PEP.

Check. Three irreversible steps, four bypass enzymes: the two hydrolases take care of the two kinase steps one for one, and the ligase–lyase pair handles the pyruvate kinase step.

Example 2: Why fasting drinkers become hypoglycemic

Problem. A patient who has not eaten for a day drinks heavily and is found with a blood glucose of 45 mg/dL. Use the chemistry of alcohol metabolism to explain.

Solution. Ethanol is oxidized in the liver by alcohol dehydrogenase to acetaldehyde and then by aldehyde dehydrogenase to acetate (Chapter 4). Both steps reduce NAD+ to NADH, so the liver fills up with NADH. Two gluconeogenic reactions depend on NAD+ being available: lactate → pyruvate and malate → oxaloacetate. With NADH in excess, both equilibria are pushed the wrong way: pyruvate is reduced to lactate and oxaloacetate to malate. The pathway's entry points are drained, so lactate, alanine, and other precursors cannot be turned into glucose. After a day without food, liver glycogen is already gone, so there is no backup, and blood glucose falls. The same NADH surplus raises blood lactate, so lactic acidosis can accompany the hypoglycemia.

Check. Alcohol does not block any single gluconeogenic enzyme; it removes the oxidized coenzyme that two of them require. A well-fed drinker with full glycogen stores is protected, which is why the danger is specific to fasting.

Example 3: Trace a lactate from muscle to glucose

Problem. Follow one lactate molecule from a sprinting thigh muscle until its carbons are part of a glucose molecule in the blood. Name each enzyme and count the energy spent.

Solution. (1) In muscle, lactate dehydrogenase makes lactate from pyruvate, regenerating NAD+. (2) Lactate crosses into the blood and is taken up by the liver. (3) Liver lactate dehydrogenase oxidizes it back to pyruvate (NAD+ → NADH). (4) In the mitochondrion, pyruvate carboxylase adds CO2 to give oxaloacetate (1 ATP; biotin). (5) Oxaloacetate leaves the mitochondrion as malate and PEP carboxykinase converts it to PEP (1 GTP, CO2 released). (6) The reversible glycolytic enzymes run backward up to fructose 1,6-bisphosphate; phosphoglycerate kinase costs 1 ATP and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase uses 1 NADH, which the NADH from step 3 supplies. (7) Two such three-carbon units combine (aldolase, in reverse) into one six-carbon fructose 1,6-bisphosphate. (8) Fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase hydrolyzes it to fructose 6-phosphate, phosphoglucose isomerase gives glucose 6-phosphate, and glucose-6-phosphatase releases free glucose to the blood. Energy per lactate: 1 ATP + 1 GTP + 1 ATP = 3 ATP-equivalents; per glucose (two lactates), 6.

Check. Lactate, unlike pyruvate from alanine, brings its own NADH (step 3), so the liver does not need to supply reducing power for step 6. The carbon count works: 2 × 3 carbons = 6 carbons of glucose, with the CO2 added in step 4 removed again in step 5.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Alcohol-related hypoglycemia (Example 2) is why an intoxicated, unresponsive patient gets a fingerstick glucose at once. Metformin, a common oral drug for type 2 diabetes, lowers blood glucose mainly by reducing hepatic gluconeogenesis; because the liver then clears less lactate, a rare but serious adverse effect is lactic acidosis. Stress hormones push the pathway the other way: cortisol and glucagon raise gluconeogenesis during trauma, surgery, or infection, producing stress hyperglycemia even in patients without diabetes, and corticosteroid drugs (cortisol look-alikes) do the same, which is why glucose is monitored during steroid therapy. Cortisol also breaks down muscle protein to supply alanine and glutamine, so a patient in prolonged critical illness loses muscle and is in negative nitrogen balance (excreting more nitrogen than is taken in; Chapter 15). In sepsis, poorly perfused tissues make lactate faster than the liver can run the Cori cycle; a rising serum lactate signals that mismatch, and falling lactate ("lactate clearance") is a sign that treatment is working. Finally, neonatal hypoglycemia is common in the first hours of life because a newborn's glycogen store is small and the gluconeogenic enzymes, especially PEP carboxykinase, are still being switched on.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

A patient who skipped breakfast for a long morning appointment is relying on liver glycogen and gluconeogenesis to hold blood glucose while sitting still and anxious, and the epinephrine released by anxiety (and the small amount in the anesthetic) raises the demand. Most patients cope, but a patient with diabetes may not. Someone taking metformin alone rarely becomes hypoglycemic, because the drug only restrains the liver's glucose output rather than forcing glucose into cells. Someone on insulin or a sulfonylurea (an oral drug that makes the pancreas release more insulin) is different: those drugs keep driving glucose out of the blood whether or not the patient has eaten, so a missed meal plus a long appointment can produce hypoglycemia in the chair (sweating, shakiness, confusion). The health history question "When did you last eat, and did you take your diabetes medicine?" is a gluconeogenesis question. Patients with heavy alcohol use carry the same risk for the reason in Example 2, on top of the xerostomia (dry mouth) and erosion that alcohol brings.

Molecule in the Clinic: Metformin

Metformin is the first-line drug for type 2 diabetes, and Rosa takes it. It lowers blood glucose mainly by decreasing the liver’s glucose production, which is largely gluconeogenesis. It is not broken down in the body; the kidneys excrete it unchanged, which is why kidney function is checked before and during treatment.

Your turn: Is metformin water-soluble or fat-soluble? Why?

Answer
Water-soluble. It is small, full of N–H groups that hydrogen-bond with water, and its basic nitrogens are protonated at body pH, so it is a cation.

Your turn: Why does slowing gluconeogenesis lower a patient’s fasting blood glucose in particular?

Answer
Overnight, with no food coming in, the liver supplies blood glucose by glycogen breakdown and gluconeogenesis. Less gluconeogenesis means less glucose released while fasting.

Summary

  • Gluconeogenesis makes glucose from lactate, glycerol, and glucogenic amino acids (alanine, glutamine), mainly in the liver and, in long fasting, the kidney cortex. It supplies the brain (about 120 g/day) and red blood cells once glycogen is gone (about 24 h).
  • Fatty acids cannot become glucose: pyruvate dehydrogenase is irreversible, so acetyl-CoA cannot return to pyruvate, and its two carbons leave the citric acid cycle as CO2.
  • Seven glycolytic steps run in reverse; the three irreversible ones are bypassed by pyruvate carboxylase (ligase, biotin, ATP, mitochondrion) plus PEP carboxykinase (lyase, GTP), fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase (hydrolase), and glucose-6-phosphatase (hydrolase, liver and kidney).
  • Cost per glucose: 4 ATP + 2 GTP = 6 ATP-equivalents and 2 NADH, versus 2 ATP gained in glycolysis. Running both at once is a futile cycle.
  • Reciprocal regulation: AMP and fructose 2,6-bisphosphate favor glycolysis (activate PFK-1, inhibit F1,6BPase); citrate and ATP favor gluconeogenesis; acetyl-CoA activates pyruvate carboxylase; insulin favors glycolysis and glucagon/cortisol favor gluconeogenesis.
  • The Cori cycle (muscle lactate → liver glucose → muscle) costs the body 4 ATP per turn and shifts the energy burden to the liver; the glucose–alanine cycle does the same with alanine and carries nitrogen to the liver.
  • Alcohol's excess NADH blocks gluconeogenesis at lactate → pyruvate and malate → oxaloacetate, causing hypoglycemia in fasting drinkers; metformin reduces hepatic gluconeogenesis; cortisol raises it.

Exercises

Exercise 1

Classify each as a gluconeogenic precursor or not, and give the reason: (a) alanine, (b) palmitic acid (a 16-carbon fatty acid), (c) glycerol, (d) lactate, (e) acetyl-CoA.

Answer
(a) Yes; transamination gives pyruvate. (b) No; it is degraded entirely to acetyl-CoA. (c) Yes; it becomes dihydroxyacetone phosphate. (d) Yes; lactate dehydrogenase gives pyruvate. (e) No; the pyruvate dehydrogenase reaction is irreversible and the cycle releases its two carbons as CO2.

Exercise 2

Name the three irreversible steps of glycolysis and the enzyme(s) that bypass each in gluconeogenesis.

Answer
Hexokinase (glucose → G6P), bypassed by glucose-6-phosphatase; PFK-1 (F6P → F1,6BP), bypassed by fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase; pyruvate kinase (PEP → pyruvate), bypassed by pyruvate carboxylase plus PEP carboxykinase.

Exercise 3

Pyruvate carboxylase is a ligase, but PEP carboxykinase is a lyase, and fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase is a hydrolase. Explain each classification from what the enzyme does.

Answer
Pyruvate carboxylase joins two molecules (pyruvate + CO2) using ATP, the definition of a ligase. PEP carboxykinase splits CO2 off oxaloacetate without using water, so it is a lyase (the phosphate from GTP is attached in the same step). Fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase uses water to split off a phosphate group, so it is a hydrolase.

Exercise 4

Show that converting two pyruvates to one glucose costs 6 ATP-equivalents, and compare with the ATP yield of glycolysis. What would happen if both pathways ran at full speed in a liver cell?

Answer
Per glucose: 2 ATP (pyruvate carboxylase) + 2 GTP (PEP carboxykinase) + 2 ATP (phosphoglycerate kinase in reverse) = 6 ATP-equivalents, plus 2 NADH. Glycolysis returns only 2 ATP. Running both would consume a net 4 ATP per cycle with no product, a futile cycle that wastes energy as heat.

Exercise 5

Fructose 2,6-bisphosphate is high after a meal and low during fasting. Predict its effect on PFK-1 and on fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase, and explain why this makes sense.

Answer
It activates PFK-1 and inhibits fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase, so after a meal (insulin high) the liver runs glycolysis and stops making glucose; during fasting (glucagon high) its level falls, PFK-1 slows, the bisphosphatase speeds up, and the liver exports glucose. One signal flips both enzymes in opposite directions.

Exercise 6

A textbook says the Cori cycle "loses 4 ATP per turn." Show where the numbers come from and explain why the body tolerates the loss.

Answer
Muscle glycolysis of one glucose to two lactates yields 2 ATP; liver gluconeogenesis from two lactates to one glucose costs 6 ATP-equivalents; 2 − 6 = −4. The loss is accepted because it lets a working muscle produce ATP without oxygen while the well-oxygenated liver pays the bill, and it clears lactate that would otherwise accumulate and cause acidosis.

Exercise 7

Explain why alcohol causes hypoglycemia in a fasting person but not in someone who drinks with a large meal.

Answer
Ethanol oxidation floods the liver with NADH, which pushes pyruvate to lactate and oxaloacetate to malate, removing the substrates gluconeogenesis needs. A fasting person has no liver glycogen left and depends on gluconeogenesis, so blood glucose falls. After a meal, dietary glucose and full glycogen stores keep blood glucose up without gluconeogenesis.

Exercise 8

A patient receiving high-dose dexamethasone after surgery has a blood glucose of 210 mg/dL and a dietitian notes muscle loss. Connect both findings to one hormone action in this section.

Answer
Dexamethasone mimics cortisol. Cortisol increases synthesis of gluconeogenic enzymes (PEP carboxykinase, glucose-6-phosphatase) and promotes breakdown of muscle protein to supply alanine and glutamine. The liver converts those amino acids to glucose (hyperglycemia), and the muscle that supplied them wastes, producing negative nitrogen balance.
