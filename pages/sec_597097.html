With the general formula ROR′, an ether may be considered a derivative of water in which both hydrogen atoms are replaced by alkyl or aryl groups. It may also be considered a derivative of an alcohol (ROH) in which the hydrogen atom of the OH group has been replaced by a second alkyl or aryl group:

\[\mathrm{HOH\underset{H\: atoms}{\xrightarrow{replace\: both}}ROR'\underset{of\: OH\: group}{\xleftarrow{replace\: H\: atom}}ROH} \nonumber \]

Simple ethers have simple common names, formed from the names of the groups attached to the oxygen atom, followed by the generic name ether. For example, CH3–O–CH2CH2CH3 is methyl propyl ether. If both groups are the same, the group name should be preceded by the prefix di-, as in dimethyl ether (CH3–O–CH3) and diethyl ether CH3CH2–O–CH2CH3.

Ether molecules have no hydrogen atom on the oxygen atom (that is, no OH group). Therefore there is no intermolecular hydrogen bonding between ether molecules, and ethers therefore have quite low boiling points for a given molar mass. Indeed, ethers have boiling points close to those of alkanes of comparable molar mass (often a few degrees higher, because the bent C–O–C is weakly polar) and much lower than those of the corresponding alcohols (Table \(\PageIndex{1}\)).

Table \(\PageIndex{1}\): Comparison of Boiling Points of Alkanes, Alcohols, and Ethers
Condensed Structural Formula Name Molar Mass Boiling Point (°C) Intermolecular Hydrogen Bonding in Pure Liquid?
CH3CH2CH3 propane 44 –42 no
CH3OCH3 dimethyl ether 46 –24 no
CH3CH2OH ethyl alcohol 46 78 yes
CH3CH2CH2CH2CH3 pentane 72 36 no
CH3CH2OCH2CH3 diethyl ether 74 35 no
CH3CH2CH2CH2OH butyl alcohol 74 118 yes

Ether molecules do have an oxygen atom, however, and engage in hydrogen bonding with water molecules. Consequently, an ether has about the same solubility in water as the alcohol that is isomeric with it. For example, dimethyl ether and ethanol (both having the molecular formula C2H6O) are completely soluble in water, whereas diethyl ether and 1-butanol (both C4H10O) are only moderately soluble in water (about 8 g/100 mL of water).

In this course both still count as soluble (four carbons or fewer, Section 4.3). “Soluble” means a useful amount dissolves; it does not mean miscible. Ethanol and dimethyl ether mix with water in any proportion, but diethyl ether and 1-butanol dissolve only to about 8 g per 100 mL, and any more forms a separate layer.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\)

What is the common name for each ether?

  1. CH3CH2CH2OCH2CH2CH3

Solution

  1. The carbon groups on either side of the oxygen atom are propyl (CH3CH2CH2) groups, so the compound is dipropyl ether.
  2. The three-carbon group is attached by the middle carbon atom, so it is an isopropyl group. The one-carbon group is a methyl group. The compound is isopropyl methyl ether.

To Your Health: Ethers as General Anesthetics

A general anesthetic acts on the brain to produce unconsciousness and a general insensitivity to feeling or pain. Diethyl ether (CH3CH2OCH2CH3) was the first general anesthetic to be used.

Diethyl ether is relatively safe because there is a fairly wide gap between the dose that produces an effective level of anesthesia and the lethal dose. However, it is highly flammable and often causes nausea, so it was replaced by nonflammable halogenated compounds. Halothane and enflurane, two of the early replacements, are now rarely used in the United States. Today the common inhaled anesthetics are sevoflurane, desflurane and isoflurane, all of which are halogenated ethers.

Halothane (a haloalkane) and enflurane and isoflurane (halogenated ethers) are not flammable, unlike diethyl ether. Of these three, only isoflurane is still in common use.

Ether molecules have no OH group and thus no intermolecular hydrogen bonding. Ethers therefore have quite low boiling points for a given molar mass. Ether molecules have an oxygen atom and can engage in hydrogen bonding with water molecules. An ether molecule has about the same solubility in water as the alcohol that is isomeric with it.

Lecture Connection

Ethers get two kinds of names in this course, and you need both: the common name ("alkyl alkyl ether") that the section text teaches, and the IUPAC name, which treats the shorter group as an alkoxy substituent on the longer chain. For properties, remember the lecture's line: ethers cannot hydrogen bond to each other, but they can accept a hydrogen bond from water or from an R–OH.

Naming ethers two ways

  • Common name: name both R groups, arrange them alphabetically, and follow with the separate word "ether": CH3OCH2CH3 is ethyl methyl ether. If both groups are the same, use "di": diethyl ether.
  • IUPAC name: the longer R group is the parent chain and the shorter R group, together with its oxygen, is a substituent on that chain called an alkoxy group. Instead of the -yl ending, use -oxy: methoxy (CH3O–), ethoxy (CH3CH2O–). CH3OCH2CH3 is methoxyethane; CH3CH2OCH2CH2CH3 is 1-ethoxypropane; CH3OCH(CH3)CH2CH3 is 2-methoxybutane. Number the chain so the alkoxy group gets the lowest number.

Practice (lecture style). Draw 2-methoxybutane: a four-carbon chain with CH3O– on C2, CH3CH(OCH3)CH2CH3. Name CH3–O–CH2CH2CH2CH2CH3: the longer group is pentane, the methoxy group is on C1, so 1-methoxypentane (common name: methyl pentyl ether).

Example: Naming an ether both ways

Problem. Give a common name and an IUPAC name for CH3OCH2CH2CH3.

Solution. The ether has a methyl group and a propyl group on oxygen. Common name: list them alphabetically, then "ether": methyl propyl ether. IUPAC: the longer chain (propane) is the parent and CH3O– is a methoxy substituent on C1: 1-methoxypropane.

Check. Ether common names end in a separate word "ether"; IUPAC ether names contain "-oxy" and no "ether" at all. The alkoxy group is always the shorter group: 1-methoxypropane, never "propoxymethane."

Boiling point: like an alkane. Solubility: like an alcohol.

An ether has no O–H, so there is no hydrogen bonding between ether molecules, and its boiling point is close to that of an alkane of the same mass. But the ether oxygen's lone pairs can accept hydrogen bonds from water, so an ether dissolves in water about as well as its isomeric alcohol. The section's table makes both points; the worked example below is the comparison the lecture uses.

Example: Comparing boiling points and solubility

Problem. Compare the boiling points and water solubility of 1-butanol, diethyl ether and pentane (all about 72–74 g/mol).

Solution. Boiling point: Pentane (36 °C) and diethyl ether (35 °C) boil at nearly the same temperature. Neither can hydrogen bond with itself, and the ether's weak dipole adds very little. 1-Butanol (118 °C) is far higher because its O–H groups hydrogen bond with each other. Solubility: Diethyl ether and 1-butanol are both moderately soluble (about 7–8 g/100 mL) because the oxygen in each can accept hydrogen bonds from water. Pentane has no polar group and is insoluble.

Check. Hydrogen bonding with itself controls boiling point. Hydrogen bonding with water controls solubility.

Professor Tip

When an exam asks you to rank boiling points, use alkane < ether < alcohol. The gap between the first two is small (1-methoxypropane 39 °C versus pentane 36 °C; diethyl ether and pentane are within a degree), while the gap to the alcohol is large.

Functional group isomers

Every ether has an alcohol isomer with the same formula: dimethyl ether and ethanol (C2H6O), diethyl ether and 1-butanol (C4H10O). The lecture calls these functional group isomers, the third kind of constitutional isomer after skeletal and positional. Section 4.2's Lecture Connection draws five of the seven C4H10O isomers side by side. The quick test for a pair: "same functional group?" If not, they are functional group isomers, and you can expect very different boiling points and reactivity (ethers do not oxidize or dehydrate).

Professor Tip

The anesthetic story in the section text is a reminder that "ether" is also a drug name. Diethyl ether's replacements are halogenated compounds: isoflurane and enflurane are halogenated ethers, while halothane (CF3CHBrCl) has no oxygen and is a haloalkane; and the eucalyptol in mouthrinses is a cyclic ether. When you see a C–O–C with no O–H, you have found the ether, whatever the rest of the molecule looks like.
