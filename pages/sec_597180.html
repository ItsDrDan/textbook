We have seen that the sequence of nucleotides in a cell’s deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) is what ultimately determines the sequence of amino acids in proteins made by the cell and thus is critical for the proper functioning of the cell. On rare occasions, however, the nucleotide sequence in DNA may be modified either spontaneously (by replication errors that escape proofreading and repair, about 1 in every 10 billion nucleotides copied) or from exposure to heat, radiation, or certain chemicals. Any chemical or physical change that alters the nucleotide sequence in DNA is called a mutation. When a mutation occurs in an egg or sperm cell that then produces a living organism, it will be present in every cell of that organism and can be passed on to its offspring.

Common types of mutations include substitution (a different nucleotide is substituted), insertion (the addition of a new nucleotide), and deletion (the loss of a nucleotide). Some books call all three “point mutations” because only one nucleotide changes. In this course the terms are narrower: a point mutation means one base replaced by another (a substitution), an insertion mutation adds a base, and a deletion mutation removes one (Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\)). Because an insertion or deletion results in a frame-shift that changes the reading of subsequent codons and, therefore, alters the entire amino acid sequence that follows the mutation, insertions and deletions are usually more harmful than a substitution in which only a single amino acid is altered.

The chemical or physical agents that cause mutations are called mutagens. Examples of physical mutagens are ultraviolet (UV) and gamma radiation. Radiation exerts its mutagenic effect either directly or by creating free radicals that in turn have mutagenic effects. Radiation and free radicals can lead to the formation of bonds between nitrogenous bases in DNA. For example, exposure to UV light can result in the formation of a covalent bond between two adjacent thymines on a DNA strand, producing a thymine dimer (Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\)). If not repaired, the dimer prevents the formation of the double helix at the point where it occurs. The genetic disease xeroderma pigmentosum is caused by a lack of the enzyme that cuts out the thymine dimers in damaged DNA. Individuals affected by this condition are abnormally sensitive to light and are more prone to skin cancer than normal individuals.

Sometimes gene mutations are beneficial, but most of them are detrimental. For example, if a point mutation occurs at a crucial position in a DNA sequence, the affected protein will lack biological activity, perhaps resulting in the death of a cell. In such cases the altered DNA sequence is lost and will not be copied into daughter cells. Nonlethal mutations in an egg or sperm cell may lead to metabolic abnormalities or hereditary diseases. Such diseases are called inborn errors of metabolism or genetic diseases. A partial listing of genetic diseases is presented in Table \(\PageIndex{1}\). You do not need to memorize this table; know PKU, sickle cell disease, cystic fibrosis and Tay–Sachs disease. Two of these diseases are discussed below. In most cases, the defective gene results in a failure to synthesize a particular enzyme.

PKU results from the absence of the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase. Without this enzyme, a person cannot convert phenylalanine to tyrosine, which is the precursor of the neurotransmitters dopamine and norepinephrine as well as the skin pigment melanin.

When this reaction cannot occur, phenylalanine builds up in the blood, and some of it is converted to phenylpyruvate. The disease is named for this phenyl ketone, which appears in the urine. The high level of phenylalanine itself is what harms the developing brain; untreated, PKU causes severe intellectual disability.

PKU is found by newborn screening: a few drops of blood from a heel stick, usually collected 24 to 48 hours after birth, are tested for phenylalanine. If the level is high, the infant is started right away on a diet very low in phenylalanine. Because phenylalanine is plentiful in ordinary proteins, the diet relies on a special medical formula plus small, measured amounts of regular foods, and it continues for life. Children who are diagnosed early and stay on the diet usually develop normally. PKU affects about 1 in 12,000 newborns in North America.

Every state in the United States has mandated that screening for PKU be provided to all newborns.

Several genetic diseases are collectively categorized as lipid-storage diseases. Lipids are constantly being synthesized and broken down in the body, so if the enzymes that catalyze lipid degradation are missing, the lipids tend to accumulate and cause a variety of medical problems. When a genetic mutation occurs in the gene for the enzyme hexosaminidase A, for example, gangliosides cannot be degraded but accumulate in brain tissue, causing the ganglion cells of the brain to become greatly enlarged and nonfunctional. This genetic disease, known as Tay-Sachs disease, leads to a regression in development, dementia, paralysis, and blindness, with death usually occurring before the age of three. There is currently no treatment, but Tay-Sachs disease can be diagnosed in a fetus by assaying the amniotic fluid (amniocentesis) for hexosaminidase A. A blood test can identify Tay-Sachs carriers—people who inherit a defective gene from only one rather than both parents—because they produce only half the normal amount of hexosaminidase A, although they do not exhibit symptoms of the disease.

Summary

  • The nucleotide sequence in DNA may be modified either spontaneously or from exposure to heat, radiation, or certain chemicals and can lead to mutations.
  • Mutagens are the chemical or physical agents that cause mutations.
  • Genetic diseases are hereditary diseases that occur because of a mutation in a critical gene.

Lecture Connection

In class you will be asked to identify the type of change in the DNA, a point mutation (one base replaced by another), a deletion mutation (a base removed) or an insertion mutation (a base added), and then to predict what that does to the protein. Count the bases first: same count means a substitution; one more or one fewer means a frameshift.

What causes mutations

Some are spontaneous copying errors that escape proofreading. Others are caused by mutagens:

  • Ultraviolet (UV) light makes two neighboring thymines bond covalently to each other (a thymine dimer), which the polymerase cannot read. Repair enzymes remove most dimers; the ones that escape cause skin cancer. Every sunburn is DNA damage.
  • Ionizing radiation (X-rays, gamma rays, radioactive particles) breaks the backbone directly or makes reactive radicals from water that attack the bases. Dental radiographs use ionizing radiation, which is why you follow ALARA (as low as reasonably achievable): digital sensors, collimation, thyroid collars, and radiographs only when they change the treatment plan.
  • Chemicals: nitrous acid (HNO2) deaminates the heterocyclic bases, converting an NH2 to C=O, so cytosine becomes uracil and pairs with A instead of G; polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons in tobacco smoke (Chapter 3) and aflatoxin (made by molds on poorly stored grain and nuts, which is why crops such as Valley almonds and pistachios are screened for it) become reactive after the liver oxidizes them and then bond to guanine. A chemical that causes mutations is a mutagen; one that causes cancer is a carcinogen, and most carcinogens are mutagens.

Worked examples

Example 1: The sickle-cell mutation

Problem: In the normal β-globin gene, the coding strand for codons 5–7 reads 5′-CCT GAG GAG-3′. In sickle cell disease the middle codon is CCT GTG GAG. Write both mRNAs, name the amino acids, classify the mutation, and explain why one amino acid matters so much.

Solution

Normal mRNA: 5′-CCU GAG GAG-3′ = Pro-Glu-Glu. Mutant mRNA: 5′-CCU GUG GAG-3′ = Pro-Val-Glu. One base (A → T in DNA, A → U in mRNA) replaced another, and the new codon codes for a different amino acid, so this is a substitution that is missense. Glutamate has a charged, polar side chain (–CH2CH2COO−); valine has a nonpolar isopropyl side chain. The nonpolar patch on the surface of hemoglobin S sticks to a pocket on neighboring molecules when oxygen is unloaded, and the molecules stack into fibers that deform the red cell.

Check: The frame is unchanged (still three-base codons), so only one amino acid differs, which matches a single substitution. A frameshift would have scrambled every codon after it.

Example 2: Classify the mutations

Problem: The mRNA 5′-AUG UGG CAU AAA UAG-3′ (Met-Trp-His-Lys) undergoes three different mutations. Classify each: (a) UGG → UAG; (b) CAU → CAC; (c) a G is inserted after AUG, giving AUG GUG GCA UAA AUA G.

Solution

(a) UGG (Trp) becomes UAG, a stop codon: a nonsense mutation; the peptide is just Met. (b) CAU and CAC both code for His: silent. (c) Reading in threes after the insertion: AUG GUG GCA UAA = Met-Val-Ala-stop. Every codon after the insertion changed and a new stop appeared early: a frameshift.

Check: Substitutions keep the number of bases (15) and change at most one amino acid; the insertion makes 16 bases, which is not a multiple of three, and changes everything downstream.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Many inherited diseases are single mutations that you can now read. Sickle cell disease is one missense substitution: in the gene for the β chain of hemoglobin, codon 6 changes from GAG to GTG (DNA coding strand), so the mRNA codon GAG (Glu) becomes GUG (Val). A polar, charged glutamate on the protein surface becomes a nonpolar valine; deoxygenated hemoglobin molecules stick to one another at that patch and polymerize into rods that bend the red cell into a sickle, causing hemolytic anemia and painful vaso-occlusive crises. Cystic fibrosis is most often F508del (also written ΔF508), a three-base deletion that removes one phenylalanine (residue 508) from the CFTR chloride-channel protein; the reading frame is intact, but the protein misfolds and is destroyed, so thick mucus clogs the airways and pancreatic ducts. Newer drugs called CFTR modulators (for example, the combination elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor) help the misfolded protein reach the cell surface and work better. Phenylketonuria (PKU) is a mutation in the gene for phenylalanine hydroxylase, the enzyme that converts phenylalanine to tyrosine (Chapter 15), so phenylalanine builds up and damages the developing brain. All three are autosomal recessive and all three are caught by newborn screening of the heel-stick blood spot (abnormal hemoglobin, elevated phenylalanine, and a CF marker confirmed by sweat chloride or DNA testing), so management such as the PKU low-phenylalanine diet can start in the first weeks of life.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Two inherited conditions you will see on radiographs are mutations in the genes for tooth matrix proteins: amelogenesis imperfecta (defective enamel proteins; thin, pitted, discolored enamel) and dentinogenesis imperfecta (defective dentin protein; opalescent teeth). Both are examples of a change in primary structure (Chapter 10) that traces back to a change in a gene, and both are diagnosed from the family history and the radiograph rather than from a blood test.
