Problem 3.53

What is the major product when 2-methyl-1-butene, CH2=C(CH3)CH2CH3, reacts with water and an acid catalyst?

Problem 3.56

Compare and predict. Cyclopentene and 1-pentene both have the formula C5H10. Each is treated with HBr. For each alkene, say whether Markovnikov's rule is needed, and give the major product. Then explain why one of them is a cleaner reaction than the other.

Answer
Cyclopentene is symmetrical: both double-bond carbons are CH and carry identical ring groups, so either placement of Br gives the same compound, bromocyclopentane, and no rule is needed. 1-Pentene is unsymmetrical: C1 has two H's and C2 has one, so Markovnikov's rule puts H on C1 and Br on C2, giving 2-bromopentane as the major product with a little 1-bromopentane as a minor product. The cyclopentene reaction is cleaner because only one product is possible.

Problem 3.57

Compare and predict. 1,3-Cyclohexadiene (C6H8, a six-membered ring with two C=C bonds) and benzene (C6H6) are each shaken with red-brown Br2 in the dark with no catalyst. Predict what you would see in each test tube and explain the difference in terms of structure.

Answer
The cyclohexadiene tube loses the bromine color: its double bonds are ordinary alkene bonds, and Br2 adds across them (addition, giving dibromo and then tetrabromo products). The benzene tube stays red-brown: benzene's six extra electrons are shared around the whole ring, and adding Br2 across one "double bond" would destroy that aromatic stability. Benzene reacts with Br2 only when FeBr3 is present, and then by substitution (bromobenzene + HBr), not addition. Two more double bonds in a ring do not make benzene; the alternating, fully shared arrangement does.

Analyze the data. The table lists boiling points for several unsaturated hydrocarbons.

More Practice: Clinical and Integrative

Problem 3.60

Nursing. A box of IV administration sets is labeled "PVC, DEHP-free." (a) Write the condensed structure of the monomer and the polymer's repeating unit, and name the reaction type that joins the monomers. (b) The monomer, vinyl chloride, is a toxic gas, yet the finished tubing is safe enough to carry fluids into a patient's vein. Explain, in terms of functional groups, why the polymer is so much less reactive than its monomer. (c) What is DEHP, and why does the label matter for a fat-emulsion infusion?

Answer
(a) Monomer: chloroethene (vinyl chloride), CH2=CHCl. Repeating unit: –CH2–CHCl–. Addition polymerization: each C=C opens and bonds to the next monomer with nothing lost. (b) Polymerization uses up every double bond. The polymer backbone is an alkane-like chain of C–C single bonds with C–Cl groups, so it has no C=C to undergo addition and is as unreactive toward body fluids as a haloalkane; the monomer's reactivity (and its ability to reach cells as a gas) came from the double bond. (c) DEHP is a phthalate ester added as a plasticizer to make rigid PVC flexible. It is not chemically bonded to the polymer and is nonpolar, so it can leach into lipid-rich fluids such as fat emulsions or blood products; DEHP-free sets avoid delivering it to the patient.

Problem 3.61

Nursing. A patient with hyperlipidemia (elevated blood lipids) brings in a snack label that lists "partially hydrogenated soybean oil." (a) Name the reaction, the reagent, and the catalyst used to make this ingredient, and state what happens to a C=C bond during it. (b) Why is the oil solid afterward? (c) Explain what "trans fat" has to do with cis/trans isomerism and which two numbers on the patient's lipid panel it tends to push in the wrong direction.

Answer
(a) Hydrogenation: H2 gas with a metal catalyst (usually Ni; Pt or Pd also work). Each C=C that reacts gains one H on each carbon and becomes a C–C single bond. (b) The natural cis double bonds put kinks in the fatty-acid chains so they cannot pack closely; removing double bonds straightens the chains, so they pack tightly, dispersion forces increase, and the fat melts above room temperature. (c) During partial hydrogenation some double bonds that survive are flipped from cis to trans, the stereoisomer with the chain groups on opposite sides of the C=C. A trans chain is nearly straight, like a saturated chain, and in the body trans fats raise LDL cholesterol and lower HDL cholesterol, both of which worsen the risk of atherosclerosis.

Problem 3.62

Dental hygiene. A pit-and-fissure sealant is dispensed as a thin liquid of methacrylate monomers (each containing a C=C) and hardens in about 20 seconds under a blue curing light. (a) Name the type of reaction that hardens it and describe what happens to the C=C bonds. (b) A manufacturer's note says that an "oxygen-inhibited layer" of uncured resin remains on the surface. Would a drop of Br2 solution react faster with the uncured surface layer or with the fully cured resin underneath? Explain. (c) Dentures and orthodontic retainers are poly(methyl methacrylate). What functional group does the monomer contain besides the C=C? (You will study it in Chapter 6.)

Answer
(a) Addition polymerization. The light starts a chain reaction in which each monomer's C=C opens and forms a new C–C single bond to the next monomer, linking thousands of monomers into one hard network with no atoms lost. (b) The uncured layer: it still contains free C=C bonds that can add Br2 and decolorize it. The cured resin has used its double bonds to build the polymer backbone, so it has almost nothing for Br2 to add to. (c) An ester group (–COOCH3). Methyl methacrylate is the methyl ester of methacrylic acid; only its C=C takes part in the polymerization, and the ester groups hang off the finished chain.

Problem 3.63

Dental hygiene. During an intake exam a patient who smokes a pack a day shows deep periodontal pockets and bone loss, signs of periodontitis. (a) Tobacco smoke contains benzo[a]pyrene, a five-ring polynuclear aromatic hydrocarbon. What structural feature makes a hydrocarbon "polynuclear aromatic," and how does naphthalene, the simplest example, end up with the formula C10H8 rather than C12H10? (b) Aromatic rings are described as unusually stable. How can a stable molecule like benzo[a]pyrene cause oral cancer? (c) Why does this patient's gum tissue bleed less than you would expect for the amount of disease present, and why is that a problem?

Answer
(a) Two or more benzene rings fused together, sharing an edge. In naphthalene the two rings share two carbons, so there are 10 carbons, not 12, and the two shared carbons have no hydrogens, leaving 8 H. (b) The ring itself does not react in the mouth. The danger comes when the body tries to get rid of this nonpolar, fat-soluble molecule: liver and tissue enzymes oxidize it to make it water soluble, and some of those oxidized intermediates are reactive enough to bond to DNA, producing mutations that can start a cancer. (c) Nicotine and other components of smoke constrict the small blood vessels of the gingiva, so the bleeding on probing that normally flags gum disease is reduced. The disease can therefore be more advanced than the bleeding suggests, which is why pocket depth and bone loss, not bleeding alone, are used to assess a smoker.

Problem 3.64

Integrative (Chapters 2 and 3). Three unlabeled vials each contain a colorless liquid with six carbons: cyclohexane, cyclohexene, and benzene. (a) Write the molecular formula of each. (b) Describe how the bromine test (Br2, in the dark) and the KMnO4 test would let you tell all three apart, naming any organic product that forms. (c) One of the three reacts with Br2 only slowly in bright light and gives off a sharp-smelling gas. Which one, what is the gas, and what reaction type is this?

Answer
(a) Cyclohexane C6H12, cyclohexene C6H10, benzene C6H6. (b) Only cyclohexene decolorizes Br2 immediately (addition to give 1,2-dibromocyclohexane) and turns purple KMnO4 brown (addition to give a diol plus MnO2). Cyclohexane and benzene fail both tests in the dark, so they must be told apart another way: benzene reacts with Br2 only when FeBr3 is added (giving bromobenzene by substitution), while cyclohexane needs light. (c) Cyclohexane. In light, Br2 slowly replaces a ring H to give bromocyclohexane and HBr; the pungent gas is HBr. This is halogenation of an alkane, a substitution reaction (Chapter 2), not an addition.

Problem 3.65

Integrative (Chapters 2, 3, and 4). 1-Methylcyclohexene is treated with water and an acid catalyst. (a) Name the product and explain how Markovnikov's rule chooses it. (b) Using the carbon-classification rules from Chapter 2, is the carbon bearing the OH primary, secondary, or tertiary? (c) In Chapter 4 you will run this reaction in reverse by heating the alcohol with acid (dehydration). Predict the major alkene from that elimination and explain whether it is the same compound you started with.

Answer
(a) 1-Methylcyclohexanol. The double-bond carbon with the methyl has no H; the other has one H. The H adds to the carbon that already has an H, and the OH goes to the carbon carrying the methyl. (b) Tertiary: that carbon is bonded to three other carbons (the methyl and two ring carbons). (c) Dehydration removes OH and an H from a neighboring carbon. Zaitsev's rule favors the more substituted double bond, which is the one inside the ring between C1 and C2 (three carbon groups on the C=C), so the major product is 1-methylcyclohexene, the original alkene; the minor product is methylenecyclohexane, with the C=C to the CH2 outside the ring. Addition and elimination are the same reaction run in opposite directions.

Problem 3.68

Match each reaction with its name: dehydration, dehydrogenation, dehydrohalogenation, dehalogenation. (1) CH3CHBrCHBrCH3 → CH3CH=CHCH3 + Br2; (2) cyclohexanol → cyclohexene + H2O; (3) CH3CH2Cl → CH2=CH2 + HCl; (4) CH3CH3 → CH2=CH2 + H2. Which addition reaction is the reverse of each?

Problem 3.69

2-Methylcyclohexanol (a cyclohexane ring with an OH on C1 and a methyl on C2) is heated with a strong acid. (a) Name the reaction type. (b) Draw or describe the two alkenes that can form. (c) Use Zaitsev's rule to pick the major product and explain.

Answer
(a) Dehydration, an elimination that removes H and OH from neighboring carbons and releases water. (b) The OH is on C1, so the H can be taken from C2 (the carbon that carries the methyl; it has only one H) or from C6 (a CH2 with two H's). Removing from C2 gives 1-methylcyclohexene, with the double bond between the methyl-bearing carbon and C1. Removing from C6 gives 3-methylcyclohexene, with the double bond between C1 and C6 and the methyl one carbon away. (c) Zaitsev: remove the lonely hydrogen from the carbon with the fewest hydrogens, C2. The major product is 1-methylcyclohexene, whose C=C carries three carbon groups (two ring carbons and the methyl) versus two for 3-methylcyclohexene.

Problem 3.70

Practice in reverse. (a) What haloalkane would you use to make cyclohexene by dehydrohalogenation? (b) What alkene and what reagent would you use to make 2-chloro-2-methylpropane, (CH3)3CCl, in a single addition step? (c) What alcohol would dehydrate to give only 2-methylpropene with no other alkene possible?

Answer
(a) Chlorocyclohexane (or bromocyclohexane). Removing HX from the ring carbon bearing the halogen and a neighbor gives cyclohexene; every neighbor is equivalent, so there is only one product. The carbon with the OH is surrounded by three identical methyls, so whichever neighbor loses an H, the product is the same 2-methylpropene.

Problem 3.74

True or false? (If false, correct it.) Zaitsev's rule states that when H–X adds to an alkene, the H bonds to the double-bond carbon that already has more hydrogens.

Answer
False. That statement is Markovnikov's rule, which governs addition. Zaitsev's rule governs elimination: the major alkene is formed by removing a hydrogen from the carbon that has the fewest hydrogens, so the more substituted alkene is the major product.

Answer
(a) A five-carbon zigzag with the first bond drawn double and a short line (methyl) off the fourth corner. (b) C4 is bonded to C3, C5, and the methyl carbon: 3°. (c) C1 (=CH2) has two H's and C2 (=CH–) has one, so H goes to C1 and OH to C2: CH3CH(OH)CH2CH(CH3)CH3, 4-methyl-2-pentanol. (d) An alcohol. Problem 3.76

For 2-butyne, CH3C≡CCH3, and 2-butene, CH3CH=CHCH3: (a) give the geometry and the C–C–C bond angle at C2 of each; (b) explain why 2-butene has cis and trans isomers but 2-butyne does not; (c) give the general formula each fits (CnH2n or CnH2n−2).

Answer
(a) 2-Butyne: C2 has two electron groups (the triple bond and one single bond), so it is linear, 180°. 2-Butene: C2 has three electron groups, trigonal planar, 120°. (b) The planar double bond has two "sides," so the two methyls can be on the same side (cis) or opposite sides (trans). A triple bond is linear: the two methyls lie on the same straight line as the alkyne carbons, so there are no sides to be on. (c) 2-Butene C4H8 fits CnH2n; 2-butyne C4H6 fits CnH2n−2.

Problem 3.77

State the relationship between the compounds in each pair: stereoisomers, constitutional isomers (say positional or skeletal), the same compound, or not isomers at all. (a) cis-2-butene and trans-2-butene; (b) 1-butene and 2-butene; (c) 1-butene and methylcyclopropane; (d) 2-butene and 2-butyne; (e) CH3CH=CHCH2CH3 and CH3CH2CH=CHCH3.

Answer
(a) Stereoisomers: same formula (C4H8) and the same atom-to-atom connections, differing only in the 3-D arrangement around the rigid C=C. (b) Constitutional (positional) isomers: both C4H8, same four-carbon chain, double bond in a different position. (c) Constitutional (skeletal) isomers: both C4H8, but one is an open chain with a double bond and the other a three-carbon ring with a branch. (d) Not isomers: C4H8 versus C4H6. (e) The same compound, 2-pentene, written from opposite ends; find the terminal CH3 groups and count the carbons between them and the double bond.
