Learning Objectives
  • Describe the structure and function of ketone bodies.

In the liver, most of the acetyl-CoA obtained from fatty acid oxidation is oxidized by the citric acid cycle. However, some of the acetyl-CoA is used to synthesize a group of compounds known as ketone bodies: acetoacetate, β-hydroxybutyrate, and acetone. Two acetyl-CoA molecules combine, in a reversal of the final step of β-oxidation, to produce acetoacetyl-CoA. The acetoacetyl-CoA reacts with another molecule of acetyl-CoA and water to form β-hydroxy-β-methylglutaryl-CoA, which is then cleaved to acetoacetate and acetyl-CoA. Most of the acetoacetate is reduced to β-hydroxybutyrate, while a small amount is decarboxylated to carbon dioxide and acetone.

The acetoacetate and β-hydroxybutyrate synthesized by the liver are released into the blood for use as a metabolic fuel (to be converted back to acetyl-CoA) by other tissues, particularly the kidney and the heart. Thus, during prolonged starvation, ketone bodies provide about 70% of the energy requirements of the brain. Under normal conditions, the kidneys excrete about 20 mg of ketone bodies each day, and the blood levels are maintained at about 1 mg of ketone bodies per 100 mL of blood.

In starvation, diabetes mellitus, and certain other physiological conditions in which cells do not receive sufficient amounts of carbohydrate, the rate of fatty acid oxidation increases to provide energy. This leads to an increase in the concentration of acetyl-CoA. The increased acetyl-CoA cannot be oxidized by the citric acid cycle because of a decrease in the concentration of oxaloacetate, which is diverted to glucose synthesis. In response, the rate of ketone body formation in the liver increases further, to a level much higher than can be used by other tissues. The excess ketone bodies accumulate in the blood and the urine, a condition referred to as ketosis. When the acetone in the blood reaches the lungs, its volatility causes it to be expelled in the breath. The sweet smell of acetone, a characteristic of ketosis, is frequently noticed on the breath of severely diabetic patients.

Because two of the three kinds of ketone bodies are weak acids, their presence in the blood in excessive amounts overwhelms the blood buffers and causes a marked decrease in blood pH (to 6.9 from a normal value of 7.4). This decrease in pH leads to a serious condition known as acidosis. One of the effects of acidosis is a decrease in the ability of hemoglobin to transport oxygen in the blood. In moderate to severe acidosis, breathing becomes labored and very painful. The body also loses fluids and becomes dehydrated as the kidneys attempt to get rid of the acids by eliminating large quantities of water. The lowered oxygen supply and dehydration lead to depression; even mild acidosis leads to lethargy, loss of appetite, and a generally run-down feeling. Untreated patients may go into a coma. At that point, prompt treatment is necessary if the person’s life is to be saved.

Lecture Connection

Know the initial reactant and final products of ketogenesis: acetyl CoA in; acetoacetate, then β-hydroxybutyrate or acetone out. Know why the pathway runs (too much acetyl CoA for a citric acid cycle that is short of oxaloacetate), and classify its steps with the same four questions as always. In this course

The section calls the dangerous state “acidosis”; the lecture term is ketoacidosis, and in a patient with diabetes, diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). Keep ketosis (ketone bodies accumulating, blood pH still buffered) separate from ketoacidosis (blood pH actually falls). The section’s 70% figure for the brain’s energy from ketone bodies applies to prolonged starvation; the lecture’s point is simply that the brain, which cannot burn fatty acids, switches to ketone bodies after a few days without carbohydrate.

Ketogenesis as a four-question table

β-Oxidation of fatty acids releases a large number of acetyl CoA molecules. If the citric acid cycle cannot process them all (during fasting or insulin deficiency the liver is pulling oxaloacetate out of the cycle to make glucose, Section 13.10), the excess acetyl CoA is diverted in the liver into the three ketone bodies. Technically the middle ketone body, β-hydroxybutyrate, is not a ketone (it has a 2° alcohol instead), but it is made from one and can be oxidized back to one, so it keeps the name. Acetone is more of a by-product than a product of the pathway; it is volatile and leaves in the breath.

  • Who uses them? The liver makes ketone bodies but lacks the enzyme to activate them, so it exports them. Heart and skeletal muscle use them readily (they are turned back into acetyl CoA); after two to three days of fasting the brain, which cannot burn fatty acids, gets most of its energy from ketone bodies, sparing muscle protein that would otherwise be broken down for gluconeogenesis.
  • Ketosis vs. ketoacidosis. Ketosis is the buildup of ketone bodies in blood and urine from starvation, vigorous dieting, alcoholism, or uncontrolled diabetes; the mild form brings headache, dry mouth, and sometimes acetone-smelling breath, and the blood buffers handle it. Ketoacidosis is the runaway version, in which the acidic ketone bodies actually lower blood pH: heavy breathing and increased urine output that can lead to dehydration, and ultimately coma and death. In DKA insulin does not function, glucose cannot be used for energy, fats are broken down to acetyl CoA, and ketogenesis runs unchecked; the ketone bodies are soluble in blood and urine, and acetone is detectable in the breath.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Reading ketones. Urine ketone strips detect acetoacetate, but in DKA most of the acid is β-hydroxybutyrate, so a blood β-hydroxybutyrate level is the better measure; as a patient recovers and β-hydroxybutyrate is oxidized back to acetoacetate, the urine strip can briefly look worse while the patient is getting better. Starvation ketosis gives modest ketones with normal glucose; DKA gives high ketones and hyperglycemia (high blood glucose) with a low bicarbonate. The signs follow the chemistry: fruity (acetone) breath, deep rapid Kussmaul breathing as the body blows off CO2 to raise pH, frequent urination as glucose and ketone bodies pull water into the urine, and dehydration. DKA is a medical emergency, and patients in a dental chair can show these signs too.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Breath tells a story. A sweet, fruity, nail-polish-remover odor is acetone: ketone bodies are being made in quantity. In a patient who is also thirsty, urinating often, breathing deeply, or confused, it may signal DKA and needs urgent referral; in a fasting or low-carb patient with no other signs, it is simple ketosis. Diabetes and the periodontium. Poorly controlled diabetes (high HbA1c) worsens periodontitis and slows healing after scaling and root planing, and periodontal inflammation in turn raises blood glucose, so your care is part of diabetes care. Dry mouth from ketosis or from the dehydration of hyperglycemia also raises caries risk (Section 13.6).
