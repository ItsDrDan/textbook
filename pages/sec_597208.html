Learning Objectives
  • To describe how excess amino acids are degraded.

The liver is the principal site of amino acid metabolism, but other tissues, such as the kidney, the small intestine, muscles, and adipose tissue, take part. Generally, the first step in the breakdown of amino acids is the separation of the amino group from the carbon skeleton, usually by a transamination reaction. The carbon skeletons resulting from the deaminated amino acids are used to form either glucose or fats, or they are converted to a metabolic intermediate that can be oxidized by the citric acid cycle. The latter alternative, amino acid catabolism, is more likely to occur when glucose levels are low—for example, when a person is fasting or starving.

Transamination

Transamination is an exchange of functional groups between any amino acid (except lysine, proline, and threonine) and an α-keto acid. The amino group is usually transferred to the keto carbon atom of pyruvate, oxaloacetate, or α-ketoglutarate, converting the α-keto acid to alanine, aspartate, or glutamate, respectively. Transamination reactions are catalyzed by specific transaminases (also called aminotransferases), which require pyridoxal phosphate as a coenzyme.

In an α-keto acid, the carbonyl or keto group is located on the carbon atom adjacent to the carboxyl group of the acid.

Oxidative Deamination

In the breakdown of amino acids for energy, the final acceptor of the α-amino group is α-ketoglutarate, forming glutamate. Glutamate can then undergooxidative deamination, in which it loses its amino group as an ammonium (NH4+) ion and is oxidized back to α-ketoglutarate (ready to accept another amino group):

This reaction occurs primarily in liver mitochondria. Most of the NH4+ ion formed by oxidative deamination of glutamate is converted to urea and excreted in the urine in a series of reactions known as the urea cycle.

The synthesis of glutamate occurs in animal cells by reversing the reaction catalyzed by glutamate dehydrogenase. For this reaction nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) acts as the reducing agent. The synthesis of glutamate is significant because it is one of the few reactions in animals that can incorporate inorganic nitrogen (NH4+) into an α-keto acid to form an amino acid. The amino group can then be passed on through transamination reactions, to produce other amino acids from the appropriate α-keto acids.

The Fate of the Carbon Skeleton

Any amino acid can be converted into an intermediate of the citric acid cycle. Once the amino group is removed, usually by transamination, the α-keto acid that remains is catabolized by a pathway unique to that acid and consisting of one or more reactions. For example, phenylalanine undergoes a series of six reactions before it splits into fumarate and acetoacetate. Fumarate is an intermediate in the citric acid cycle, while acetoacetate must be converted to acetoacetyl-coenzyme A (CoA) and then to acetyl-CoA before it enters the citric acid cycle.

Those amino acids that can form any of the intermediates of carbohydrate metabolism can subsequently be converted to glucose via a metabolic pathway known as gluconeogenesis. These amino acids are called glucogenic amino acids. Amino acids that are converted to acetoacetyl-CoA or acetyl-CoA, which can be used for the synthesis of ketone bodies but not glucose, are called ketogenic amino acids. Some amino acids fall into both categories. Leucine and lysine are the only amino acids that are exclusively ketogenic. Career Focus: Exercise Physiologist

An exercise physiologist works with individuals who have or wish to prevent developing a wide variety of chronic diseases, such as diabetes, in which exercise has been shown to be beneficial. Each individual must be referred by a licensed physician. An exercise physiologist works in a variety of settings, such as a hospital or in a wellness program at a commercial business, to design and monitor individual exercise plans. A registered clinical exercise physiologist must have an undergraduate degree in exercise physiology or a related degree. Some job opportunities require a master’s degree in exercise physiology or a related degree.

Summary

Generally the first step in the breakdown of amino acids is the removal of the amino group, usually through a reaction known as transamination. The carbon skeletons of the amino acids undergo further reactions to form compounds that can either be used for the synthesis of glucose or the synthesis of ketone bodies.

Lecture Connection

Unlike glucose and fat, amino acids are not stored. Whatever the body does not need for protein synthesis is broken down in two parts: the nitrogen (the amino group) is removed and disposed of as urea, and the carbon skeleton is burned or converted to glucose or fat. The lecture asks you to classify the two nitrogen-removing reactions with the same four questions as every other step: transamination is a transfer (transferase, vitamin B6), oxidative deamination is an oxidation (oxidoreductase, NAD+). The urea cycle is in Section 15.10 and the carbon skeletons in Section 15.4.

Step 1: Transamination

An aminotransferase (also called a transaminase; enzyme class transferase) swaps the amino group of an amino acid with the keto oxygen of an α-keto acid. The usual acceptor is α-ketoglutarate, the citric acid cycle intermediate, which becomes glutamate. The coenzyme is pyridoxal phosphate (PLP), the active form of vitamin B6, which holds the amino group between the two halves of the reaction. After transamination the original amino acid contains only C, H, and O. The reaction is reversible, which is why the same enzymes also make nonessential amino acids (Section 15.11).

The two aminotransferases you will see on lab reports are ALT (alanine aminotransferase: alanine + α-ketoglutarate → pyruvate + glutamate) and AST (aspartate aminotransferase: aspartate + α-ketoglutarate → oxaloacetate + glutamate). Transamination funnels the nitrogen of almost every amino acid into one molecule, glutamate.

Example 1: Transamination

Problem. Write the products when alanine transfers its amino group to α-ketoglutarate.

Solution. Alanine + α-ketoglutarate → pyruvate + glutamate (catalyzed by ALT). Pyruvate can become glucose, so alanine is glucogenic.

Check: The amino acid lost its NH2 and gained a C=O; the keto acid did the reverse. Nothing else changed, and no nitrogen was released.

Step 2: Oxidative deamination

In the mitochondria, glutamate dehydrogenase (an oxidoreductase, using NAD+ or NADP+) oxidizes glutamate and releases its nitrogen as free ammonium ion, NH4+, regenerating α-ketoglutarate for another round of transamination:

glutamate + NAD+ + H2O → α-ketoglutarate + NH4+ + NADH + H+

Ammonia is toxic, especially to the brain, so it cannot be allowed to build up in the blood. The liver converts it to urea (Section 15.10). Run in reverse with NADPH, as the section above notes, the same enzyme is one of the few ways the body puts inorganic nitrogen into an amino acid.

Practice: Fill in the four columns for each reaction. (a) Transamination of an α-amino acid with α-ketoglutarate. (b) Oxidative deamination of glutamate.

Answer
(a) Type: transfer of an amino group (a group exchange; no oxidation, since one C=O is traded for one C–NH2). How can I tell: the amino acid became an α-keto acid and the α-keto acid became an amino acid; no coenzyme changed form. Enzyme: aminotransferase, a transferase. Coenzyme: pyridoxal phosphate (vitamin B6). (b) Type: oxidation (a C–NH3+ became a C=O and NAD+ gained hydrogen); NH4+ is released. Enzyme: glutamate dehydrogenase, an oxidoreductase. Coenzyme: NAD+ → NADH.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

ALT and AST on a lab report. The aminotransferases are intracellular enzymes; they appear in blood only when cells are damaged and leak. ALT is concentrated in liver cells, so a high ALT points to hepatocyte injury (viral hepatitis, alcohol, acetaminophen overdose, fatty liver). AST is also abundant in heart and skeletal muscle, so an isolated AST rise can come from a muscle injury or a strenuous workout rather than the liver; the two are read together. Because the enzymes need vitamin B6 as PLP, a deficient patient (alcohol use disorder, isoniazid therapy for tuberculosis without B6 supplementation) can have a falsely low ALT, and the same deficiency shows up at the dental chair as glossitis (a smooth, sore, red tongue) and angular cheilitis (cracks at the corners of the mouth), because fast-dividing oral tissues feel a coenzyme shortage first. When transamination fails or the liver fails, the nitrogen cannot be handled, and blood ammonia rises (Section 15.10).
