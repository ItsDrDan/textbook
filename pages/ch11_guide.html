Learning Objectives

  • Explain how enzymes speed reactions, using the lock-and-key and induced-fit models.
  • Classify an enzyme into one of the six main classes from the reaction it catalyzes.
  • Read graphs of enzyme activity vs. temperature, pH, and substrate concentration.
  • Distinguish competitive, noncompetitive, and irreversible inhibition.
  • Connect water-soluble vitamins to coenzymes, and know the main roles of vitamins A, D, and K.

Key Ideas

1. What enzymes do

  • Enzymes are protein catalysts. They lower the activation energy, so reactions go faster, but they are not used up and do not change the overall energy of the reaction.
  • The substrate binds in the active site. In the lock-and-key model the site is rigid. In the induced-fit model (more accurate) the site adjusts its shape around the substrate.
  • Names usually end in -ase and tell you the substrate or the reaction: sucrase, lipase, protease, amylase, lactate dehydrogenase.

2. Six enzyme classes

OxidoreductaseOxidation–reduction (moves H or e−)dehydrogenase, oxidase

HydrolaseBreaks bonds by adding waterlipase, protease, sucrase

Professor Tip

Read the enzyme's name like a sentence. "Glucose-6-phosphate isomerase" rearranges glucose-6-phosphate. "Lactate dehydrogenase" removes hydrogen from lactate. The name tells you the class and the substrate, which makes the metabolism chapters much easier.

3. What affects enzyme activity

  • Temperature: activity rises to an optimum (about 37 °C for human enzymes), then falls sharply as the enzyme denatures.
  • pH: each enzyme has an optimum pH (pepsin in the stomach is about 2, trypsin in the intestine is about 8). Far from it, the charges in the active site change.
  • Substrate concentration: rate rises, then levels off (maximum rate) once every active site is busy (saturation).
  • Enzyme concentration: with plenty of substrate, rate rises in direct proportion.

4. Inhibition

  • Competitive: the inhibitor looks like the substrate and binds the active site. It can be overcome by adding more substrate.
  • Noncompetitive: binds elsewhere and changes the shape of the active site. More substrate does not help.
  • Irreversible: forms a covalent bond to the enzyme and permanently inactivates it.

Many enzymes are made as inactive zymogens (pepsinogen, trypsinogen, proinsulin) and activated only where they are needed.

5. Cofactors and vitamins

  • Cofactors are non-protein helpers: metal ions (Zn2+, Mg2+, Fe2+) or organic coenzymes.
  • Many coenzymes are made from water-soluble B vitamins: niacin (B3) → NAD+; riboflavin (B2) → FAD; pantothenic acid (B5) → coenzyme A. You will see all three in metabolism.
  • Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) are stored in fat. A: vision. D: calcium absorption for bones and teeth. E: antioxidant. K: blood clotting.

6. Specificity and regulation

  • Specificity: some enzymes act on one substrate only (absolute), others on a type of bond or group (for example, any peptide bond next to a particular amino acid), and most can tell D from L.
  • Allosteric control: a regulator binds a site other than the active site and changes the enzyme's shape, turning it up (activator) or down (inhibitor).
  • Feedback control: the end product of a pathway inhibits an enzyme near the start, so the cell stops making what it already has enough of.
  • Covalent modification: adding or removing a group, usually a phosphate (by a kinase or a phosphatase), switches an enzyme on or off. Zymogen activation, where part of the chain is cut off, is another example.

Health Career Connection

  • Dental hygiene – salivary amylase: Starch digestion begins in the mouth, where amylase hydrolyzes α(1→4) links. This is why starchy snacks that linger on teeth can still feed acid-producing bacteria.
  • Dental hygiene – how fluoride works: Fluoride does not act mainly on enzymes. Its main anticavity effect is catalytic: it speeds up the regrowth (remineralization) of calcium phosphate crystals in softened enamel. In sodium monofluorophosphate toothpastes, the F− is released by hydrolysis of a P–F bond, which can be chemical or enzyme-catalyzed.
  • Nursing – diagnostic enzymes: Damaged cells leak enzymes into the blood. High troponin and CK-MB point to heart damage, and ALT/AST to liver damage.
  • Nursing – drugs as inhibitors: ACE inhibitors (lisinopril) are competitive inhibitors that lower blood pressure. Penicillin irreversibly inhibits the bacterial enzyme that builds cell walls. Warfarin blocks vitamin K recycling, which slows clotting, so patients on it need monitoring before dental procedures.
  • Dental hygiene – saliva's enzymes: Saliva contains lysozyme, a hydrolase that breaks the polysaccharide backbone of bacterial cell walls, and lactoferrin, an iron-binding protein that limits bacterial growth. Both are part of the mouth's natural defense against plaque.

Further reading: M. Epple and J. Enax, "The Chemistry of Dental Care," ChemistryViews (Part 1, Part 3).

Worked Examples

Example 1: Classify the enzyme

Classify the enzyme for each reaction: (a) lactate + NAD+ → pyruvate + NADH + H+; (b) maltose + H2O → 2 glucose; (c) glucose + ATP → glucose-6-phosphate + ADP.

Solution

(a) H is removed and NAD+ is reduced: oxidoreductase (lactate dehydrogenase). (b) Water splits a bond: hydrolase (maltase). (c) A phosphate moves from ATP to glucose: transferase (hexokinase).

Example 2: Type of inhibition

An inhibitor slows an enzyme, but adding a large excess of substrate restores the normal maximum rate. What type of inhibition is this?

Solution

Competitive. Substrate can out-compete the inhibitor for the active site.

Example 3: Reading the temperature effect

A human enzyme is assayed at 37 °C and then at 65 °C. Predict the activity at 65 °C and explain.

Solution

Activity drops sharply. Above its optimum the enzyme denatures, losing the tertiary structure that forms the active site.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Saying enzymes change ΔH or are consumed. They only lower activation energy.
  • Thinking more substrate overcomes noncompetitive or irreversible inhibition.
  • Mixing up "kinase" (moves a phosphate, transferase) with "phosphatase" (removes a phosphate with water, hydrolase).

Practice

Exercise 1

Classify the enzyme: fructose-6-phosphate → glucose-6-phosphate.

Answer
Isomerase (glucose-6-phosphate isomerase)

Exercise 2

Why does pepsin stop working when stomach contents move into the small intestine?

Answer
The pH rises from about 2 to about 8, far from pepsin's optimum, so the charges in its active site change and it loses activity.

Exercise 3

Which vitamin is the precursor of NAD+? Is it fat- or water-soluble?

Answer
Niacin (vitamin B3); water-soluble

Exercise 4

Why are digestive proteases made as zymogens?

Answer
So they do not digest the proteins of the cells (pancreas, stomach lining) that make them. They are activated only after reaching the digestive tract.

Exercise 5

In a five-step pathway, high levels of the final product slow down the first enzyme. What kind of regulation is this?

Answer
Feedback control (feedback inhibition), usually acting through an allosteric site
