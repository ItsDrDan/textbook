Learning Objectives

  • Describe phosphate esters.
  • Understand why phosphate esters are important in living cells.

Just as carboxylic acids do, inorganic acids such as nitric acid (HNO3), sulfuric acid (H2SO4), and phosphoric acid (H3PO4) also form esters. The esters of phosphoric acid are especially important in biochemistry. A phosphoric acid molecule can form a monoalkyl, a dialkyl, or a trialkyl ester by reaction with one, two, or three molecules of an alcohol.

Esters of pyrophosphoric acid and triphosphoric acid are also important in biochemistry.

Esters of these acids are present in every plant and animal cell. They are biochemical intermediates in the transformation of food into usable energy. The bonds between phosphate units in adenosine triphosphate (ATP) are called phosphoanhydride bonds. Their hydrolysis releases a large amount of energy, so they are often called “high-energy” bonds; Chapter 13 explains where that energy really comes from. ATP is made using energy from the metabolism of foods. Hydrolysis of ATP releases energy as it is needed for biochemical processes (for instance, for muscle contraction). Phosphate esters are also important structural constituents of phospholipids and nucleic acids.

Nitroglycerin

The explosive nitroglycerin (glyceryl trinitrate) is an ester formed from glycerol and nitric acid. It is used in medicine to relieve chest pain in heart disease.

Lecture Connection

This section is a preview, not an exam topic for this chapter. The lecture's point is simply that "ester" is a pattern, acid + alcohol minus water, and that phosphoric acid makes esters too. You will meet phosphate esters constantly in the biochemistry chapters, so learn to recognize the C–O–P link now.

Any acid with an –OH can make an ester

Not every ester is a carboxylic ester: any acid with an –OH can condense with an alcohol. Phosphoric acid, H3PO4, has three –OH groups, so it forms phosphate esters (mono-, di- or triesters, as described above), which you will meet in the sugar phosphates of glycolysis (Chapter 13), the backbone of DNA and RNA (Chapter 12) and the phospholipids of cell membranes (Chapter 9). Recognize the pattern, C–O–P, when it appears; the names and chemistry come later. Nitric acid forms esters the same way, and nitroglycerin, the chest-pain medication the section mentions, is the trinitrate ester of glycerol, not a carboxylic ester at all.

Professor Tip

For this chapter you only need to recognize a phosphate ester in a structure and know that it forms, like any ester, by condensation of an acid –OH with an alcohol –OH. No naming of phosphate esters is asked.

Professor Tip

At body pH the remaining P–OH groups of a phosphate ester are ionized, so biochemists draw them as P–O− and the molecule carries a negative charge (glycerol 3-phosphate above is 2−). That charge is why sugar phosphates cannot leak out of a cell through the membrane, a point that returns in glycolysis.
