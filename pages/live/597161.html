Learning Objectives

  • Define the tertiary structure of proteins and understand the interactions that form and maintain the tertiary structure.

What is the tertiary structure of proteins?

The secondary structure of proteins represents the folding of portions of the polypeptide held primarily by hydrogen bonding between \(\ce{C=O}\) and \(\ce{N-H}\) groups of the polymer backbone. The overall protein chain also folds in a configuration that causes more stabilizing interaction primarily between the groups on the side chains (\(\ce{-R}\)) and between side chain groups and the groups on the polymer backbone.

Tertiary structure

The three-dimensional arrangement of all the atoms of a single polypeptide chain in space, held together by stabilizing interactions between the side chain and the backbone groups, is called the tertiary structure of proteins.

The interactions stabilizing the tertiary structure include disulfide linkage, salt bridge, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic interaction, as shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\) and explained below. (The figure also shows bonds to metal ions, which are not covered in this course.)

Disulfide linkage

When thiol (\(\ce{-SH}\)) groups of two cysteine residues come close to each other in a folded protein chain, they can be oxidized to a disulfide linkage (\(\ce{-S-S{-}}\)). Besides the peptide bond, this is the only covalent bond that holds amino acid residues together in proteins.

\(\ce{R-SH + HS-R' ->[Oxidation] R-S-S-R' + 2H^{+} + 2e^{-}}\)

Salt bridge

At body pH, the side chain of an acidic amino acid such as Asp carries a negative charge, and the side chain of a basic amino acid such as Lys carries a positive charge. When opposite charges come close, they attract each other like the ions in a salt. This ionic attraction is called a salt bridge, for example \(\ce{...-CH2-COO^{-} ^{+}NH3-(CH2)4-...}\) between Asp and Lys residues.

Hydrogen bonding

Hydrogen atoms bonded to oxygen or nitrogen atoms, e.g., in \(\ce{-OH}\) and \(\ce{-NH2}\), can make hydrogen bonds with any oxygen or nitrogen atom. These bonds could be between side chains, backbone groups, or side chains and backbone groups. For example, \(\ce{-OH}\) in the side chain of serine can make a hydrogen bond with the carbonyl oxygen of the amide group (\(\ce{-CONH2}\)) of asparagine residue, as shown below.

Hydrophobic interactions

Amino acids with nonpolar side chains, such as valine and phenylalanine, form hydrophobic interactions, as shown below.

Hydrophobic interactions are attractions (London dispersion forces) between nonpolar groups. The inside of the body is mostly water. When oil is mixed with water, the oil drops merge and push the water away. In the same way, hydrophobic groups in proteins come close to each other due to the hydrophobic interaction and tend to stay in the interior of the protein, expelling water away from those regions. Ionic and polar compounds dissolve in water. Similarly, ionic and polar groups on the side chains stay on the proteins' outer surfaces, interacting more with water.

The tertiary structure of proteins may be predominantly \(\alpha\)-helix, \(\beta\)-pleated sheets, or random coil, but often it is a mix of these, for example, Carboxypeptidase A from bovine pancreas, shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\).

Lecture Connection

Tertiary structure is where the R groups take over. The exam skill is: given two side chains, name the interaction between them. Classify each side chain first (nonpolar, polar neutral, acidic, basic, or cysteine); the pair of classes names the interaction.

The four R-group interactions

Four interactions hold the fold of one chain:

  1. Hydrophobic interactions: nonpolar side chains (Leu, Val, Ile, Phe) cluster in the interior away from water.
  2. Hydrogen bonds between polar side chains (Ser, Thr, Asn, Gln, Tyr) or between a side chain and water on the surface.
  3. Salt bridges, also called ionic bonds or electrostatic attractions, between a − side chain (Asp, Glu: the acid) and a + side chain (Lys, Arg: the base), the result of an acid–base neutralization between the two side chains.
  4. Disulfide bonds (disulfide bridges), covalent –S–S– links formed when two cysteine –SH groups are oxidized (Chapter 4).

Disulfide bonds are covalent; the other three are the weak attractions you met in Chapter 1. That makes the disulfide the strongest of the four and the hardest to undo: it survives heat and alcohol but not a reducing agent. Hydrophobic side chains face the inside of the protein, away from water; hydrophilic ones face the outside. Do not call a disulfide bond a hydrogen bond; it is the only tertiary interaction a reducing agent (a hair perm, for example) can break.

Worked examples

Example 1: Identify the tertiary interaction

Problem. What interaction forms between the side chains of (a) Glu and Arg, (b) Phe and Val, (c) Ser and Asn?

Solution. (a) Glu is − and Arg is +: salt bridge. (b) Both nonpolar: hydrophobic interaction. (c) Both polar with O–H or N–H: hydrogen bond.

Check. Classify each side chain first (acidic, basic, nonpolar, polar neutral); the pair of classes names the interaction. Only two Cys residues would give the fourth type, a disulfide bond.

Example 2: What holds this fold together?

Problem. A small water-soluble enzyme has Cys at positions 14 and 72, Asp at 30, Lys at 95, Thr at 41, Gln at 58, and a cluster of Leu, Ile and Val at positions 20–25 and 80–85. Identify the interaction each group of residues is most likely to form and the level of structure it stabilizes.

Solution. Classify each side chain, then pair up partners.

  • Cys 14 and Cys 72: two thiols can be oxidized to a disulfide bond, a covalent cross-link that pulls two distant parts of the chain together.
  • Asp 30 (−) and Lys 95 (+): a salt bridge (ionic attraction).
  • Thr 41 (–OH) and Gln 58 (amide): a hydrogen bond between polar side chains.
  • The Leu/Ile/Val clusters: hydrophobic interactions; these nonpolar stretches pack together in the interior, away from water.

All four are side-chain (R-group) interactions, so they stabilize tertiary structure. The backbone hydrogen bonds that make the helices and sheets are secondary structure and are not listed here.

Check. Partners that are far apart in the sequence (14 and 72; 30 and 95) can still touch once the chain folds, which is the whole point of tertiary structure. Only the disulfide bond is covalent; it will survive heat and alcohol but not a reducing agent.

Summary

  • Tertiary structure is the overall three-dimensional folding of one polypeptide chain.
  • It is held by four R-group interactions: hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, salt bridges (ionic bonds) and disulfide bonds.
  • In a globular protein, nonpolar side chains pack inside, away from water, and polar and charged side chains face the water.
