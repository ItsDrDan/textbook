Meet the Patient: Rosa’s Story, Part 15 of 15

A sick day, and one year later.

In the winter, Rosa catches a stomach virus and cannot keep food down for two days. Following the written sick-day plan from her care team, she sips fluids, checks her glucose more often and tests her urine with a ketone strip, which shows moderate ketones. Her daughter notices a fruity smell on her breath. Vomiting that will not stop, ketones and fruity breath are warning signs of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), so her plan says to get care right away. At the emergency department a blood test shows that her blood pH is normal: she has ketosis from not eating, not ketoacidosis (Section 15.5). She is given IV fluids, and her nurse practitioner adjusts her medicines until she can eat again.

At a follow-up visit, her lab report includes BUN (blood urea nitrogen) and creatinine to check her kidneys. One year after her deep cleaning, Rosa’s gums bleed much less and her A1c is 7.0%. Studies suggest that treating gum disease can modestly improve blood glucose control, along with the medicines, diet and exercise you have followed through this book.

Keep Rosa in mind as you read. Her questions for this chapter are in the Clinical Update on the Chapter Guide page. Rosa is a fictional patient; her story is not medical advice.

Career Spotlight: Dialysis Nurse

Dialysis nurses care for patients whose kidneys can no longer clear urea, the waste product of the urea cycle. Blood urea nitrogen (BUN), ketones and electrolytes are numbers they watch every treatment.
