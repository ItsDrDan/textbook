The next functional group we consider, the carbonyl group, has a carbon-to-oxygen double bond.

Carbonyl groups define two related families of organic compounds: the aldehydes and the ketones.

The carbonyl group is ubiquitous in biological compounds. It is found in carbohydrates, fats, proteins, nucleic acids, hormones, and vitamins—organic compounds critical to living systems.

The carbon-to-oxygen double bond is not shown but understood to be present. Because they contain the same functional group, aldehydes and ketones share many common properties, but they still differ enough to warrant their classification into two families.

Naming Aldehydes and Ketones

Both common and International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) names are frequently used for aldehydes and ketones, with common names predominating for the lower homologs. The common names of aldehydes are taken from the names of the acids into which the aldehydes can be converted by oxidation.

The stems for the common names of the first four aldehydes are as follows:

  • 1 carbon atom: form-
  • 2 carbon atoms: acet-
  • 3 carbon atoms: propion-
  • 4 carbon atoms: butyr-

Because the carbonyl group in a ketone must be attached to two carbon groups, the simplest ketone has three carbon atoms. It is widely known as acetone, a unique name unrelated to other common names for ketones.

Generally, the common names of ketones consist of the names of the groups attached to the carbonyl group, followed by the word ketone. (Note the similarity to the naming of ethers.) Another name for acetone, then, is dimethyl ketone. The ketone with four carbon atoms is ethyl methyl ketone.

Here are some simple IUPAC rules for naming aldehydes and ketones:

  • The stem names of aldehydes and ketones are derived from those of the parent alkanes, defined by the longest continuous chain (LCC) of carbon atoms that contains the functional group.
  • For an aldehyde, drop the -e from the alkane name and add the ending -al. Methanal is the IUPAC name for formaldehyde, and ethanal is the name for acetaldehyde.
  • For a ketone, drop the -e from the alkane name and add the ending -one. Propanone is the IUPAC name for acetone, and butanone is the name for ethyl methyl ketone.
  • To indicate the position of a substituent on an aldehyde, the carbonyl carbon atom is always considered to be C1; it is unnecessary to designate this group by number.
  • To indicate the position of a substituent on a ketone, number the chain in the manner that gives the carbonyl carbon atom the lowest possible number. In cyclic ketones, it is understood that the carbonyl carbon atom is C1.

Summary

The common names of aldehydes are taken from the names of the corresponding carboxylic acids: formaldehyde, acetaldehyde, and so on. The common names of ketones, like those of ethers, consist of the names of the groups attached to the carbonyl group, followed by the word ketone. Stem names of aldehydes and ketones are derived from those of the parent alkanes, using an -al ending for an aldehyde and an -one ending for a ketone.

Lecture Connection

The lecture's first job with carbonyls is reading: tell an aldehyde (C=O at the end of a chain, written –CHO) from a ketone (C=O inside the chain, –CO–), and tell both from the alcohols and ethers of Chapter 4. Then name them, and when a molecule carries two groups, let the higher-priority group pick the suffix.

Reading condensed formulas: carbon needs 4, oxygen needs 2

Carbon needs four bonds and oxygen needs two. CH3CH2CHO can only be an aldehyde: the last carbon has an H and a C, so two of its four bonds are left, and both go to the O as a double bond. CH3CH2COCH3 is a ketone, not an ether, because the C between the two carbons already has two bonds (one to each carbon); its other two bonds go to the oxygen, and with nothing else written on the O, the oxygen uses both of its bonds on this carbon, so it must be C=O. In a line structure the aldehyde H is sometimes drawn and sometimes not; the C=O at the end of the chain is the clue. The rule that H is written after the atom it is attached to is why an aldehyde is –CHO and never –COH (which would read as an alcohol).

Example: Aldehyde, ketone, alcohol or ether?

Problem. Classify each condensed formula and identify the functional group: (a) CH3CH2CH2CH2CHO; (b) CH3COCH2CH2CH3; (c) CH3CH2OCH2CH3; (d) CH3CH2CH(OH)CH3.

Solution. Count bonds. (a) The final carbon has one C, one H and one O; to reach four bonds the C–O must be a double bond: a C=O with an H on it, an aldehyde (pentanal). (b) "CO" between two carbons: that carbon has two C neighbors and one O, so again the O is double-bonded: a ketone (2-pentanone). (c) The O sits between two carbons with no H; it uses both of its bonds on carbon: an ether (diethyl ether). (d) "OH" on a carbon that also has an H and two carbons: a 2° alcohol (2-butanol).

Check. Formulas (a) and (b) are both C5H10O, so the aldehyde and ketone are functional group isomers; so are the ether and the alcohol (both C4H10O). Only (a) would give a silver mirror with Tollens' reagent (Section 5.4).

Naming aldehydes and ketones

Aldehydes. (1) Find the longest carbon chain containing the CHO group. (2) Change the -e of the parent alkane to -al. (3) The C of the CHO is C1, so it never gets a number. (4) Apply all the other nomenclature rules for substituents. CH3CH(CH3)CH(CH3)CHO is 2,3-dimethylbutanal. Simple aldehydes also have common names ending in -aldehyde: formaldehyde (methanal), acetaldehyde (ethanal). The aldehyde of benzene is benzaldehyde, which is its IUPAC name as well.

Ketones. (1) Find the longest chain containing the C=O. (2) Change the -e to -one. (3) Number the chain to give the C=O the lowest number; include the number unless only one position is possible (propanone, butanone). (4) Apply all other rules. A six-carbon ring with the C=O in it is cyclohexanone; the C=O carbon is C1, and substituents are numbered from there: 3-ethyl-4-methylcyclohexanone. Common names: acetone (propanone), the solvent in nail-polish remover; "alkyl alkyl ketone" names (ethyl methyl ketone) work like ether common names.

Naming molecules with more than one functional group

Many biological molecules carry two or more functional groups: lactic acid is an alcohol and a carboxylic acid; the ketone bodies of Chapter 15 include acetoacetic acid (a ketone and a carboxylic acid) and β-hydroxybutyric acid (an alcohol and a carboxylic acid; it has no ketone group even though it is called a ketone body). IUPAC settles this with one rule: the highest-priority group gets the suffix and decides the numbering (it gets the lowest possible number). Generally, the more oxidized functional group (more C–O bonds) takes priority. Every other, lower-priority group becomes a prefix, listed alphabetically with its own locant alongside the substituent names. In this chapter the decisions you will face most are aldehyde vs. ketone (aldehyde wins; the ketone becomes oxo-), carbonyl vs. alcohol (carbonyl wins; the alcohol becomes hydroxy-), and carbonyl vs. ether or halogen (ethers and halogens are always prefixes). Examples: CH3COCH2CH2OH is 4-hydroxy-2-butanone (ketone outranks alcohol, so the chain is numbered from the end nearer the C=O). CH3CH(OCH3)CH2CHO is 3-methoxybutanal. The Chapter Guide works two more of these in its Worked Examples.

Professor Tip

Two numbering traps. An aldehyde never gets a locant (butanal, not 1-butanal), because its carbon is always C1. A ketone with a lower-priority group is still numbered from the end nearer the C=O: 4-hydroxy-2-pentanone, never 2-hydroxy-4-pentanone. Decide the suffix first, then number.
