Multiple Choice

Problem 1.1

How many lone pairs are on the nitrogen atom of a neutral amine such as CH3NH2?

  1. 0
  2. 1
  3. 2
  4. 3
Answer
B. Neutral N makes 3 bonds and has 1 lone pair.

Problem 1.2

Which atom in this list would carry a positive formal charge?

  1. O with 2 bonds and 2 lone pairs
  2. N with 4 bonds and 0 lone pairs
  3. C with 4 bonds and 0 lone pairs
  4. Cl with 1 bond and 3 lone pairs
Answer
B. Nitrogen with one more bond than usual (4) and no lone pair is positive, as in NH4+.

Problem 1.3

What is the shape around the oxygen atom in methanol, CH3OH?

  1. Linear
  2. Trigonal planar
  3. Bent
  4. Tetrahedral
Answer
C. Oxygen has 2 bonds + 2 lone pairs (4 electron groups), so the atoms form a bent shape (~105°).

Problem 1.4

What is the ideal bond angle around the carbonyl carbon in methanal, H2C=O?

  1. 90°
  2. 109.5°
  3. 120°
  4. 180°
Answer
C. Three electron groups give trigonal planar geometry, 120°.

Problem 1.5

Which molecule is nonpolar even though it contains polar bonds?

  1. CH2Cl2
  2. CO2
  3. NH3
  4. CH3F
Answer
B. CO2 is linear, so its two C=O dipoles cancel.

Problem 1.6

Which pure substance has hydrogen bonding between its molecules?

  1. CH3CH2F
  2. CH3OCH3
  3. CH3CH2NH2
  4. CH3CH2CH3
Answer
C. Only it has an H bonded directly to N, O, or F.

Problem 1.7

Which has the highest boiling point?

  1. CH4
  2. C2H6
  3. C3H8
  4. C4H10
Answer
D. All are nonpolar; the largest molecule has the strongest dispersion forces.

True or False? (If false, correct it.)

Problem 1.8

When water boils, O–H covalent bonds inside the water molecules are broken.

Answer
False. Boiling breaks only the hydrogen bonds between molecules; the O–H covalent bonds stay intact.

Problem 1.9

Dispersion forces are present between all molecules, polar or nonpolar.

Answer
True. Every molecule has electrons that can shift to form temporary dipoles, so dispersion forces act between all molecules; polar molecules simply have dipole–dipole forces as well.

Matching

Problem 1.10

Match each substance with the strongest intermolecular force between its molecules: (1) Br2 (2) CH3Cl (3) CH3OH. Choices: hydrogen bonding, dipole–dipole, dispersion.

Answer
(1) dispersion; (2) dipole–dipole; (3) hydrogen bonding

Short Answer

Problem 1.11

Draw the Lewis structure of formaldehyde, CH2O. How many valence electrons does it have, and how many lone pairs are on O?

Answer
12 valence electrons (4 + 2 + 6). C is double-bonded to O and single-bonded to two H. Oxygen has 2 lone pairs.

Problem 1.12

Explain why 1-propanol (bp 97 °C) boils far higher than its isomer ethyl methyl ether (bp 7 °C).

Answer
1-Propanol has an O–H group, so its molecules hydrogen bond with one another. The ether has no O–H and only has dipole–dipole and dispersion forces, which are much weaker.

More Practice: Analyze and Compare

Problem 1.52

In a line (skeletal) structure, which statement is true?

  1. Every corner of the zigzag is a hydrogen atom.
  2. Every corner and every end of a line is a carbon atom with enough hidden hydrogens to make four bonds.
  3. Oxygen and nitrogen atoms are left out, just like carbon.
  4. Lone pairs must always be drawn on every oxygen.
Answer
B. Line structures hide carbons and their hydrogens, but every corner or line end is a carbon that needs four bonds. Atoms other than C and H (and any hydrogens on them) are always written in, and lone pairs are normally omitted.

Problem 1.53

Which functional group is present in CH3CH2COCH3?

  1. Aldehyde
  2. Ketone
  3. Ester
  4. Ether
Answer
B. The C=O (written CO in a condensed formula) has a carbon on each side, CH3CH2 and CH3, so it is a ketone. An aldehyde would have an H on the carbonyl carbon, an ester an O–R, and an ether has no C=O at all.

Problem 1.54

Which compound is most soluble in water?

  1. CH3(CH2)5CH3
  2. CH3CH2OCH2CH3
  3. CH3CH2CH2OH
  4. CH3CH2CH2Cl
Answer
C. Propan-1-ol has an O–H that both donates and accepts hydrogen bonds with water, and only three carbons of nonpolar chain. The ether (B) can only accept a hydrogen bond, the chloroalkane (D) is polar but cannot hydrogen bond, and heptane (A) is completely nonpolar.

Problem 1.55

In the thiolate ion CH3S−, the sulfur has 1 bond and 3 lone pairs. Sulfur is in the same group as oxygen. What charge does sulfur carry, and why?

  1. +1, because it has an extra lone pair
  2. 0, because sulfur normally makes one bond
  3. −1, because it has one fewer bond and one more lone pair than a neutral group 16 atom
  4. −2, because it has three lone pairs
Answer
C. A neutral group 16 atom (O or S) makes 2 bonds and has 2 lone pairs. One fewer bond and one extra lone pair means one extra electron, so the charge is −1, exactly like the oxygen in CH3O−.

Problem 1.56

The table lists four compounds of similar molar mass with their boiling points. Use it to answer the questions that follow.

Molar masses and boiling points of four compounds (for the questions below)

ButaneCH3CH2CH2CH358−1
PropanalCH3CH2CHO5848
Propan-1-olCH3CH2CH2OH6097
Ethane-1,2-diol (ethylene glycol)HOCH2CH2OH62197

(a) Name the strongest IMF for each compound. (b) Propan-1-ol and ethylene glycol both hydrogen bond, yet ethylene glycol boils 100 °C higher. Explain. (c) Why does ethylene glycol (antifreeze) make a poor skin antiseptic compared with rubbing alcohol (isopropyl alcohol, bp 82 °C)? Use the table.

Answer
(a) Butane: dispersion only. Propanal: dipole–dipole (polar C=O, no O–H). Propan-1-ol: hydrogen bonding (one O–H). Ethylene glycol: hydrogen bonding (two O–H). Problem 1.57

Lidocaine, the most common dental local anesthetic, is shown as a line structure. (a) How many carbon atoms does it contain? (b) Identify every functional group. (c) Give the shape around the carbonyl carbon and around the nitrogen that carries two ethyl groups. (d) Can lidocaine donate a hydrogen bond to water? Accept one?

Answer
(a) 14 carbons: 6 in the ring, 2 methyl groups on the ring, the carbonyl carbon, the CH2 next to it, and 2 ethyl groups (2 carbons each) on the far nitrogen. (b) An aromatic ring; an amide (the C=O bonded to N–H); and an amine (the nitrogen bonded to three carbons, with no C=O next to it). The two methyl and two ethyl groups are alkane-type fragments. (c) The carbonyl carbon has three electron groups (C=O, N, CH2), so it is trigonal planar, 120°. The amine nitrogen has three bonds and one lone pair, so it is trigonal pyramidal, about 107°. (d) Yes to both: the N–H of the amide can donate a hydrogen bond, and the C=O oxygen and the amine nitrogen can accept one (the amide nitrogen’s lone pair is shared with the C=O, so it is a poor acceptor).

Problem 1.58

Butan-1-ol, CH3CH2CH2CH2OH, and butane-1,4-diol, HOCH2CH2CH2CH2OH, have the same carbon chain. Compare the two: what do they share, how do they differ, and which one do you predict has the higher boiling point and the higher water solubility?

Answer
Both are four-carbon alcohols with the same dispersion forces (same chain) and both hydrogen bond. The diol has a second O–H, which doubles the number of hydrogen bonds each molecule can form with neighbors and with water. Prediction: butane-1,4-diol has the higher boiling point (230 °C versus 118 °C) and the higher water solubility (it mixes with water in all proportions, while butan-1-ol dissolves only about 7 g per 100 mL). Adding polar O–H groups to a fixed carbon skeleton always pushes both properties up.

Problem 1.59

Acetone (propanone, CH3COCH3, bp 56 °C) and isopropyl alcohol (propan-2-ol, CH3CH(OH)CH3, bp 82 °C) are both three-carbon liquids found in a clinic. Predict which one evaporates faster from skin, and explain the prediction using functional groups, shapes, and IMFs.

Answer
Acetone evaporates faster. Both molecules are polar, but the ketone's C=O gives only dipole–dipole forces (its hydrogens are all on carbon), whereas the alcohol's O–H forms hydrogen bonds. Weaker attractions between acetone molecules mean they escape into the vapor more easily, which the lower boiling point confirms. Shape supports the same conclusion: the carbonyl carbon is trigonal planar with the polar C=O exposed, but no hydrogen on oxygen is available to bridge molecules the way the bent C–O–H of propan-2-ol does.

More Practice: Clinical and Integrative

Problem 1.60

A patient who is NPO (nothing by mouth) receives an IV infusion of D5W, 5% dextrose (glucose, C6H12O6) in water. Open-chain glucose has five O–H groups and one aldehyde group. (a) Which IMFs let glucose dissolve so readily in water? (b) A nurse would never try to dissolve a fat-based drug directly in D5W. Use "like dissolves like" to explain why.

Answer
(a) Each O–H group on glucose can donate and accept hydrogen bonds with water, and the C=O of the aldehyde can accept them, so glucose is surrounded by a shell of hydrogen-bonded water molecules. With six oxygens on a six-carbon molecule, the polar groups dominate. (b) A fat-based drug is mostly long nonpolar hydrocarbon chains that interact only through dispersion forces. Water molecules hold on to each other with hydrogen bonds far more strongly than they can attract a nonpolar molecule, so the drug separates out instead of dissolving. Nonpolar drugs need a lipid-based carrier (an emulsion) rather than a sugar solution.

Problem 1.61

In diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), the body makes large amounts of acetone, and a nurse may notice a fruity odor on the patient's breath. (a) Draw or write the condensed formula of acetone and name its functional group. (b) Give the shape and bond angle around its central carbon. (c) Explain, using IMFs, why acetone escapes into exhaled air easily enough to be smelled while glucose, which is also elevated in DKA, never shows up on the breath.

Answer
(a) CH3COCH3; a ketone (C=O with a carbon on each side). (b) Three electron groups: trigonal planar, 120°. (c) Acetone is a small polar molecule held together only by dipole–dipole and dispersion forces, with no O–H or N–H to form hydrogen bonds, so it is volatile (bp 56 °C) and readily passes from blood into the air in the lungs. Glucose carries five O–H groups that hydrogen bond strongly with water and with each other, so it stays dissolved in blood and is essentially nonvolatile.

Problem 1.62

Eugenol, the active component of clove oil, is mixed with zinc oxide to make a temporary dental cement and is shown as a line structure. (a) Identify all of its functional groups. (b) Count its carbons and give its molecular formula. (c) Eugenol dissolves poorly in water but mixes well with oils. Explain using its structure. (d) Sodium fluoride varnish, by contrast, dissolves easily in saliva. What kind of bonding holds NaF together, and how does that differ from the bonding in eugenol?

Answer
(a) An aromatic ring; a phenol (O–H directly on the ring); an ether (the –OCH3 group, oxygen between two carbons); and an alkene (the C=C at the end of the three-carbon chain). (b) 10 carbons: 6 in the ring, 1 in the OCH3, and 3 in the side chain. Adding hidden hydrogens gives C10H12O2. (c) Only one O–H can hydrogen bond with water; the rest of the molecule is a nonpolar ring, a hydrocarbon chain, and an ether oxygen that cannot donate a hydrogen bond. Dispersion forces with oily, nonpolar surroundings win, so eugenol stays put in the cement rather than washing away in saliva. (d) NaF is an ionic compound: Na+ and F− ions held in a lattice by ionic bonds, not a molecule at all. Polar water molecules surround and separate the ions (ion–dipole attraction), so it dissolves. Eugenol is a covalent molecule whose atoms share electrons, and its weak IMFs with water cannot compete with water's hydrogen bonding.

Problem 1.63

Ibuprofen (shown) and acetaminophen (Example 6 in the chapter guide) are both over-the-counter pain relievers. (a) Identify the functional groups in ibuprofen. (b) Which of the two drugs do you predict is more soluble in water? Justify your answer by comparing the polar groups and the nonpolar hydrocarbon portion of each molecule. (c) Which chapter of this book will teach you how the carboxylic acid group reacts, and which will teach you about the amide?

Answer
(a) An aromatic ring and a carboxylic acid (C=O with an O–H on the same carbon). The branched four-carbon chain at the far end of the ring and the methyl group next to the acid are alkane-type fragments. (b) Acetaminophen. Ibuprofen has 13 carbons and only one polar group (the COOH), so most of its surface is nonpolar and interacts with water only by dispersion. Acetaminophen has 8 carbons and two polar, hydrogen-bonding groups (phenol O–H and amide N–H/C=O), so a larger fraction of the molecule can hydrogen bond with water. (Measured solubilities agree: roughly 14 g/L for acetaminophen versus about 0.02 g/L for ibuprofen.) (c) Carboxylic acids are covered in Chapter 6; amides in Chapter 7.

Problem 1.64

Stearic acid, CH3(CH2)16COOH, is a solid at room temperature (mp 69 °C). Oleic acid has the same 18 carbons but contains one C=C in the middle of the chain, and it is a liquid (mp 13 °C). Both are fatty acids you will meet in Chapter 9. (a) What is the shape and bond angle around each carbon of the C=C, and how does that change the overall shape of the chain? (b) Use IMFs to explain why the double bond lowers the melting point so much even though the two molecules have the same functional group and almost the same molar mass.

Answer
(a) Each carbon of the C=C has three electron groups, so it is trigonal planar with 120° angles, while every other chain carbon is tetrahedral (109.5°). The double bond cannot rotate, and in oleic acid it is cis, so it puts a rigid bend in what is otherwise a flexible zigzag chain (a trans double bond would leave the chain nearly straight). (b) Melting point depends on how well molecules pack. Straight stearic acid chains line up side by side and touch along their whole length, giving strong dispersion forces. The kinked oleic acid chains cannot nestle together as closely, so there is less surface contact, weaker dispersion forces, and less energy is needed to melt the solid. This is the same reasoning used for branched versus straight alkanes, and it is why oils rich in unsaturated fatty acids are liquids while saturated fats are solids.

More Practice: Structures, Functional Groups, and Organic versus Inorganic

Problem 1.65

Which statement about organic compounds is correct?

  1. They are held together by ionic bonds and have high melting points.
  2. They exist as discrete molecules held together by covalent bonds and generally have low melting and boiling points.
  3. They separate into ions when they dissolve in water.
  4. They are always solids at room temperature.
Answer
B. Nearly every organic compound is molecular: its atoms share electrons in covalent bonds. Only weak intermolecular forces hold the molecules to one another, so melting and boiling points are low compared with ionic compounds, and many organic compounds are liquids or gases at room temperature. Most molecular compounds do not ionize in water (C); the acids and amines of Chapters 6 and 7 are the exceptions. Ionic bonding (A) describes salts such as NaCl.

Problem 1.66

True or false? Table salt (NaCl, melting point 801 °C) melts so much higher than propan-1-ol (melting point −126 °C) because NaCl is inorganic and propan-1-ol is organic. If false, correct the statement.

Answer
False as explained. The real difference is ionic versus molecular. NaCl is a lattice of Na+ and Cl− ions held by strong electrostatic attractions, and melting it means pulling those ions apart. Propan-1-ol is a molecular compound; melting it only breaks the hydrogen bonds and dispersion forces between molecules, which are far weaker than ionic bonds. "Organic" happens to mean molecular, but inorganic molecular compounds such as water and CO2 also melt at low temperatures.

Problem 1.67

Which atom is most likely to form a polar covalent bond with carbon?

  1. H
  2. C
  3. S
  4. N
Answer
D. Bond polarity depends on the electronegativity difference. Carbon and hydrogen are close enough that C–H is treated as nonpolar, C–C has no difference at all, and sulfur is nearly the same as carbon. Nitrogen is much more electronegative than carbon, so C–N is polar with the δ− on N (C–O and C–F would be more polar still).

Problem 1.68

Match each general formula with its functional group: (1) R–SH (2) R–CHO (3) R–NH2 (4) R–COOR′ (5) R–X (X = Cl, Br, I). Choices: amine, haloalkane, thiol, ester, aldehyde.

Answer
(1) thiol, an S–H on carbon; (2) aldehyde, a carbonyl with an H on the carbonyl carbon; (3) amine, a nitrogen bonded only to carbon and hydrogen; (4) ester, a carbonyl whose carbon also carries an O–R′; (5) haloalkane, a halogen on carbon. R and R′ stand for any carbon chain or ring.

Problem 1.69

Which feature is most important in deciding what chemical reactions an organic molecule will undergo?

  1. Its molar mass
  2. The number of C–H bonds it contains
  3. Its functional groups
  4. Whether its longest chain is branched
Answer
C. A functional group (a heteroatom, a multiple bond, or both) undergoes the same reactions in every molecule that contains it, so the functional groups dictate the chemistry. Molar mass, branching, and the number of C–H bonds influence physical properties such as boiling point, not which reactions occur.

Problem 1.70

Each of the following is a proposed Lewis structure for a neutral molecule. Find the error in each. (a) Chloromethane drawn with carbon bonded to three H and one Cl, and the Cl carrying two lone pairs. (b) Dimethyl ether, CH3OCH3, drawn with the oxygen holding two bonds and three lone pairs. (c) Ethene drawn as H2C–CH2 with only a single bond between the carbons and no lone pairs anywhere. (d) Water drawn with one of the hydrogens bonded to both the oxygen and the other hydrogen.

Answer
(a) Chlorine's octet is not satisfied: with one bond and two lone pairs it has only 6 electrons. A neutral halogen has 1 bond and 3 lone pairs. (b) Too many electrons: the structure has 2(4) + 6(1) + 6 = 20 valence electrons available, but eight bonds plus three lone pairs uses 22. An oxygen with 2 bonds and 3 lone pairs would also have 10 electrons around it, breaking the octet. Oxygen needs 2 lone pairs. (c) Each carbon has only three bonds (6 electrons), so neither has an octet; the 12 available electrons are also not all used. The carbons must share a double bond: H2C=CH2. (d) Hydrogen can never form two bonds; it holds a maximum of 2 electrons (a duet). Each H bonds only to the oxygen.

Problem 1.71

(a) Condense CH3CH2CH2CH2CH2CH2Br further by collecting the repeated CH2 groups in parentheses. (b) Write out CH3(CH2)3CH(CH3)2 with every carbon shown, and give its molecular formula. (c) A student writes (CH2)4O for the five-membered ring that has four CH2 groups and one oxygen (tetrahydrofuran, a solvent). What is wrong with that formula?

Answer
(a) CH3(CH2)5Br. Five identical CH2 units in a row are written once with a subscript. (b) CH3CH2CH2CH2CH(CH3)CH3. The CH at the end carries two methyl groups, one written in parentheses and one as the final CH3. Seven carbons and 3 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 1 + 3 + 3 = 16 hydrogens: C7H16. (c) It hides the ring. "(CH2)4O" reads as a four-carbon chain with an oxygen on one end, which would need an O–H to be a real molecule. The whole point of a structural formula is to show how atoms are connected, so a ring must be drawn as a polygon with the O at one corner.

Problem 1.72

Sketch (or describe in words) each intermolecular attraction, drawing at least two molecules and a dashed line where the attraction occurs: (a) hydrogen bonding between two molecules of ethanol, CH3CH2OH; (b) dipole–dipole attraction between two molecules of chloromethane, CH3Cl; (c) dispersion forces between two molecules of pentane.

Answer
(a) Draw two ethanol molecules so that the H of one O–H points at a lone pair on the oxygen of the other; the dashed line runs from that H to the O (H–O ··· H–O). The donor is the O–H, the acceptor is the lone pair on the neighboring O. (b) Mark the C–Cl bond of each chloromethane with δ+ on carbon and δ− on chlorine, then line the molecules up head to tail so the δ− Cl of one sits next to the δ+ C of the other, with the dashed line between those two atoms. No hydrogen bond is possible because every H is on carbon. (c) Draw two pentane zigzags lying side by side and put dashed lines between the chains along their length. Pentane is nonpolar, so the only attraction is the momentary dipole in one chain inducing a dipole in its neighbor; the more surface the chains share, the stronger the dispersion.

Problem 1.73

Rank from most to least soluble in water: octane, methanol, 1-octanol, 1-pentanol. Explain using intermolecular forces between solute and solvent.

Answer
Methanol > 1-pentanol > 1-octanol > octane. Every alcohol has an O–H that hydrogen bonds with water, but the nonpolar hydrocarbon chain attached to it interacts with water only by weak dispersion forces, so as the chain grows the molecule becomes more "like" oil and less "like" water. Methanol (one carbon) mixes with water in all proportions, 1-pentanol dissolves only a few grams per 100 mL, and 1-octanol barely dissolves. Octane has no polar group at all and is insoluble.

Problem 1.74

Using atomic masses C = 12.01, H = 1.008, N = 14.01, and O = 16.00 g/mol, calculate the molar mass of (a) isopropyl alcohol, C3H8O, and (b) acetaminophen, C8H9NO2.

Answer

Problem 1.75

Problem 1.76

Vitamin B5 (pantothenic acid) and aspartame (the sweetener in sugar-free gum) are both drawn with an amide group. A student circles the nitrogen of each amide a second time and labels it "amine." (a) Why is that double-counting wrong? (b) Aspartame also contains a –COOCH3 group. A student labels the oxygen between the carbonyl carbon and the CH3 as an "ether." Answer
(a) In an amide the nitrogen is attached directly to the carbonyl carbon, and the nitrogen's lone pair is shared with the C=O, so the two behave as a single unit with its own reactivity (Chapter 7). An amine is a nitrogen that is not next to a C=O. Each atom belongs to exactly one functional group. (b) It is part of an ester: a carbonyl whose carbon carries an O–R. Problem 1.78

Answer
The student confused polar bonds with a polar molecule. Each C=O bond is polar, but CO2 is linear (two electron groups on C, 180°), so the two bond dipoles point in opposite directions and cancel.
