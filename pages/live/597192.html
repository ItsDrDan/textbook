Learning Objectives

  • Describe the function of glycolysis and identify its major products.
  • Describe how the presence or absence of oxygen determines what happens to the pyruvate and the NADH that are produced in glycolysis.
  • Determine the amount of ATP produced by the oxidation of glucose in the presence and absence of oxygen.

ATP numbers in this course

Older sources count 3 ATP per NADH and 2 per FADH2 (36–38 ATP per glucose). This course uses 2.5 ATP per NADH and 1.5 per FADH2: 10 ATP per acetyl CoA and 30–32 ATP per glucose (32 with the malate–aspartate shuttle, 30 with the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle). Use the course values on exams.

In stage II of catabolism, the metabolic pathway known as glycolysis converts glucose into two molecules of pyruvate (a three-carbon compound) with the corresponding production of adenosine triphosphate (ATP). The individual reactions in glycolysis were determined during the first part of the 20th century. It was the first metabolic pathway to be elucidated, in part because the participating enzymes are found in soluble form in the cell and are readily isolated and purified. The pathway is structured so that the product of one enzyme-catalyzed reaction becomes the substrate of the next. The transfer of intermediates from one enzyme to the next occurs by diffusion.

Steps in Glycolysis

The 10 reactions of glycolysis fall into two phases. In phase I (steps 1–5), glucose is phosphorylated twice, using 2 ATP, and split into two three-carbon molecules of glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate. In phase II (steps 6–10), each glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate is oxidized (making NADH) and converted to pyruvate, making 2 ATP; because there are two of them, phase II makes 4 ATP and 2 NADH per glucose. The net result is 2 ATP, 2 NADH and 2 pyruvate. Steps 7 and 10 make ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation: a phosphate is passed directly from a high-energy molecule to ADP, unlike the oxidative phosphorylation of Section 14.4. The ten steps, with their structures, enzymes, reaction types and coenzymes, are taught in Section 13.5.

To Your Health: Diabetes

Although medical science has made significant progress against diabetes, it continues to be a major health threat. Some of the serious complications of diabetes are as follows:

  • It is the leading cause of lower-limb amputations not caused by injury in the United States.
  • It is a leading cause of blindness in working-age adults.
  • It is the leading cause of kidney failure.
  • It increases the risk of having a heart attack or stroke by two to four times.

Because a person with diabetes is unable to use glucose properly, excessive quantities accumulate in the blood and the urine. Other characteristic symptoms are constant hunger, weight loss, extreme thirst, and frequent urination because the kidneys excrete large amounts of water in an attempt to remove excess sugar from the blood.

There are two main types of diabetes. In type 1 diabetes, the body’s immune system destroys the insulin-making β cells of the pancreas, so little or no insulin is made. It is most often found in children and young adults, but it can start at any age. People with type 1 diabetes need insulin every day for life, given by injection or by an insulin pump. Insulin is a protein, so it would be digested in the stomach and small intestine if it were swallowed.

A blood test for the antibodies that attack the β cells can show who is at high risk of type 1 diabetes, sometimes years before symptoms begin.

Type 2 diabetes is by far the more common type: about 90–95% of people with diabetes have type 2. (Current CDC estimates are about 38 million Americans with diabetes; Section 13.9 gives the figures for Valley counties.) In type 2 diabetes the body still makes insulin, but muscle, fat, and liver cells respond poorly to it (insulin resistance), and over time the pancreas cannot make enough extra insulin to keep up. It usually develops slowly in adults, but it is now also seen in teenagers and children. Healthy eating, regular physical activity, and weight loss (for people who are overweight) lower blood glucose, and for some people they are enough on their own.

Many people with type 2 diabetes also take medicine. Metformin, usually the first drug used, lowers the amount of glucose the liver releases and helps cells respond better to insulin (Section 13.8). Sulfonylureas, such as glyburide (shown above), make the pancreas release more insulin; because they push insulin out even when the person has not eaten, they can cause low blood glucose. GLP-1 receptor agonists (for example, semaglutide) increase insulin release after meals, slow stomach emptying, and reduce appetite. SGLT2 inhibitors make the kidneys excrete more glucose in the urine. Many people with type 2 diabetes eventually need insulin as well. In every type of diabetes, blood glucose is checked often and diet and medicine are adjusted to keep it as close to normal as is safe. A normal fasting blood glucose is 70–99 mg/dL (Section 13.9).

Metabolism of Pyruvate

The presence or absence of oxygen determines the fates of the pyruvate and the NADH produced in glycolysis. When plenty of oxygen is available, pyruvate is completely oxidized to carbon dioxide, with the release of much greater amounts of ATP through the combined actions of the citric acid cycle, the electron transport chain, and oxidative phosphorylation. However, in the absence of oxygen (that is, under anaerobic conditions), the fate of pyruvate is different in different organisms. In vertebrates, pyruvate is converted to lactate, while other organisms, such as yeast, convert pyruvate to ethanol and carbon dioxide. These possible fates of pyruvate are summarized in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\). The conversion to lactate or ethanol under anaerobic conditions allows for the reoxidation of NADH to NAD+ in the absence of oxygen.

ATP Yield from Glycolysis

The net energy yield from anaerobic glucose metabolism can readily be calculated in moles of ATP. In the initial phosphorylation of glucose (step 1), 1 mol of ATP is expended, along with another in the phosphorylation of fructose 6-phosphate (step 3). In step 7, 2 mol of BPG (recall that 2 mol of 1,3-BPG are formed for each mole of glucose) are converted to 2 mol of 3-phosphoglycerate, and 2 mol of ATP are produced. In step 10, 2 mol of pyruvate and 2 mol of ATP are formed per mole of glucose.

For every mole of glucose degraded, 2 mol of ATP are initially consumed and 4 mol of ATP are ultimately produced. The net production of ATP is thus 2 mol for each mole of glucose converted to lactate or ethanol. Each mole of ATP stores 7.3 kcal, and complete oxidation of 1 mol of glucose can release about 670 kcal. The energy conserved when glucose is broken down anaerobically to two lactate (or two ethanol) is therefore:

\[\mathrm{\dfrac{2\times 7.3\: kcal}{670\: kcal}\times100=2.2\%} \nonumber \]

Thus anaerobic cells extract only a very small fraction of the total energy of the glucose molecule.

Contrast this result with the amount of energy obtained when glucose is completely oxidized to carbon dioxide and water through glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, the electron transport chain, and oxidative phosphorylation as summarized in Table \(\PageIndex{1}\), which uses this course’s values of 2.5 ATP per NADH and 1.5 ATP per FADH2. Note the indication in the table that a variable amount of ATP is made, depending on the tissue, from the NADH formed in the cytosol during glycolysis. This is because NADH cannot cross the inner mitochondrial membrane, where the enzymes of the electron transport chain are located. Instead, brain and muscle cells use the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle, which passes the electrons from cytosolic NADH to FAD inside the mitochondria, forming FADH2; this route yields only 1.5 ATP per NADH, so a glucose oxidized in muscle or brain gives 30 ATP. Liver, heart, and kidney cells use the more efficient malate–aspartate shuttle, in which each cytosolic NADH gives one mitochondrial NADH and 2.5 ATP, so a glucose oxidized in the liver gives 32 ATP. (Older books use 3 ATP per NADH and 2 per FADH2 and report 36–38 ATP per glucose.) The total amount of energy conserved in the aerobic catabolism of glucose in the liver is as follows:

\[\mathrm{\dfrac{32\times7.3\: kcal}{670\: kcal}\times100=35\%} \nonumber \]

Conservation of about 35% (roughly one-third) of the total energy released, with the rest leaving as body heat, compares favorably with the efficiency of any machine. In comparison, automobiles are only about 20%–25% efficient in using the energy released by the combustion of gasoline.

The other 65% of the released energy is not conserved. It enters the surroundings (that is, the cell) as heat that helps to maintain body temperature. If we are exercising strenuously and our metabolism speeds up to provide the energy needed for muscle contraction, more heat is produced. We begin to perspire to dissipate some of that heat. As the perspiration evaporates, the excess heat is carried away from the body by the departing water vapor.

Summary

  • The monosaccharide glucose is broken down through a series of enzyme-catalyzed reactions known as glycolysis.
  • For each molecule of glucose that is broken down, two molecules of pyruvate, two molecules of ATP, and two molecules of NADH are produced.
  • In the absence of oxygen, pyruvate is converted to lactate, and NADH is reoxidized to NAD+. In the presence of oxygen, pyruvate is converted to acetyl-CoA and then enters the citric acid cycle.
  • More ATP can be formed from the breakdown of glucose when oxygen is present.

Lecture Connection

Two lecture rules sit on top of this section. First, glycolysis is an anaerobic pathway in the cytosol: no molecular oxygen is used and no CO2 is released, so its net yield is always 2 ATP and 2 NADH per glucose. Second, pyruvate has three pathways, and which one runs depends on whether oxygen is available. The step-by-step chemistry of the ten reactions is on the next page (Section 13.5); here we settle the bookkeeping.

Three fates of pyruvate

Glycolysis leaves a cell with two problems: pyruvate still holds most of the energy of glucose, and the NADH made in step 6 must be turned back into NAD+ or step 6 stops and glycolysis stalls. Oxygen decides how both problems are solved.

  • Aerobic conditions (oxygen present, cell has mitochondria): three-carbon pyruvate is oxidized and decarboxylated to two-carbon acetyl CoA + CO2 by the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex inside the mitochondrion. One carbon leaves as CO2, NAD+ is reduced to NADH, and the acetyl group is attached to coenzyme A as a thioester. The enzyme is an oxidoreductase that also needs a coenzyme made from thiamine (vitamin B1). Acetyl CoA then enters the citric acid cycle, and the NADH from glycolysis is re-oxidized by the electron transport chain (Chapter 14). This step is also called oxidative decarboxylation, or the link reaction, because it links glycolysis to the citric acid cycle.
  • Anaerobic conditions in humans (strenuous exercise, when not enough O2 is inhaled to re-oxidize NADH; red blood cells, which have no mitochondria): lactate dehydrogenase (an oxidoreductase) reduces pyruvate to lactate, using NADH + H+ and regenerating NAD+. The ketone C=O of pyruvate becomes the 2° alcohol C–OH of lactate. The net gain is still only 2 ATP per glucose, but it is fast and needs no oxygen. Lactate builds up in muscle during intense exercise along with H+, and the H+ contributes to the burning feeling and fatigue; heavy breathing after the exercise repays this oxygen debt, so lactate can be re-oxidized to pyruvate, and lactate also travels in the blood to the liver, which rebuilds it into glucose (the Cori cycle, Section 13.8). The soreness felt a day or two later comes from small injuries to the muscle fibers, not from lactate.
  • Anaerobic fermentation in yeast and other microorganisms: pyruvate is decarboxylated to acetaldehyde, and acetaldehyde is reduced to ethanol, regenerating NAD+. The CO2 raises bread and carbonates beer. Humans do not have pyruvate decarboxylase, so in humans anaerobic conditions lead to lactate, never ethanol.

Practice (Cori cycle): Lactate is converted back to pyruvate in the liver. What reaction is taking place, what class of enzyme is needed, and is a coenzyme needed?

Answer
An oxidation: the 2° alcohol C–OH of lactate becomes the ketone C=O of pyruvate (more C–O bonds). The enzyme is lactate dehydrogenase, an oxidoreductase, now running in the opposite direction from muscle. Yes, a coenzyme: NAD+ is the oxidizing agent and is reduced to NADH + H+.

Keep the NAD+ bookkeeping straight: in a red blood cell, glycolysis makes 2 NADH per glucose and lactate dehydrogenase uses 2 NADH per glucose, so the cell’s net products are 2 ATP and 2 lactate with no NADH left over. In a muscle cell with enough oxygen, the same 2 NADH are instead cashed in for ATP in the mitochondria.

Overall, per glucose. With oxygen, glycolysis and the link reaction give 2 acetyl CoA, 2 CO2, 2 ATP and 4 NADH (2 from glycolysis, 2 from pyruvate dehydrogenase), and the acetyl CoA goes on to the citric acid cycle. In human cells without oxygen, glucose gives 2 lactate and a net 2 ATP. In yeast, glucose gives 2 ethanol, 2 CO2 and a net 2 ATP.

Example 1: Fate of pyruvate

Problem. During an all-out 400 m sprint, what happens to most of the pyruvate in leg muscle, and why?

Solution. O2 supply cannot keep up, so pyruvate is reduced to lactate. This step regenerates NAD+, which glycolysis needs to keep producing ATP.

Check: Lactate dehydrogenase is an oxidoreductase running in the reduction direction: pyruvate’s C=O becomes lactate’s C–OH while NADH becomes NAD+.

Example 2: A lactate result

Problem. A patient arriving with a severe infection has a serum lactate of 4.8 mmol/L (reference < 2). Explain in chemical terms where the lactate came from and why the blood pH is likely to be low.

Solution. Poor perfusion means the tissues are short of O2, so the pyruvate dehydrogenase route and the mitochondria are starved. To keep making ATP, cells keep glycolysis running and use lactate dehydrogenase to reduce pyruvate to lactate, regenerating NAD+. Lactic acid, a carboxylic acid, is fully ionized at blood pH, so each lactate released into blood comes with an H+; when production outruns the liver’s ability to clear it, bicarbonate is used up and pH falls: lactic acidosis.

Check: The same chemistry occurs in a sprinting athlete, but there the liver and heart clear the lactate within an hour; in sepsis, production is widespread and clearance is impaired, so the number stays high.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Lactate as a vital sign. In shock or sepsis (a body-wide response to infection), tissues do not get enough oxygen, pyruvate is shunted to lactate, and the H+ that comes with it causes lactic acidosis. A serum lactate above about 2 mmol/L is a red flag for poor tissue perfusion; watching it fall shows the resuscitation is working. Lactated Ringer’s, a common IV fluid, contains sodium lactate; a working liver converts that lactate to bicarbonate, so the fluid does not add acid. Red blood cells have no mitochondria and live on glycolysis (2 ATP per glucose) plus the pentose phosphate pathway (Section 13.6). Thiamine is made into a coenzyme (TPP) of pyruvate dehydrogenase: without it pyruvate cannot become acetyl CoA, lactate rises, and the heavily aerobic brain and heart suffer first. That is beriberi (heart failure, neuropathy) and Wernicke encephalopathy (confusion, unsteady gait) in alcohol use disorder, which is why thiamine is given with IV dextrose. In healthy exercise, lactate rises and clears within an hour; it is fuel, not a poison.
