Multiple Choice

Problem 10.1

Which amino acid has a nonpolar R group?

  1. serine
  2. valine
  3. aspartate
  4. lysine
Answer
B. Valine’s R group is an isopropyl group, CH(CH3)2, which is all C–H. Serine has a polar OH, while aspartate and lysine have charged side chains.

Problem 10.2

  1. H2N–CH2–COOH
  2. +H3N–CH2–COOH
  3. +H3N–CH2–COO−
  4. H2N–CH2–COO−
Answer

Problem 10.3

An amino acid in a solution with pH below its pI carries a net charge of

  1. positive
  2. negative
  3. zero
  4. it depends on the R group only
Answer
A. Below the pI there are extra H+ ions, so the amino acid picks up H+ (COO− becomes COOH) and the net charge becomes positive.

Problem 10.4

In the tripeptide Ala-Ser-Gly, the C-terminal amino acid is

  1. alanine
  2. serine
  3. glycine
  4. it has none
Answer
C. Peptides are written from the N-terminus (left) to the C-terminus (right), so the last residue, glycine, has the free COO− end.

Problem 10.5

An α-helix is an example of

  1. primary structure
  2. secondary structure
  3. tertiary structure
  4. quaternary structure
Answer
B. Secondary structure is the regular coiling or folding of the backbone held by hydrogen bonds between C=O and N–H groups; the α-helix and β-pleated sheet are the two main types.

Problem 10.6

Problem 10.8

Hemoglobin has quaternary structure because it is made of four polypeptide chains.

Answer
True. Quaternary structure is the arrangement of two or more polypeptide chains; hemoglobin has four (two α and two β) held together by R-group interactions.

More Practice: Analyze and Compare

Problem 10.55

Which amino acid has an aromatic, nonpolar R group?

  1. threonine
  2. phenylalanine
  3. glutamine
  4. arginine
Answer
B. Phenylalanine's side chain is –CH2–phenyl: a benzene ring with no O–H, so it is aromatic and hydrophobic. Threonine (–OH) and glutamine (amide) are polar neutral; arginine is basic.

Problem 10.56

Lysine has a second amino group on its side chain. At pH 12, well above the pI of 9.7, the net charge of lysine is

  1. +2
  2. +1
  3. 0
  4. −1
Answer
D. At pH 12 both amino groups have lost their H+ and are neutral –NH2, while the carboxyl group is –COO−. The only charge left is the −1 on the carboxylate. (At pH 1 the answer would be +2: two –NH3+ and a neutral –COOH.)

Problem 10.57

Which interaction that stabilizes tertiary structure is a covalent bond?

  1. hydrophobic interaction between Leu and Ile
  2. hydrogen bond between Ser and Thr
  3. salt bridge between Glu and Lys
  4. disulfide bond between two Cys
Answer
D. A disulfide bond is a real S–S covalent bond formed by oxidizing two thiol groups. The other three are intermolecular-type attractions (dispersion/hydrophobic, hydrogen bonding, ionic) between side chains, which is why heat and alcohol can undo them but not a disulfide.

Problem 10.58

The hydrogen bonds that hold an α-helix together form between

  1. the R groups of neighboring residues
  2. the C=O and N–H groups of the backbone
  3. the N-terminus and the C-terminus
  4. cysteine side chains
Answer
B. Secondary structure (helix and sheet) is a backbone-only affair: each peptide C=O hydrogen-bonds to an N–H further along the chain. R groups point outward and are not involved; they take over in tertiary structure.

Problem 10.59

Analyze the table. Four amino acids are placed in the middle of an electrophoresis gel buffered at pH 6.0; the left electrode is negative and the right electrode is positive. For each amino acid, give its net charge sign at pH 6.0 and say whether it moves left, right, or stays put.

Amino acidR-group classpI
Aspartate (D)acidic3.0
Alanine (A)nonpolar6.0
Histidine (H)basic7.6
Arginine (R)basic10.8
Answer
Compare pH 6.0 with each pI. Aspartate: pH is above its pI, so it is net negative and moves right toward the positive electrode. Alanine: pH equals its pI, so it is a zwitterion with net zero charge and stays put. Histidine: pH is below its pI, net positive, moves left toward the negative electrode. Comparing pH with pI gives the direction only; how fast each one moves also depends on the size of its charge and on its size and shape, so these data do not rank the speeds.

Problem 10.60

Analyze the figure, which shows a tripeptide drawn at pH 7.4.

(a) Identify the three residues from their side chains and write the peptide in three-letter and one-letter form. (b) Which residue is the N-terminus? (c) Name the peptide. (d) How many peptide bonds does it contain, and what are the products of complete hydrolysis? (e) Which residue could form a disulfide bond in a larger protein?

Problem 10.61

Compare and contrast the dipeptides Gly-Ala and Ala-Gly. Do they have the same molecular formula? The same structure? Explain what differs.

Answer
Both contain one glycine and one alanine joined by a single peptide bond with one water removed, so they have the same molecular formula (C5H10N2O3) and are constitutional isomers. They are different compounds, though. In Gly-Ala (glycylalanine) the glycine carboxyl group was used to make the peptide bond, so glycine is the N-terminus with the free –NH3+, and alanine is the C-terminus with the free –COO−. In Ala-Gly (alanylglycine) the roles are reversed: alanine's carboxyl is in the amide and glycine's carboxyl is free. Because the two ends of a peptide are chemically different, the order of residues defines the molecule, which is why peptides are always written N-terminus first.

Problem 10.62

Predict and compare: two protein samples are treated. Sample 1 is keratin from hair, rich in cysteine. Sample 2 is a small enzyme that contains no cysteine. Each sample is split in half: one half is heated to 90 °C, the other is treated at room temperature with a reducing agent (thioglycolate). Which treatments denature which sample, and why?

Answer
Heat denatures both. Heat disrupts hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, which every folded protein depends on, so both keratin and the enzyme lose secondary and tertiary structure (the enzyme stops working). The reducing agent denatures only keratin. Thioglycolate reduces –S–S– disulfide bonds back to two –SH groups; keratin has many, so its structure loosens (that is the first step of a perm). The cysteine-free enzyme has no disulfide bonds to break, so the reducing agent leaves its hydrogen bonds, salt bridges and hydrophobic core untouched and it keeps its shape. In every case the peptide bonds and primary structure survive, because neither heat nor a reducing agent hydrolyzes amides.

More Practice: Clinical and Integrative

Problem 10.63

A patient with cirrhosis has a serum albumin of 2.1 g/dL (normal about 3.5–5.0 g/dL) and swollen ankles. (a) Is albumin a fibrous or a globular protein, and what does that tell you about its solubility? (b) Explain in one or two sentences why low albumin leads to edema. (c) A family member asks why the patient cannot simply drink an albumin solution. Answer using the chemistry of this chapter.

Answer
(a) Albumin is globular: compact, with polar and charged side chains on the surface, so it is very water-soluble and stays dissolved in plasma. (b) Albumin is too large to pass through capillary walls, so it exerts oncotic pressure, an osmotic pull that holds water inside the blood vessels. With too little albumin, that pull weakens and fluid leaks into the tissues, producing edema. (c) Albumin taken by mouth would be treated like any dietary protein: stomach acid denatures it and pepsin, then trypsin and chymotrypsin, hydrolyze its peptide bonds to free amino acids. None of the intact protein reaches the blood, so albumin (like insulin) must be given intravenously.

Problem 10.64

Chlorhexidine mouth rinse owes its long-lasting effect (substantivity) to the fact that the chlorhexidine molecule carries positive charges and binds to the proteins of the acquired pellicle and oral mucosa, which have a net negative charge at the pH of saliva (about 7). (a) Which amino acid side chains give these proteins their negative charges at pH 7, and what is each side chain's structure? (b) What type of interaction holds chlorhexidine to the protein? (c) Would you expect the binding to be stronger or weaker if the mouth became strongly acidic? Explain.

Answer
(a) The negative charges come from the acidic amino acids aspartate (–CH2–COO−) and glutamate (–CH2CH2–COO−), whose side-chain carboxyl groups are deprotonated at pH 7 (like every carboxylic acid, they lose their H+ at body pH). The C-terminal carboxylate contributes one more. (b) An ionic attraction between the positive chlorhexidine and the negative carboxylates, the same kind of attraction as a salt bridge inside a protein. (c) Weaker. Strong acid would protonate the carboxylates to neutral –COOH (in strong acid a carboxylic acid keeps its H), removing the negative charges that chlorhexidine binds to. This is also why acidic conditions can denature proteins: salt bridges are lost when the charges disappear.

Problem 10.65

In sickle cell disease, position 6 of hemoglobin's β chain is valine instead of glutamate. (a) Classify the normal and the substituted side chains. (b) Which level of protein structure is changed directly by the mutation? (c) Explain why hemoglobin S molecules stick to one another when oxygen is low. (d) Hemoglobin electrophoresis is run at pH 8.6. Predict whether HbS moves toward the positive electrode faster or slower than normal HbA, and why.

Answer
(a) Glutamate is acidic (side-chain carboxylate, −1 at body pH); valine is nonpolar (branched hydrocarbon). (b) The primary structure: one residue in the sequence is different. (c) The mutation puts a hydrophobic side chain on the surface of the protein, where it is exposed to water. Deoxygenated hemoglobin exposes a matching hydrophobic pocket on a neighboring molecule, so the valines pack together by hydrophobic interactions, the molecules polymerize into long fibers, and the fibers bend the red cell into a sickle. (d) Slower. At pH 8.6 every glutamate side chain is negative, so HbS, which has lost two negative charges (one per β chain), is less negative than HbA and migrates less far toward the positive electrode. That difference is what the lab test detects.

Problem 10.66

Integrative (Chapters 6, 7, 10). Aspartame, the sweetener in many sugar-free gums and diet sodas, is the methyl ester of the dipeptide Asp-Phe. (a) Name the dipeptide and give its one-letter form. (b) List the functional groups present in aspartame (think about both ends and the side chain). (c) Write the products of complete hydrolysis in the gut. (d) Why do products containing aspartame carry a warning for people with phenylketonuria (PKU)?

Answer
(a) Aspartylphenylalanine, DF; aspartate is the N-terminus. (b) An amine (the free N-terminal –NH3+), an amide (the peptide bond), a carboxylic acid/carboxylate (the aspartate side chain), an ester (the C-terminal carboxyl group esterified with methanol), and an aromatic ring (the phenylalanine side chain). (c) Hydrolysis of the ester (Chapter 6) releases methanol, and hydrolysis of the amide/peptide bond (Chapter 7) releases the free amino acids aspartate and phenylalanine. (d) People with PKU lack the enzyme that converts phenylalanine to tyrosine, so phenylalanine from any source, including aspartame, accumulates to harmful levels; they must limit their total phenylalanine intake.

Problem 10.67

Integrative (Chapters 4 and 10). A salon "perm" first applies a solution of a thiol (thioglycolate) to the hair, wraps the hair on rollers, and then applies a dilute hydrogen peroxide "neutralizer." (a) Which bond in keratin does the first solution break, and is that step an oxidation or a reduction of the keratin? (b) What does the peroxide do, and is that step an oxidation or a reduction? (c) Which level of protein structure is altered, and are any peptide bonds broken?

Answer
(a) The thiol solution breaks keratin's disulfide bonds, converting each –S–S– into two –SH groups. Gaining H (and losing the S–S link) is a reduction of the keratin (Chapter 4: thiol ↔ disulfide is a redox pair). (b) With the hair held in its new shape, hydrogen peroxide oxidizes pairs of –SH groups that are now next to each other back into new –S–S– disulfide bonds, locking in the curl. (c) The tertiary structure (and the quaternary packing between keratin chains) is rearranged. No peptide bonds are broken; the primary structure is unchanged, which is why the hair is reshaped rather than destroyed.

Problem 10.68

A patient on a very restricted diet develops bleeding, swollen gums and loose teeth, and the clinic suspects scurvy (vitamin C deficiency). (a) Which fibrous protein of the gingiva and periodontal ligament is affected? (b) Vitamin C is required by the enzyme that converts proline residues in this protein to hydroxyproline. Hydroxyproline's extra –OH groups let the three chains hydrogen-bond to one another. Which level of structure is weakened when they are missing? (c) Why does the defect show up in gums and teeth rather than in, say, hemoglobin?

Answer
(a) Collagen (type I), the triple-helix protein that makes up the periodontal ligament, the gingival connective tissue and the organic matrix of dentin, cementum and bone. (b) The triple helix, collagen's secondary structure, is held together by hydrogen bonds between chains, and the hydroxyproline –OH groups supply many of them. Without them the helix is unstable, and the collagen fibers that form are weak. (c) Hemoglobin contains no hydroxyproline and is not a collagen, so it is unaffected. Tissues that are rich in collagen and constantly rebuild it, such as gums, periodontal ligament, blood-vessel walls and healing wounds, are the first to fail.

Problem 10.69

Which amino acid has a hydrophilic R group?

  1. isoleucine
  2. methionine
  3. threonine
  4. proline
Answer
C. Threonine's side chain, –CH(OH)CH3, has an O–H that hydrogen-bonds with water, so it is polar (hydrophilic, neutral). Isoleucine and proline are hydrocarbons and methionine's side chain is a thioether; none can hydrogen-bond, so all three are hydrophobic.

Problem 10.70

A tripeptide contains cysteine, glycine and threonine. If glycine is the C-terminus, which could be its name?

  1. glycylcysteinylthreonine
  2. threonylglycylcysteine
  3. cysteinylthreonylglycine
  4. glycylthreonylcysteine
Answer
C. The C-terminal residue is the only one that keeps its full name, and it comes last. Only "cysteinylthreonylglycine" (Cys-Thr-Gly) ends in glycine; threonylcysteinylglycine (Thr-Cys-Gly) would also have been correct. In A and D glycine is the N-terminus; in B cysteine is the C-terminus.

Problem 10.71

Which pair of side chains can hydrogen-bond with each other in the tertiary structure of a protein?

  1. valine and leucine
  2. serine and glutamine
  3. lysine and glutamate
  4. alanine and phenylalanine
Answer
B. Serine has an O–H and glutamine an amide N–H and C=O, so they can hydrogen-bond. Valine/leucine and alanine/phenylalanine are nonpolar pairs (hydrophobic interactions); lysine/glutamate are + and − (a salt bridge).

Problem 10.72

True or false? If false, correct it: "The α-helix is held together by intermolecular hydrogen bonds between R groups."

Answer
False on both counts. The hydrogen bonds of an α-helix are intramolecular (within one chain) and form between backbone groups, the N–H of one residue and the C=O of the residue four positions along. R groups point outward and are not involved; they matter in tertiary structure.

Problem 10.73

A lab report lists one peptide as "Gly, Leu, Ser" and another as "Gly-Leu-Ser." What does each notation tell you, and how many different tripeptides could the first one be?

Answer
Commas mean only the composition is known: the peptide contains one glycine, one leucine and one serine in an unknown order. Dashes mean the order is known and the dashes stand for the peptide bonds: glycine is the N-terminus, serine the C-terminus. The comma version could be any of 3 × 2 × 1 = 6 tripeptides (Gly-Leu-Ser, Gly-Ser-Leu, Leu-Gly-Ser, Leu-Ser-Gly, Ser-Gly-Leu, Ser-Leu-Gly).

Problem 10.74

Draw the tripeptide Ser-Gly-Lys as it exists at pH 7.4 (a condensed drawing is fine). Give its name, its one-letter form, and its net charge.

Answer
Backbone first (N–C–CO three times, N-terminus on the left), then the side chains: +H3N–CH(CH2OH)–C(=O)–NH–CH2–C(=O)–NH–CH[(CH2)4NH3+]–COO−. Name: serylglycyllysine; one-letter form SGK. Charges at 7.4: N-terminus +1, C-terminus −1, and the lysine side chain +1 (lysine is a basic amino acid: like every amine, its side-chain –NH2 picks up H+ at body pH). Net charge +1. The serine O–H stays neutral.

Problem 10.75

Write the condensation reaction in which valine and serine form the dipeptide Val-Ser. Show both reactants and both products (structures at pH 7.4), and name the new bond.

Answer
Reactants: valine, +H3N–CH[CH(CH3)2]–COO−, and serine, +H3N–CH(CH2OH)–COO−. The carboxyl group of valine (the N-terminal residue) and the amino group of serine lose the parts of a water molecule. Products: the dipeptide +H3N–CH[CH(CH3)2]–C(=O)–NH–CH(CH2OH)–COO− (valylserine) + H2O. The new C(=O)–NH link is a peptide bond, which is an amide. Note that Ser-Val would use serine's carboxyl and valine's amine instead and is a different compound.

Problem 10.76

The dipeptide +H3N–CH(CH2SH)–C(=O)–NH–CH(CH3)–COO− is heated with aqueous acid. (a) Name the dipeptide and the reaction. (b) Draw the products as they would exist at pH 7.4. (c) Which level of protein structure does this reaction destroy, and would 70% ethanol do the same?

Answer
(a) The N-terminal residue carries –CH2SH (cysteine) and the C-terminal residue –CH3 (alanine): cysteinylalanine, Cys-Ala. Acid and heat cause hydrolysis of the peptide bond. (b) Water adds across the C(=O)–NH bond and the two free amino acids return as zwitterions: cysteine, +H3N–CH(CH2SH)–COO−, and alanine, +H3N–CH(CH3)–COO−. (c) Hydrolysis breaks the peptide bonds, so it destroys the primary structure. Ethanol would not; it only denatures (disrupts hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions), leaving every peptide bond intact.

Problem 10.77

Analyze the figure. The amino acid is drawn as a zwitterion.

(a) Identify the carboxyl group, the amine group, the α-carbon and the R group. (b) Name the amino acid and give its three- and one-letter abbreviations. (c) Classify the R group as polar, nonpolar, acidic or basic and explain your choice. (d) Draw the structure at a pH well below the pI and well above the pI.

Answer
(a) Carboxyl group: the C(=O)O− at the right; amine group: the –NH3+ above the central carbon; α-carbon: the carbon bonded to both; R group: everything to the left, –CH2–C(=O)NH2. (b) A side chain ending in an amide, one CH2 from the α-carbon: asparagine, Asn, N (glutamine would have two CH2 groups). (c) Polar (hydrophilic, neutral). The amide C=O and N–H hydrogen-bond with water, but an amide nitrogen is not basic (its lone pair is tied up with the carbonyl) and there is no carboxyl group on the side chain, so it is neither basic nor acidic and carries no charge at pH 7.4. Problem 10.81

A student explains: “At pH 7.4, the peptide Ala-Lys-Gly has a net charge of 0, because only the two ends of a peptide carry charges and they cancel.” Find the error and correct it.

Cumulative Problem 10.1 (Chapters 6, 7 and 10)

Alanine in water at pH 7 exists mainly as

  1. CH3CH(NH3+)COO−, a zwitterion
  2. CH3CH(NH2)COOH, a neutral molecule
  3. CH3CH(NH3+)COOH, a cation
  4. CH3CH(NH2)COO−, an anion
Answer
A. An amino acid carries a carboxylic acid and an amine. At pH 7 the acid has lost its proton (Chapter 6) and the amine has gained one (Chapter 7), so the molecule is a dipolar ion, or zwitterion, with no net charge. Form C dominates at pH 1 (both groups protonated) and form D at pH 12 (both deprotonated). The neutral form B is never present in any significant amount. The zwitterion is why amino acids are crystalline solids that melt above 200 °C and dissolve in water but not in ether.

Cumulative Problem 10.2 (Chapters 5 and 10)

Nineteen of the twenty amino acids are chiral; glycine is not. Explain why, and state which enantiomer the body uses to build proteins.

Answer
The α-carbon of every amino acid carries an H, an –NH3+, a –COO− and a side chain R. When R is anything other than H, the carbon has four different groups and is chiral (Chapter 5). In glycine R is H, so the carbon carries two identical hydrogens and the molecule is achiral. Proteins are built from the L-amino acids only; D-amino acids occur in some bacterial cell walls and in a few antibiotics, which is part of why those antibiotics are not digested by our enzymes.

Cumulative Problem 10.3 (Chapters 6, 7 and 10)

Glycine and alanine condense to form a dipeptide. (a) What bond forms, what family of compound does it belong to, and what is lost? (b) Draw Gly-Ala and explain why Ala-Gly is a different compound. (c) What reaction, run by digestive enzymes, reverses the condensation?

Answer
(a) A peptide bond, which is an amide (Chapter 7) formed between the carboxyl of one amino acid and the amino group of the next with loss of water. (b) In Gly-Ala, glycine supplies the free –NH3+ (N-terminus) and its carboxyl is tied up in the amide; alanine keeps the free –COO− (C-terminus): H3N+–CH2–CO–NH–CH(CH3)–COO−. In Ala-Gly the roles are swapped, so the free amine and free carboxyl sit on different residues: a different molecule with different properties, even though the atoms are the same. (c) Hydrolysis, the reverse of condensation; pepsin, trypsin and the peptidases of the gut add water back across each peptide bond to release free amino acids.

Cumulative Problem 10.4 (Chapters 1, 4, 6, 7 and 10)

For each pair of side chains, name the interaction that holds them together in a folded protein and the chapter where you first met that force: (a) serine and threonine, (b) lysine and aspartate, (c) leucine and phenylalanine, (d) two cysteines.

Answer
(a) Hydrogen bonding between the –OH groups (an alcohol-to-alcohol hydrogen bond, Chapters 1 and 4). (b) A salt bridge, the ionic attraction between lysine’s –NH3+ and aspartate’s –COO− (Chapters 6 and 7 acid–base chemistry). (c) Hydrophobic interaction: nonpolar side chains cluster together inside the protein away from water, held by dispersion forces (Chapter 1). (d) A disulfide bond, a true covalent S–S bond formed by oxidizing two thiol –SH groups (Chapter 4). Only the disulfide survives boiling; the others are the ones that heat, pH change and alcohol disrupt.

Cumulative Problem 10.5 (Chapters 1, 4, 6 and 10)

Explain, at the level of intermolecular forces, why each of the following denatures a protein, and give a practical example from health care of each: (a) heat, (b) a strong change in pH, (c) 70% isopropyl alcohol, (d) heavy-metal ions such as Hg2+.

Answer
(a) Heat makes the chain vibrate enough to break hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic clustering; the egg white solidifies, and an autoclave kills bacteria by cooking their proteins. (b) pH change adds or removes protons from acidic and basic side chains (Chapters 6 and 7), destroying salt bridges and some hydrogen bonds; stomach acid unfolds dietary protein so pepsin can reach it. (c) Alcohol inserts into hydrophobic regions and competes for hydrogen bonds; 70% isopropanol denatures bacterial and viral proteins on skin and instruments (the water in the mixture helps the alcohol penetrate, which is why 70% works better than 100%). (d) Heavy-metal ions bind to –SH groups and to carboxylates, breaking disulfide bridges and salt bridges; this is why mercury is toxic and why egg white or milk was once given as a first-aid antidote to tie up the metal.

Cumulative Problem 10.6 (Chapters 1, 5 and 10)

In the clinic. In sickle-cell hemoglobin a single glutamate on the surface of the β chain is replaced by valine. (a) Compare the two side chains by polarity. (b) Use intermolecular forces to explain why this one change makes deoxygenated hemoglobin molecules stick together into fibers that deform the red cell. (c) Hemoglobin has four chains. (c) Hydrophobic interaction: nonpolar side chains cluster together inside the protein away from water, held by dispersion forces (Chapter 1). (d) A disulfide bond, a true covalent S–S bond formed by oxidizing two thiol –SH groups (Chapter 4). Only the disulfide survives boiling; the others are the ones that heat, pH change and alcohol disrupt.

Cumulative Problem 10.5 (Chapters 1, 4, 6 and 10)

(a) Heat makes the chain vibrate enough to break hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic clustering; the egg white solidifies, and an autoclave kills bacteria by cooking their proteins. (b) pH change adds or removes protons from acidic and basic side chains (Chapters 6 and 7), destroying salt bridges and some hydrogen bonds; stomach acid unfolds dietary protein so pepsin can reach it. (c) Alcohol inserts into hydrophobic regions and competes for hydrogen bonds; 70% isopropanol denatures bacterial and viral proteins on skin and instruments (the water in the mixture helps the alcohol penetrate, which is why 70% works better than 100%). (d) Heavy-metal ions bind to –SH groups and to carboxylates, breaking disulfide bridges and salt bridges; this is why mercury is toxic and why egg white or milk was once given as a first-aid antidote to tie up the metal.

Cumulative Problem 10.6 (Chapters 1, 5 and 10)

(a) Glutamate carries a charged, polar carboxylate side chain; valine has a nonpolar, hydrophobic isopropyl group. (b) A charged group on the surface is comfortable in water. The valine patch is not, so it seeks another nonpolar surface: in deoxygenated hemoglobin it finds a hydrophobic pocket on a neighboring molecule and the molecules stack into long fibers through hydrophobic interactions. The fibers stiffen the cell into a sickle shape that blocks capillaries. One amino acid out of 146, and it comes down to polarity. (c) Four chains together are quaternary structure. Each chain holds a heme, a flat ring (a porphyrin) with an iron ion at its center that binds O2. In Chapter 12 you will see that the valine substitution traces back to a single base change in the DNA, GAG to GTG.
