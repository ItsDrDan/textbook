Learning Objectives

  • Describe the structure of glycogen (α-1,4 chains, α-1,6 branches, reducing and non-reducing ends) and state where the body stores it and why.
  • List the steps of glycogenesis, naming each enzyme, its enzyme class, and the reaction type, and account for the energy cost.
  • List the steps of glycogenolysis and contrast them with glycogenesis: enzymes, energy, activating nucleotide, hormone control, and tissue.
  • Explain how glucagon, epinephrine, and insulin switch the two pathways on and off through phosphorylation, and how AMP, ATP, and glucose 6-phosphate fine-tune them.
  • Connect glycogen chemistry to fasting hypoglycemia, glycogen storage diseases, and exercise.

A patient who has been NPO (nothing by mouth) since midnight, a marathon runner at mile 20, and a child with a glycogen storage disease are all living on the same chemistry: how fast the body can tuck glucose away as glycogen and how fast it can get it back. This section follows one glucose unit into the glycogen granule and out again, and shows you which enzymes, nucleotides, and hormones decide the direction of traffic.

Glycogen: the body's glucose bank

Glycogen is the storage polysaccharide of animals (Chapter 8). It is a tree of glucose units joined by α-1,4-glycosidic bonds in the straight runs, with an α-1,6-glycosidic bond starting a new branch about every 8 to 12 glucose units. The one free hemiacetal end (the reducing end) is tied to a small protein, glycogenin, that starts every molecule. Every other chain end is a non-reducing end, a glucose whose carbon 4 OH is free. The enzymes that add and remove glucose work only at non-reducing ends, so a highly branched tree with thousands of ends can be built and dismantled far faster than one long chain.

Two tissues hold almost all of it, and they use it differently:

  • Liver: about 100 g (roughly 10% of the organ's mass after a meal). Liver glycogen is a public reserve: it is broken down to free glucose and exported to keep blood glucose near 70–100 mg/dL between meals. It is largely gone after 12 to 24 hours of fasting.
  • Skeletal muscle: about 300–400 g in total (1–2% of muscle mass). Muscle glycogen is private: a muscle cell burns its own glycogen for its own contraction and cannot release glucose to the blood, for a reason you will see below.

Glycogenesis: building the store

Glycogenesis is the synthesis of glycogen from glucose. Like every biosynthesis in this book, it does not simply run the breakdown pathway backward: it uses a different set of enzymes and pays with a nucleotide. In one line, the pathway is this: glucose is phosphorylated, the phosphate is moved to carbon 1, the sugar is activated with UTP, and the activated sugar is added to a growing chain. In lecture the same pathway is counted as three steps: (1) glucose → G6P → G1P (phosphorylation, then isomerization), (2) G1P + UTP → UDP-glucose + PPi (activation), and (3) UDP-glucose + glycogen → longer glycogen + UDP (glycogen synthase). Table 1 simply splits the first lecture step in two and adds the branching enzyme. Glycogenolysis, below, is a two-step pathway: glycogen phosphorylase, then phosphoglucomutase.

Notice what is not on the list: glycogen synthase cannot use free glucose or even G1P. It needs the activated form, UDP-glucose, in which the glucose is attached to the nucleotide uridine diphosphate (UDP) and is easy to hand on. This is the same strategy as attaching an amino acid to tRNA before protein synthesis (Chapter 12): spend a nucleotide first, then the joining step is downhill.

Energy cost. Phosphorylating free glucose to glucose 6-phosphate (hexokinase) spends one ATP. Activating glucose 1-phosphate to UDP-glucose spends one UTP, and because the cell regenerates UTP from UDP with ATP (UDP + ATP → UTP + ADP), that UTP counts as a second ATP. So storing one molecule of free glucose in glycogen costs about 2 ATP-equivalents (1 if the starting molecule is already glucose 6-phosphate). That is cheap insurance for a molecule that will give back 30–32 ATP when fully oxidized (Chapter 14).

Glycogenolysis: making a withdrawal

Glycogenolysis is the breakdown of glycogen. Its key enzyme, glycogen phosphorylase, does not hydrolyze the α-1,4 bond. It uses inorganic phosphate (Pi) instead of water to split the bond, a reaction called phosphorolysis:

glycogen(n) + Pi → glycogen(n−1) + glucose 1-phosphate

Because the phosphate is transferred onto the sugar rather than water being added, phosphorylase is classified as a transferase, not a hydrolase. The trick is efficient: the glucose comes off already phosphorylated, so the cell does not have to spend an ATP to trap it. Phosphorylase requires the coenzyme pyridoxal phosphate (PLP), made from vitamin B6; this enzyme holds most of the body's vitamin B6.

Phosphorylase works down a chain until it is four units from an α-1,6 branch point and then stalls. The debranching enzyme finishes the job in two acts: first it transfers a block of three glucose units from the stub onto the end of a neighboring chain (a transferase action), and then it hydrolyzes the single remaining α-1,6-linked glucose (a hydrolase action). That last glucose comes off as free glucose, not G1P. Since roughly one glucose in ten sits at a branch point, about 10% of the glucose released from glycogen is free glucose and about 90% is G1P.

Phosphoglucomutase then converts G1P to G6P, the same reversible isomerization as in glycogenesis. What happens next depends on the tissue:

  • In the liver (and kidney), glucose-6-phosphatase, a hydrolase, removes the phosphate: G6P + H2O → glucose + Pi. Free glucose can cross the membrane and leaves for the blood.
  • Muscle has no glucose-6-phosphatase. Its G6P cannot leave the cell (phosphorylated sugars do not cross membranes) and instead enters glycolysis (Section 13.5) to power contraction. This is why muscle glycogen never raises blood glucose.

Regulation: one switch, two enzymes

If glycogen synthase and glycogen phosphorylase were both fully active, the cell would build and tear down glycogen at the same time, burning 2 ATP per cycle for nothing. The body prevents this with reciprocal regulation: the same signal that turns one enzyme on turns the other off. The signal is covalent modification by phosphorylation, the mechanism introduced in Section 11.7.

  • Fasting or stress. Glucagon (from the pancreas when blood glucose falls; acts on the liver) and epinephrine (from the adrenal medulla during stress or exercise; acts on liver and muscle) bind receptors on the cell surface. The receptor raises the level of the second messenger cyclic AMP (cAMP), which activates protein kinase A. Protein kinase A, directly and through a helper kinase, adds phosphate groups to both enzymes. Phosphorylated phosphorylase is active; phosphorylated synthase is inactive. The net effect is glycogenolysis ON, glycogenesis OFF.
  • Fed state. Insulin (released when blood glucose rises) activates a protein phosphatase that strips those phosphate groups off. Dephosphorylated synthase is active; dephosphorylated phosphorylase is inactive. Glycogenesis ON, glycogenolysis OFF.

Allosteric fine-tuning. Hormones set the overall direction, but the cell's own energy state adjusts the dial (allosteric control, Section 11.6). In muscle, AMP, which accumulates when ATP is being used faster than it is made, binds phosphorylase and switches it on even in its dephosphorylated form, so a hard-working fiber gets fuel at once. ATP and glucose 6-phosphate signal "enough fuel" and inhibit phosphorylase; G6P also activates glycogen synthase. In the liver, free glucose itself inhibits phosphorylase, so once blood glucose is restored the liver stops releasing more.

Professor Tip

Students mix up which enzyme is "on" when phosphorylated. Remember that phosphorylase phosphorylated = on: the enzyme with "phosph" in its name is activated by a phosphate. Then synthase must be the opposite. For hormones, think of the problem each one solves: glucagon and epinephrine arrive when glucose is scarce or needed fast, so they open the bank (breakdown); insulin arrives when glucose is plentiful, so it makes a deposit (synthesis).

Worked examples

Example 1: The ATP cost of a glycogen round trip

Problem. A muscle cell takes up one glucose after a meal, stores it in glycogen, and releases it during a workout to run glycolysis. (a) How many ATP-equivalents does storage cost? (b) How many ATP does glycolysis of that glucose unit yield? (c) Compare with glycolysis of a glucose taken straight from the blood.

Solution. (a) Hexokinase spends 1 ATP (glucose → G6P) and UDP-glucose pyrophosphorylase spends 1 UTP, which the cell replaces using 1 ATP. Storage costs 2 ATP-equivalents. (b) Phosphorylase releases the unit as G1P using Pi, not ATP; phosphoglucomutase makes G6P for free. Glycolysis from G6P skips the hexokinase step, so it spends only the 1 ATP at phosphofructokinase-1 and makes 4, a net of 3 ATP (plus 2 NADH). (c) Glucose from the blood must first pay the hexokinase ATP, so its net is 2 ATP. Over the whole round trip the muscle spent 2 and got back 3: the net price of having stored the glucose was 1 ATP.

Check. Storage can never be free, or the cell could make energy by cycling. A net cost of 1 ATP-equivalent per glucose stored and released is a sensible insurance premium.

Example 2: Classify the enzymes

Problem. Assign each enzyme to one of the six classes from Chapter 11 and name the reaction type: (a) glycogen phosphorylase, (b) glucose-6-phosphatase, (c) phosphoglucomutase, (d) UDP-glucose pyrophosphorylase, (e) glycogen synthase.

Solution. (a) Transferase: it transfers a glucosyl unit onto phosphate (phosphorolysis). It is not a hydrolase because water is not the reagent. (b) Hydrolase: water splits the phosphate ester bond of G6P, releasing Pi. (c) Isomerase: G6P and G1P have the same formula; the phosphate is simply relocated. (d) Transferase: the UMP portion of UTP is transferred to G1P, releasing PPi. (e) Transferase: a glucosyl group is transferred from UDP-glucose to the growing chain, forming an α-1,4 bond.

Check. A quick test: if H2O appears as a reactant, think hydrolase; if a group moves from one molecule to another, think transferase; if reactant and product are isomers, think isomerase.

Example 3: The liver 10 hours after dinner

Problem. A patient ate dinner at 7 p.m. and is NPO for a 7 a.m. procedure. Predict the state of the liver's glycogen enzymes at 5 a.m. and what the liver is doing to blood glucose.

Solution. Ten hours without food means blood glucose has drifted toward the low end of normal and insulin is low while glucagon is high. Glucagon raises cAMP in liver cells, protein kinase A is active, and both enzymes are phosphorylated: glycogen phosphorylase is ON and glycogen synthase is OFF. Phosphorylase and debranching enzyme release G1P and some free glucose; phosphoglucomutase makes G6P; glucose-6-phosphatase hydrolyzes it to free glucose, which leaves for the blood. The liver's roughly 100 g store is perhaps half gone by now, so the liver is also ramping up gluconeogenesis (Section 13.8) to take over as glycogen runs out.

Check. The patient's blood glucose should still be near normal: that is the whole point of liver glycogen. If the patient had instead been fasting for two days, glycogen would be exhausted and the answer would rest entirely on gluconeogenesis.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Glycogen storage diseases are inherited defects in single enzymes of this section. In von Gierke disease (type I), glucose-6-phosphatase is missing, so the liver can make G6P from glycogen (and from gluconeogenesis) but cannot release free glucose: the result is severe fasting hypoglycemia (low blood glucose), hepatomegaly (an enlarged liver stuffed with glycogen), and lactic acidosis because trapped G6P is pushed through glycolysis to lactate. In McArdle disease (type V), muscle glycogen phosphorylase is defective; blood glucose is normal, but muscles cannot tap their own glycogen, so patients have exercise intolerance with cramps and myoglobinuria (dark urine from muscle breakdown) after exertion. A marathoner "hits the wall" when muscle and liver glycogen run out near 30–35 km, which is why athletes carbohydrate-load for days before a race to fill the stores. In anyone, liver glycogen is gone after about 24 hours of starvation. That matters for emergency care: an injection of glucagon for severe hypoglycemia works only by triggering liver glycogenolysis, so it fails in a patient whose liver glycogen is already empty from prolonged fasting or heavy alcohol use (alcohol also blocks gluconeogenesis, Section 13.8). Such a patient needs glucose itself.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

A child with von Gierke disease avoids hypoglycemia by eating small carbohydrate meals every few hours and often takes uncooked cornstarch at bedtime as a slow-release glucose source. Chemically that is a nearly continuous supply of fermentable carbohydrate to plaque bacteria, so the risk of dental caries (tooth decay) is high and these patients need an intensive prevention plan. The epinephrine in a local anesthetic cartridge is the same hormone that drives glycogenolysis; the small amount in dental anesthesia raises blood glucose only slightly, but it is one reason the health history asks about diabetes and why you note how much epinephrine you used. Finally, athletes who rely on sports drinks and gels during long training sessions bathe their teeth in a liquid that is both acidic (pH often 3–4, below the critical pH of 5.5 for enamel demineralization) and sugary; frequent sipping produces erosion plus caries. The chemistry that refills their glycogen is the same chemistry that feeds plaque.

Summary

  • Glycogen is a branched polymer of glucose: α-1,4 chains with α-1,6 branches every 8–12 units, one reducing end on glycogenin, and many non-reducing ends where the enzymes work. The liver stores about 100 g for the blood; muscle stores about 300–400 g for itself.
  • Glycogenesis: glucose → G6P (hexokinase/glucokinase, transferase) → G1P (phosphoglucomutase, isomerase) → UDP-glucose (UDP-glucose pyrophosphorylase, transferase, uses UTP) → glycogen (glycogen synthase, transferase, α-1,4); branching enzyme (transferase) makes α-1,6 branches. Cost: about 2 ATP-equivalents per glucose.
  • Glycogenolysis: glycogen phosphorylase (transferase, PLP) uses Pi to release G1P (phosphorolysis); debranching enzyme transfers three units and hydrolyzes the α-1,6 bond to free glucose (about 10%); G1P → G6P; in liver, glucose-6-phosphatase (hydrolase) releases free glucose to the blood; muscle lacks it and sends G6P into glycolysis.
  • Glucagon (liver) and epinephrine (liver and muscle) raise cAMP, activate protein kinase A, and phosphorylate the enzymes: phosphorylase ON, synthase OFF. Insulin dephosphorylates them: synthase ON, phosphorylase OFF.
  • Allosteric control: in muscle, AMP activates phosphorylase while ATP and G6P inhibit it; G6P activates synthase.
  • Von Gierke disease (no glucose-6-phosphatase) causes fasting hypoglycemia, hepatomegaly, and lactic acidosis; McArdle disease (no muscle phosphorylase) causes exercise intolerance. Glucagon rescue works only when liver glycogen remains.

Exercises

Exercise 1

Name the two kinds of glycosidic bond in glycogen, state where each occurs, and explain why having many non-reducing ends is useful.

Answer
α-1,4 bonds join glucose units along each chain; α-1,6 bonds start branches about every 8–12 units. Glycogen synthase and glycogen phosphorylase act only at non-reducing ends, so many branches mean many ends and therefore fast storage and fast release.

Exercise 2

Write the reaction catalyzed by UDP-glucose pyrophosphorylase and explain why this "activation" step is needed before glycogen synthase can act.

Answer
Glucose 1-phosphate + UTP → UDP-glucose + PPi. Glycogen synthase cannot use free glucose or G1P; it transfers a glucosyl group from UDP-glucose, in which the glucose is attached to UDP and is easy to hand on, so bond formation is energetically downhill. Hydrolysis of PPi to 2 Pi makes the activation effectively irreversible.

Exercise 3

Phosphorolysis and hydrolysis both break a glycosidic bond. Compare the two reactions in terms of the reagent, the product, and the enzyme class of the enzyme that catalyzes each in glycogen breakdown.

Answer
Phosphorolysis uses inorganic phosphate (Pi) and gives glucose 1-phosphate; it is catalyzed by glycogen phosphorylase, a transferase. Hydrolysis uses water and gives free glucose; in glycogen breakdown it is carried out by the α-1,6-glucosidase activity of the debranching enzyme, a hydrolase. Phosphorolysis saves the ATP that would otherwise be needed to phosphorylate free glucose.

Exercise 4

A muscle cell and a liver cell both break down glycogen to glucose 6-phosphate. What happens to the G6P in each tissue, and which enzyme explains the difference?

Answer
Liver cells have glucose-6-phosphatase, which hydrolyzes G6P to free glucose that leaves for the blood. Muscle lacks this enzyme; its G6P cannot cross the membrane and enters glycolysis to supply ATP for contraction. So liver glycogen supports blood glucose while muscle glycogen supports only the muscle.

Exercise 5

Fill in each blank with "active" or "inactive": After glucagon binds a liver cell, protein kinase A phosphorylates glycogen phosphorylase, making it ______, and glycogen synthase, making it ______. After insulin acts, both enzymes are dephosphorylated; synthase is now ______ and phosphorylase is ______.

Answer
Active; inactive; active; inactive. Phosphorylation turns phosphorylase on and synthase off (glucose release); dephosphorylation does the reverse (glucose storage).

Exercise 6

During a sprint, muscle ATP falls and AMP rises before any hormone has time to arrive. Explain how glycogen breakdown still speeds up within seconds, and name the kind of regulation involved.

Answer
AMP binds an allosteric site on muscle glycogen phosphorylase and activates it even in its dephosphorylated form, so G1P production rises immediately; ATP and G6P, which fall during work, no longer inhibit it. This is allosteric regulation, as opposed to the slower covalent (phosphorylation) control triggered by epinephrine.

Exercise 7

A patient with von Gierke disease has low blood glucose, an enlarged liver, and a high blood lactate. Explain all three findings from the missing enzyme.

Answer
Without glucose-6-phosphatase the liver cannot convert G6P to free glucose, so neither glycogenolysis nor gluconeogenesis can raise blood glucose (fasting hypoglycemia). Glycogen that cannot be exported accumulates, enlarging the liver (hepatomegaly). The trapped G6P is shunted through glycolysis to pyruvate and lactate, which spill into the blood (lactic acidosis).

Exercise 8

An emergency department nurse gives a glucagon injection to an unconscious patient with hypoglycemia who has been drinking heavily and not eating for two days. Blood glucose barely changes. Explain, and state what the patient needs instead.

Answer
Glucagon works by switching on liver glycogenolysis. After two days without food the liver's glycogen is already exhausted, and alcohol additionally blocks gluconeogenesis, so there is no glucose for glucagon to release. The patient needs glucose supplied directly, such as IV dextrose.
