Learning Objectives

  • Understand the catabolism of fatty acids, i.e., \(\beta\)-oxidation of fatty acids, the reactions involved in the process, the number of cycles needed, and the calculation of ATP yield per fatty acid.
  • Understand obesity and the catabolism of glycerol, associated with \(\beta\)-oxidation of fatty acids (ketone bodies are in Section 15.5).

\(\beta\)-Oxidation of fatty acids

Although glucose is a quick energy source that animals need, fatty acids have higher energy per unit mass. Therefore, fats are the main energy storage compounds in animals. Fatty acids (\(\ce{R-COOH}\)) in fats are a major source of energy for animals. Hydrolysis of fats, i.e., the 1st stage of catabolism of fats, begins in the digestive tract and completes in the cytosol of the cells. The second stage of fatty acid catabolism starts with the conversion of (\(\ce{R-COOH}\)) to the thioester group of coenzyme A, (\(\ce{R-CO-S-CoA}\)), i.e., an acyl-\(\ce{CoA}\), at the cost of energy of \(\ce{ATP}\) to \(\ce{AMP}\) conversion. The thioester is called an activated fatty acid. Some of the important terms used in this section are illustrated in the figure below.

Acyl-\(\ce{CoA}\) is transported from the cytosol into mitochondria for the second stage of its catabolism. In the second stage, the Acyl-\(\ce{CoA}\) is fragmented into two \(\ce{C's}\) fragments in the form of acetyl-\(\ce{CoA}\). The activated fatty acid goes through a set of four reactions in which the \(\beta\ce{C}\) is oxidized to a carbonyl \(\ce{C=O}\) group. Another coenzyme A makes thioester group with the \(\beta\)-\(\ce{C=O}\), and an acetyl-\(\ce{CoA}\) group is split off, leaving behind an acyl-\(\ce{CoA}\) with two \(\ce{C's}\) less than the initial acyl-\(\ce{CoA}\). This process is called \(\beta\)-Oxidation of fatty acids. The process repeats on the fragment acyl-\(\ce{CoA}\) again and again until the last acyl-\(\ce{CoA}\) fragment left is acetyl-\(\ce{CoA}\). The acetyl-\(\ce{CoA}\) enters the citric acid cycle, which is the third stage of the catabolism, as in the case of the catabolism of glucose. These reactions are explained next.

Activation of fatty acids

Activation begins when the fatty acid (as its carboxylate ion) reacts with \(\ce{ATP}\): an AMP unit is attached to the fatty acid (acyl-adenylate) and pyrophosphate is released, as shown below. Overall, activation is: fatty acid + ATP + CoA → fatty acyl CoA + AMP + PPi.

Pyrophosphate is removed by hydrolysis, making the reaction irreversible: \(\ce{PP_i + H2O -> 2P_i + 2H^{+}}\).

Coenzyme A (\(\ce{HS-CoA}\)) then takes the place of the AMP, giving the fatty acyl CoA (a thioester) and releasing AMP.

As described next, acyl-CoA product is transported from the cytosol into mitochondria for the \(\beta\)-oxidation process.

Reaction 1 -oxidation

Reaction 1 is the dehydrogenation of acyl-\(\ce{CoA}\) by enzyme acyl-\(\ce{CoA}\)-dehydrogenase that removes \(\ce{H}\) from \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) \(\ce{C's}\) generating a trans \(\ce{C=C}\) bond. An \(\ce{FAD}\) is reduced to \(\ce{FADH2}\) as the acyl-\(\ce{CoA}\) is oxidized in this reaction.

Reaction 2 -hydration

The \(\ce{C=C}\) is hydrated by addition of \(\ce{H2O}\) that selectively installs an \(\ce{-OH}\) group at \(\beta\)\(\ce{C}\), creating a new chiral center in L-configuration. This is a hydration, an addition of water like the hydration of an alkene (Chapter 3); here the \(\ce{-OH}\) always ends up on the \(\beta\) carbon.

Reaction 3 -oxidation

The secondary \(\ce{-OH}\) group is oxidized to a ketone (\(\ce{C=O}\)) group at the expense of reduction of \(\ce{NAD^{+}}\) to \(\ce{NADH}\) by the following reaction.

Reaction 4 -cleavage by thiolysis

In the fourth reaction, coenzyme A (\(\ce{HS-CoA}\)) splits the bond between the \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) carbons (thiolysis). The two-carbon end leaves as acetyl CoA, and the rest of the chain, now two carbons shorter, stays attached to the new CoA, ready for the next turn.

The second product of the above reaction is an acyl-\(\ce{CoA}\) with two \(\ce{C's}\) fewer than the initial acyl-\(\ce{CoA}\).

Number of \(\beta\)-oxidation cycles

The product acyl-\(\ce{CoA}\) of the first \(\beta\)-oxidation cycle goes through the process of four reactions again and again until the last acyl-\(\ce{CoA}\) product is acetyl-\(\ce{CoA}\), i.e., \(\ce{CH3CO-S-CoA}\). The process is illustrated in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\), with the help of an example of \(\beta\)-oxidation of stearic acid which is a typical \(\ce{18C's}\) saturated fatty acid.

If the starting fatty acid has \(\ce{nC's}\), there are: \(\frac{n}{2}\) acetyl-\(\ce{CoA}\) produced. Since the last cycle produces two acetyl-\(\ce{CoA}\), the number of cycles for a fatty acid containing \(\ce{nC's}\) is: \(\frac{n}{2}-1\). For example, stearic acid has \(\ce{18C's}\) and it goes through: \(\frac{18}{2}-1 = 8~\beta\text{-oxidation cycles}\).

\(\ce{ATP}\) Yield from fatty acid oxidation

Each \(\beta\)-oxidation cycle of a fatty acid yields one \(\ce{FADH2}\), one \(\ce{NADH}\) and one acetyl-\(\ce{CoA}\). These molecules produce \(\ce{ATP's}\) when they enter the citric acid cycle and oxidative phosphorylation, as described earlier in the catabolism of glucose. One \(\ce{FADH2}\) ≈ 1.5 \(\ce{ATP's}\), one \(\ce{NADH}\) ≈ 2.5 \(\ce{ATP's}\), and one acetyl-\(\ce{CoA}\) ≈ 10 \(\ce{ATP's}\) that makes 14 \(\ce{ATP's}\) per \(\beta\)-oxidation cycle. Since there are (\(\frac{n}{2}-1\)) or (\(0.5\times{n}-1\)) cycles for a fatty acid containing \(\ce{nC's}\), there are: (\((0.5\times{n}-1)\times{14}))~\ce{ATP's}\) produced. The last cycle yields two acetyl-\(\ce{CoA}\), so there are an additional \(\ce{10ATP's}\) from the last acetyl-\(\ce{CoA}\). The activation step converts one \(\ce{ATP}\) to one \(\ce{AMP}\) which is equivalent to conversion of \(\ce{2ATP}\) to \(\ce{2ADP}\). So, after adding ten from the last acetyl-\(\ce{CoA}\) to the formula ( \((0.5\times{n}-1)\times{14})~\ce{ATP's}\) and subtracting 2 for the \(\ce{2ATP's}\) consumed in the activation step, the formula for the number of \(\ce{ATP's}\) produced per fatty acid containing \(\ce{nC's}\) becomes the following:

\(\ce{ATP's}~\text{produced per fatty acid containing}~\ce{nC's} = ((0.5\times{n}-1)\times{14} + 10 -2)~\ce{ATP's}\).

It simplifies to:

\(\ce{ATP's}~\text{produced per fatty acid containing}~\ce{nC's} = (7\times{n}-6)~\ce{ATP's}\).

For example, stearic acid contains \(\ce{18C's}\) and produces \((7\times18-6) = 120~\ce{ATP's}\). So, one mole of stearic acid (284.48 g/mol) produces 120 mol \(\ce{ATP's}\), which is significantly higher than 32 mol \(\ce{ATP's}\) produced by one mole of glucose (180.156 g/mol). When converted to moles of \(\ce{ATP's}\) produced per unit mass, stearic acid produces 0.42 mol \(\ce{ATP's}\)/g, which is more than two times higher than 0.18 mol \(\ce{ATP's}\)/g of glucose. This is because the average oxidation state of glucose \(\ce{C's}\) is higher than those of fatty acid \(\ce{C's}\). Glucose is a quick energy source, but animals use fats as relatively longer-term energy storage molecules due to their higher energy density.

Unsaturated fatty acids

Unsaturated fatty acids have \(\ce{C=C}\) bond in their alkyl chain. \(\beta\)-Oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids happens the same way as for saturated fatty acids except for the following changes. If \(\ce{C=C}\) bond is a trans-\(\ce{C=C}\) bond between \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) \(\ce{C's}\), reaction 1 in the \(\beta\)-oxidation is not needed and \(\ce{FADH2}\) from reaction 1 is not produced. If \(\ce{C=C}\) bond is a cis-\(\ce{C=C}\) bond or it is between \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\) \(\ce{C's}\), it goes through isomerization reactions catalyzed by isomerase enzymes to convert the \(\ce{C=C}\) bond into a trans \(\ce{C=C}\) bond between \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) \(\ce{C's}\) before reaction 2 happens on it. Unsaturated fatty acids yield a little less \(\ce{ATP's}\) due to one \(\ce{FADH2}\) less produced per \(\ce{C=C}\) bond, but the difference is small and the formula: \((7\times{n}-6)~\ce{ATP's}\), still gives a reasonably accurate estimate of \(\ce{ATP}\) yield.

Fat and obesity

Although carbohydrates and glucose are quick energy sources, fat has more energy per mass or volume. Storage of fats for long-term energy supply is an important survival feature for several animals. For example, bears build up fat when food is plentiful and then live on it for months in their winter dens. Whales and penguins are kept warm by a layer of body fat called blubber and use it as an energy source to survive when food is unavailable. Camels store fat in their humps and draw on it when food is scarce. Migratory birds also store fat and use it as an energy source during migration.

Humans can store fats. In earlier times, major human food was vegetables, and fats in food accounted for about 20% of food calories, but these days about one-third of food calories (roughly 33–36% in the United States) come from fats. The accumulation of too much fat is associated with overweight and obesity. Obesity is an excess accumulation of body fat that negatively affects health. It is measured in terms of body mass index (BMI), which is the ratio of a person's mass to the square of a person's height. BMI 18.5 kg/m2 to 24.9 kg/m2 is normal, less is underweight, more is overweight, and over 30 kg/m2 is obese, as shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\). Obesity is associated with medical conditions like diabetes, high blood pressure, stroke, heart disease, gallstones, arthritis, and some cancers. According to NIH, nearly 3 out of 4 adults aged 20 or above in the US are overweight or obese. It is a preventable condition, as described in the following video message from NIH.

Just as insulin regulates blood glucose, leptin, a hormone made by adipose tissue, governs the long-term balance between food intake and energy expenditure. When there is more adipose tissue (fatty tissues), more leptin is released, which decreases hunger by giving a feeling of fullness, and less fatty tissue means less fat and less leptin, which has the opposite effect. Just as diabetes disturbs the function of insulin, obesity may be related to the malfunctioning of leptin. This is an active area of research these days for finding obesity treatment.

Ketone bodies

When β-oxidation produces more acetyl CoA than the citric acid cycle can use, as in fasting or uncontrolled diabetes, the liver turns the surplus into ketone bodies. Their structures, ketosis and ketoacidosis are taught in Section 15.5.

Catabolism of glycerol

Glycerol, a part of triglycerides (fats), accounts for about 5% of their energy. Glycerol is first converted into glycerol-3-phosphate by glycerol kinase, which transfers a phosphate group from ATP to an end (primary) –OH of glycerol and releases ADP. Then, the secondary alcohol of glycerol is oxidized to a ketone group at the expense of reduction of a \(\ce{NAD^{+}}\) coenzyme into \(\ce{NADH}\) as shown below.

Lecture Connection

This is stage 3 of fatty acid breakdown, the fatty acid spiral. The lecture wants two things from it: the four-question table for the four reactions (they follow the citric acid cycle pattern, oxidize, hydrate, oxidize, then a cleavage), and the arithmetic. For a chain with n carbons, slice it every two carbons: acetyl CoA = n/2, repetitions = n/2 − 1, and ATP = (acetyl CoA × 10) + (NADH × 2.5) + (FADH2 × 1.5) − 2 for activation. Glycerol, the other half of a fat, gets its own two steps at the end of this page.

Professor Tip

Count ATP from β-oxidation step by step on exams (2.5 per NADH, 1.5 per FADH2, 10 per acetyl CoA, minus 2 for activation); the formula 7n − 6 is a check, and both give 106 for palmitate and 120 for stearate. You are responsible for the reaction type, enzyme class and coenzyme of each step, not the mechanisms. The obesity, BMI and leptin material is background; ketone bodies are in Section 15.5.

β-Oxidation: the four reactions

β-Oxidation is a sequence of four reactions that repeatedly cleaves two-carbon units from the carboxyl end of the acyl CoA. The name comes from the β carbon, the second carbon from the carboxyl end (the α carbon is the first): it is the carbon that gets oxidized, from CH2 to C=O, before the bond between the α and β carbons is broken. “Pathway” is a better word than “cycle” here: it is a linear set of four reactions that repeats, and each repetition gives a different (shorter) product rather than regenerating the starting material. The first three reactions are the pattern you saw in the citric acid cycle (succinate → fumarate → malate → oxaloacetate): dehydrogenate to a C=C, hydrate it, dehydrogenate the new alcohol to a ketone; the fourth, thiolysis, is unique to β-oxidation. Practice: At which step of β-oxidation does each event occur? (a) A C–C single bond is converted to a C=C. (b) NAD+ is reduced to NADH. (c) A hydration reaction occurs. (d) An acetyl CoA molecule is produced.

Answer
(a) Step 1. (b) Step 3. (c) Step 2. (d) Step 4.

Counting repetitions, acetyl CoA, and ATP

  • Activation (Section 15.3) costs the equivalent of 2 ATP, once per fatty acid.
  • Each repetition removes 2 carbons as acetyl CoA and produces 1 FADH2 + 1 NADH, leaving an acyl CoA two carbons shorter.
  • For a fatty acid with n carbons (even): repetitions = n/2 − 1, acetyl CoA = n/2. The last repetition splits a 4-carbon piece into two acetyl CoA (the “extra” acetyl CoA), which is why there is one fewer repetition than acetyl CoA. A C18 fatty acid needs 8 repetitions to give 9 acetyl CoA; a C14 fatty acid needs 6 to give 7.
  • ATP total = (acetyl CoA × 10) + (NADH × 2.5) + (FADH2 × 1.5) − 2. Each acetyl CoA yields about 10 ATP in the citric acid cycle (Chapter 14); each repetition’s FADH2 and NADH go straight to electron transport. For stearic acid (18:0): 9 acetyl CoA (90) + 8 NADH (20) + 8 FADH2 (12) = 122, minus 2 for activation = 120 ATP.

Professor Tip

Draw the chain and slice it every 2 carbons from the carboxyl end. Count the cuts (repetitions of β-oxidation) and the pieces (acetyl CoA). A 10-carbon chain gets 4 cuts and makes 5 pieces. Do the counting before you touch any ATP numbers, and remember that the final cut gives two pieces at once, which is why pieces = cuts + 1.

Fats versus carbs, carbon for carbon

Example 2: Carbs versus fat, carbon for carbon

Problem. How many glucose molecules contain the same number of carbons as one stearic acid (18:0)? Compare the ATP from those glucose molecules with the ATP from the stearic acid, and explain the result in terms of oxidation state.

Solution. Glucose has 6 carbons, so 3 glucose = 18 carbons. ATP from 3 glucose = 3 × 32 = 96 ATP (using 32 per glucose, the malate–aspartate-shuttle value; with 30 per glucose it is 90, and the conclusion is the same). ATP from stearic acid = 9 acetyl CoA (90) + 8 NADH (20) + 8 FADH2 (12) − 2 (activation) = 120 ATP. The fatty acid gives more ATP per carbon because its carbons are mostly –CH2– groups with no oxygen, so each one has more hydrogens (electrons) to give to NAD+ and FAD; glucose carbons each already carry an oxygen and are partly oxidized before metabolism begins.

Check. Per carbon: 120/18 ≈ 6.7 ATP for stearic acid versus 96/18 ≈ 5.3 for glucose. On a mass basis the gap widens to about 2.4 times, which is the 9 kcal/g versus 4 kcal/g you see on nutrition labels (Chapter 14).

Unsaturated fatty acids

A double bond that is already present means the FAD step is skipped for that repetition: the pathway starts at step 2, so each C=C gives one fewer FADH2 (1.5 fewer ATP). Less energy is released from an unsaturated fatty acid than from the saturated one of the same length. There is a wrinkle: the double bonds in natural fatty acids are cis, and the hydration step adds water only to a trans double bond between the α and β carbons. A cis–trans isomerase (an isomerase) converts the cis bond, which is usually one carbon too far down the chain, into the trans-(2,3) bond that step 2 needs. At the level of this course, count one fewer FADH2 per pre-existing double bond; oleic acid (18 C, one C=C) gives 120 − 1.5 = 118.5 ATP. The number of acetyl CoA and the number of repetitions do not change.

Glycerol: the other half of a fat

Glycerol is worth a closer look because it is the only part of a fat that can become glucose. Released from adipose tissue by hormone-sensitive lipase (Section 15.2), it dissolves in blood and travels to the liver (fat cells lack the first enzyme), where it is converted to dihydroxyacetone phosphate in two steps:

  1. Phosphorylation of the 1° hydroxyl group. Glycerol kinase (a kinase, so a transferase; ATP → ADP) phosphorylates an end carbon to make glycerol 3-phosphate. How can you tell? A phosphate was added and ATP became ADP.
  2. Oxidation of the 2° alcohol to a ketone. Glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (an oxidoreductase; NAD+ → NADH) oxidizes the middle carbon, giving dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP). How can you tell? A C–OH became a C=O, and NAD+, the oxidizing agent, was reduced.

DHAP is a glycolysis intermediate, so it can go “downhill” to pyruvate when energy is needed or “uphill” to glucose during fasting (Section 13.8). Two glycerols make one glucose. The amount is small (glycerol is about one-tenth of a fat by mass and about 5% of its energy), but during a long fast it is a steady contribution to blood glucose.

Practice: True or false, and correct any false statement. (a) Glycerol 3-phosphate is reduced to dihydroxyacetone phosphate. (b) NAD+ is the oxidizing agent. (c) NAD+ is being reduced. (d) A dehydrogenase enzyme is likely involved.

Answer
(a) False. A C–OH becoming a C=O is an oxidation; glycerol 3-phosphate is oxidized. (b) True. (c) True: the oxidizing agent is itself reduced, to NADH. (d) True: an oxidation that reduces NAD+ is run by a dehydrogenase, an oxidoreductase.

Summary

  • β-Oxidation removes two carbons at a time as acetyl CoA in four repeated reactions: oxidation (FAD), hydration, oxidation (NAD+) and thiolysis.
  • A fatty acid with n carbons gives n/2 acetyl CoA in n/2 − 1 cycles.
  • Count ATP at 2.5 per NADH, 1.5 per FADH2 and 10 per acetyl CoA, minus 2 for activation: palmitate (16 C) gives 106 ATP, stearate (18 C) 120.
  • Glycerol from fat enters glycolysis as dihydroxyacetone phosphate.
