Learning Objectives

  • Explain how enzymes speed reactions by lowering activation energy without changing ΔG, using the lock-and-key and induced-fit models.
  • Read an enzyme's name (substrate + reaction + -ase) and classify it into one of the six main classes from the reaction it catalyzes, including reactions you will meet in metabolism.
  • Read and sketch graphs of enzyme activity vs. temperature, pH, substrate concentration, and enzyme concentration.
  • Distinguish competitive, noncompetitive, and irreversible inhibition and give an example (a drug or a poison) of each.
  • Describe the five ways cells regulate enzymes: allosteric control, feedback inhibition, zymogen activation, covalent modification, and genetic control.
  • Connect water-soluble vitamins to their coenzymes, know the main roles of vitamins A, D, E, and K, and match each deficiency disease to its vitamin.

1. What enzymes do

An enzyme is a biological catalyst, almost always a globular protein. It lowers the activation energy by binding the substrate in its active site (E + S ↔ ES ↔ E + P); it does not change ΔG, ΔH or the equilibrium, and it is not used up. Specificity comes in levels: absolute (urease), group (hexokinase), linkage (lipases, proteases), and stereo (D vs. L). Lock-and-key says the site is rigid; induced fit, the better model, says the site molds around the substrate. Names end in -ase and tell you the substrate and often the reaction (lactate dehydrogenase); pepsin, trypsin and chymotrypsin are the exceptions. Energy diagrams and naming: Section 11.1; specificity and the two models: Section 11.2.

2. Six enzyme classes

Classify by the reaction: oxidoreductase (NAD+/FAD in the equation; dehydrogenase, oxidase, reductase), transferase (a group moves between molecules; kinase, transaminase), hydrolase (water splits one molecule into two; protease, lipase, amylase, phosphatase), lyase (a small molecule leaves or adds at a double bond with no water and no redox; decarboxylase, dehydratase, hydratase, aldolase, most synthases), isomerase (one molecule rearranges; mutase, epimerase), ligase (ATP is spent and two molecules become one; synthetase, carboxylase; plus citrate synthase, the course exception). Read the name like a sentence. The full table, cues and metabolism examples: Section 11.1.

3. What affects enzyme activity

Four curves to sketch. Rate vs. substrate: rises, then plateaus at the maximum rate when every active site is busy (saturation). Rate vs. enzyme: a straight line when substrate is in excess (the basis of blood enzyme tests). Rate vs. temperature: up to the optimum (37 °C for human enzymes), then a steep fall as the enzyme denatures; cold only slows it, reversibly. Rate vs. pH: a hump at the optimum pH (pepsin 2, trypsin 8, most enzymes 7.4) because active-site R groups must carry the right charges. Curves, examples, diagnostic enzymes and the lab: Section 11.3.

4. Inhibition

Competitive: looks like the substrate, binds the active site, reversible, overcome by more substrate (statins, methotrexate, malonate, fomepizole or ethanol for methanol poisoning). Noncompetitive: binds elsewhere and changes the shape, reversible, more substrate does not help, maximum rate lowered (cyanide; heavy-metal ions also count here). Feedback inhibition by a pathway’s end product is allosteric regulation (Section 11.6), not one of these three inhibitor types. Irreversible: covalent bond to an R group, usually in or near the active site, permanent (heavy metals on –SH groups in or away from the active site, which count as both irreversible and noncompetitive; organophosphates and nerve agents on acetylcholinesterase, penicillin on transpeptidase, aspirin on COX). Table, drug table and the Try It widget: Section 11.4.

5. Cofactors and vitamins

Lecture definitions: a cofactor is inorganic (Zn2+, Mg2+, Fe2+, Cu2+); a coenzyme is organic and built from a water-soluble vitamin. Know the pairs: niacin → NAD+ (hydride carrier; pellagra), riboflavin → FAD (two H; cracked lips), pantothenic acid → coenzyme A (acyl carrier), thiamine → TPP (decarboxylation; beriberi, Wernicke), B6 → PLP (amino groups), biotin (CO2), folate → THF (one-carbon units; neural-tube defects), B12 (pernicious anemia), C (collagen; scurvy). Fat-soluble A, D, E, K are stored, can be toxic, and are not coenzymes: vision, calcium absorption (rickets), antioxidant, clotting (warfarin blocks K). Full tables, clinical box and Experiment 14: Section 11.5.

6. Regulation

Five ways a cell controls an enzyme. Fast and reversible: allosteric control (a regulator binds a site other than the active site; negative slows, positive speeds) and feedback inhibition (the end product is a negative allosteric regulator of the first enzyme of its own pathway). Fast but one-way: zymogen activation (a blocking piece is cut off by hydrolysis; trypsinogen → trypsin, prothrombin → thrombin; irreversible); fast and reversible: covalent modification (a kinase adds a phosphate, a phosphatase removes it; the glycogen switch); slow: genetic control (induction or repression of the gene; hours to days). Allosteric and feedback control: Section 11.6; zymogens, phosphorylation and genetic control: Section 11.7.

7. Enzymes in the clinic: diagnosis, drug targets, and infection control

Enzymes stay inside healthy cells, so an enzyme in the blood means damaged cells, and which enzyme tells you which tissue: troponin and CK-MB for the heart, ALT and AST (transaminases) for the liver, lipase and amylase for the pancreas, alkaline phosphatase for bone and bile ducts. Because enzymes are specific, one drug can block one enzyme (statins, penicillin, aspirin, methotrexate). Heat sterilizes, and alcohol disinfects, by denaturing microbial enzymes. Blood-test table and temperature box: Section 11.3; drug table: Section 11.4; vitamins on the ward: Section 11.5; enzymes in the mouth: Section 11.1.

Health Career Connection

  • Nursing – diagnostic enzymes: high troponin and CK-MB point to heart damage; ALT/AST to liver damage; lipase and amylase to the pancreas (Section 11.3).
  • Nursing – drugs as inhibitors: statins are competitive inhibitors of the enzyme that makes cholesterol; penicillin irreversibly inhibits the bacterial cell-wall enzyme; warfarin blocks vitamin K recycling, so patients on it need monitoring before dental procedures (Sections 11.4, 11.5).
  • Nursing – thiamine first: IV glucose without thiamine can precipitate Wernicke encephalopathy in a thiamine-deficient patient (Section 11.5).
  • Dental hygiene – enzymes in the mouth: salivary amylase and lysozyme are hydrolases; S. mutans glucosyltransferase glues plaque to enamel from sucrose; zinc-dependent MMPs degrade collagen in periodontitis and are the target of low-dose doxycycline (Sections 11.1, 11.4).

Worked Examples

Example 1: Classify the enzyme

Problem. Classify the enzyme for each reaction: (a) lactate + NAD+ → pyruvate + NADH + H+; (b) maltose + H2O → 2 glucose; (c) glucose + ATP → glucose-6-phosphate + ADP.

Solution. (a) H is removed and NAD+ is reduced: oxidoreductase (lactate dehydrogenase). (b) Water splits a bond: hydrolase (maltase). (c) A phosphate moves from ATP to glucose: transferase (hexokinase).

Check. In (c) ATP is used but two molecules do not become one (glucose-6-phosphate and ADP are both products), so it is a transferase, not a ligase.

Example 2: Type of inhibition

Problem. An inhibitor slows an enzyme, but adding a large excess of substrate restores the normal maximum rate. What type of inhibition is this?

Solution. Competitive. Substrate can out-compete the inhibitor for the active site.

Check. If the inhibition were noncompetitive or irreversible, the maximum rate would stay lowered no matter how much substrate was added.

Example 3: Name the regulation

Problem. Identify the type of regulation in each case. (a) In a bacterium, the amino acid isoleucine binds a site away from the active site of threonine deaminase, the first enzyme of the pathway that makes isoleucine, and slows it. (b) After a meal, insulin causes a phosphate to be removed from glycogen synthase, and the enzyme becomes active. (c) A patient who starts drinking heavily every day makes several times more of the liver enzyme that oxidizes alcohol after two weeks. (d) Trypsinogen entering the small intestine loses a six-amino-acid piece and becomes trypsin.

Solution. (a) Feedback inhibition through an allosteric site: the end product inhibits the first enzyme. (b) Covalent modification (dephosphorylation by a phosphatase). (c) Genetic control (enzyme induction: more enzyme is made). (d) Zymogen activation by hydrolysis of a peptide bond, which is irreversible.

Check. Ask two questions: how fast (seconds for allosteric and feedback, minutes for covalent modification, hours to days for genetic control) and is it reversible (all but zymogen activation).

Example 4: Match the enzyme name to the reaction

Problem. Match each enzyme name to the reaction it catalyzes: (1) decarboxylase, (2) transaminase, (3) hydratase, (4) protease, (5) carboxylase. Reactions: (A) adds water across a C=C; (B) removes CO2 from a substrate; (C) joins CO2 to a substrate using ATP; (D) hydrolyzes a peptide bond; (E) moves an amino group from one molecule to another.

Solution. (1) B, a lyase that removes a small molecule. (2) E, a transferase. (3) A, a lyase (water adds to one molecule, nothing is split). (4) D, a hydrolase. (5) C, a ligase; carboxylases form a bond between CO2 and the substrate at the cost of ATP.

Check. "De-" means a group is removed (decarboxylase, dehydratase, deaminase); "-ase" on a substrate name usually means hydrolysis (protease, lipase, nuclease); "trans-" means a group moves; "carboxylase" and "synthetase" are the ATP-using ligases.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Saying enzymes change ΔH, ΔG, or the equilibrium, or are consumed. They only lower activation energy and speed up the approach to equilibrium.
  • Thinking more substrate overcomes noncompetitive or irreversible inhibition.
  • Mixing up "kinase" (moves a phosphate from ATP, transferase) with "phosphatase" (removes a phosphate with water, hydrolase).
  • Calling fumarase or any "hydratase" a hydrolase. Water that adds across a double bond is a lyase reaction; water that splits one molecule into two is hydrolysis. Aconitase (citrate → isocitrate, Chapter 14) is the trap: the overall change looks like an isomerization, but the enzyme works by removing water and adding it back across a C=C, so it is classified as a lyase (a hydratase).
  • Calling every ATP-using enzyme a ligase. Kinases use ATP too, but they transfer the phosphate; a ligase uses ATP to join two molecules into one. As a rule, a "synthase" (no ATP) is a lyase and a "synthetase" (ATP) is a ligase; the one exception in this course is citrate synthase (Chapter 14), which is classified as a ligase.
  • Treating "optimum pH" as always 7. Pepsin's optimum is about 2 and alkaline phosphatase's about 9; the optimum is wherever the enzyme normally works.
  • Confusing cold with heat. Refrigeration slows an enzyme reversibly; heat above the optimum denatures it irreversibly.
  • Assuming fat-soluble vitamins are coenzymes. The coenzymes come from the water-soluble B vitamins; A, D, E, and K act as a light sensor, a hormone, an antioxidant, and a helper in blood clotting.
  • Using "cofactor" and "coenzyme" as if they were the same. In this course a cofactor is inorganic (a metal ion such as Zn2+ or Mg2+) and a coenzyme is organic (made from a vitamin, such as NAD+ from niacin).
  • Treating a heavy-metal ion as a cofactor. Mg2+, Zn2+, Fe2+ and Cu2+ are cofactors; Hg2+ and Pb2+ are poisons that bond to –SH groups and inhibit enzymes irreversibly (and noncompetitively).

Clinical Update: Rosa’s Story, Part 11 of 15

How Rosa’s medicines work

Rosa’s story for this chapter is on the Chapter 11 opening page. Quick recap for the questions below: atorvastatin blocks HMG-CoA reductase, and part of the drug resembles the enzyme’s natural substrate; low-dose doxycycline slows the collagenases in Rosa’s gums, which need a zinc ion, by binding metal ions; and her nurse practitioner checked her ALT, a liver enzyme measured in the blood.

Case Question 11.1

Atorvastatin resembles the enzyme’s substrate and binds the active site. What type of inhibition is this, and can it be overcome?

Answer
Competitive inhibition. The inhibitor and the substrate compete for the same active site, so a high enough substrate concentration can overcome it.

Case Question 11.2

Zn2+ is a cofactor for collagenase. What is a cofactor, and why would binding it slow the enzyme?

Answer
A cofactor is an inorganic, non-protein helper, such as a metal ion like Zn2+, that an enzyme needs in order to work. If the metal ion is tied up, the enzyme loses activity.

Case Question 11.3

ALT is an enzyme that works inside liver cells. Why does a high ALT level in the blood suggest liver damage?

Answer
When liver cells are damaged, their contents leak into the blood, so enzymes that normally stay inside the cells show up at higher levels. That is why ALT is used as a liver test.

Where this chemistry is taught: Section 11.4, Section 11.5. Rosa’s story continues at the start of Chapter 12.

Practice

Exercise 1

Classify the enzyme: fructose-6-phosphate → glucose-6-phosphate.

Answer
Isomerase (glucose-6-phosphate isomerase)

Exercise 2

Why does pepsin stop working when stomach contents move into the small intestine?

Answer
The pH rises from about 2 to about 8, far from pepsin's optimum, so the charges in its active site change and it loses activity.

Exercise 3

Which vitamin is the precursor of NAD+? Is it fat- or water-soluble?

Answer
Niacin (vitamin B3); water-soluble

Exercise 4

Why are digestive proteases made as zymogens?

Answer
So they do not digest the proteins of the cells (pancreas, stomach lining) that make them. They are activated only after reaching the digestive tract.

Exercise 5

In a five-step pathway, high levels of the final product slow down the first enzyme. What kind of regulation is this?

Answer
Feedback control (feedback inhibition), usually acting through an allosteric site

Exercise 6

Classify the enzyme and name the clue: (a) isocitrate + NAD+ → α-ketoglutarate + CO2 + NADH + H+; (b) glucose-1-phosphate → glucose-6-phosphate; (c) acetyl-CoA + CO2 + ATP → malonyl-CoA + ADP + Pi.

Answer
(a) Oxidoreductase (isocitrate dehydrogenase): NAD+ is reduced to NADH, which outranks the loss of CO2 when you classify. (b) Isomerase (phosphoglucomutase): one molecule, same formula, phosphate moved to a different carbon. (c) Ligase (acetyl-CoA carboxylase): ATP is spent to join CO2 to acetyl-CoA; biotin is the coenzyme.

Exercise 7

A patient's blood work shows high ALT and AST with normal CK-MB and troponin. Which organ is most likely damaged, and what class of enzyme are ALT and AST?

Answer
The liver. ALT and AST are transaminases, which are transferases (they move an amino group from an amino acid to an α-keto acid). Normal cardiac markers argue against heart damage.
