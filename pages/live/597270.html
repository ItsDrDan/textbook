Who this book is for

Introductory Organic and Biological Chemistry for FCC is a free, openly licensed textbook built for CHEM 3B at Fresno City College. It follows the course outline of record for CHEM 3B (effective Spring 2027, C-ID CHEM 102) chapter by chapter, and it was written with students headed into nursing, dental hygiene, radiologic technology, respiratory therapy and the other health professions in mind. You will meet the chemistry first and then see, in every chapter, where that chemistry shows up in a clinic, a pharmacy, a dental operatory or a lab result.

Version 1.1, November 2026 (last updated November 11, 2026). Remixed and written by Daniel Kwasniewski, Department of Chemistry, Fresno City College.

Free to use

This book is free to read online and free to download. Students are never required to purchase it. Because it is an Open Educational Resource, you may keep your own copy, use it, revise it, remix it with other open material and share it (the “5 Rs”), within the terms of its license below. CHEM 3B sections that use it, together with the free lab manual and the free study tools, qualify as zero-textbook-cost sections under California Education Code section 66406.9. A printed copy may be offered at cost, but it is optional.

How this book is organized

Each chapter opens with a landing page: a “Your Route Through Chapter N” box with a readiness check and a suggested study path, and the start of that chapter’s part of Rosa’s Story. After the landing page come three kinds of pages.

  • Section pages (for example 4.1–4.10) carry the core reading. Most begin with a section adapted from an open textbook (see Sources, below) and edited for CHEM 3B, so that its wording, conventions and scope match what you will be assessed on. Each of these pages then has a Lecture Connection: what CHEM 3B emphasizes about that topic, with worked examples, figures, Professor Tips and clinical connections. Ten sections that were empty placeholders in the source books were written new for this course (4.10, 7.11, 11.7, 12.5, 13.6–13.9, 14.5 and 15.2).
  • Chapter Guide pages are the study guide for the chapter: learning objectives mapped to the course outline, short Key Ideas with pointers to the sections, Professor Tips, exam-style worked examples in Problem/Solution/Check form, common mistakes, video lessons from the instructor's CHEM 3B playlist, and interactive Try It practice. Each guide also carries one part of Rosa’s Story, a running patient case: a fictional adult with type 2 diabetes whose dental and medical care brings in that chapter’s chemistry, from the polarity of her medicines in Chapter 1 to her ketones and BUN in Chapter 15.
  • Practice Problems and Practice Games pages close each chapter. The problems are original to this book and include multiple choice, compare-and-contrast, data analysis and clinical-application items, all with worked answers behind an “Answer” toggle. Each Practice Problems page also names the chapter’s graded homework in LibreTexts ADAPT (see How to use it, below). The games pages embed the free study games from Dr. Dan's Chemistry Study Hub that fit the chapter.

Valley Connection boxes (green, at least one per chapter) tie the chemistry to life in the San Joaquin Valley and to the health careers most CHEM 3B students are headed into. Each one cites its sources and ends with a “Think about it” question: 1.8 tule fog; 2.7 ozone and smog; 3.4 ethylene and fruit ripening; 4.5 Valley wine grapes and how the liver oxidizes ethanol; 5.2 aldehydes in wildfire smoke; 6.5 tartaric acid in raisins and esters in peaches; 7.3 dairy ammonia and ammonium nitrate haze; 8.6 lactose, Tulare County dairy and tooth decay; 9.2 almond oil versus butterfat; 10.9 nitrate in well water and “blue baby” hemoglobin; 10.10 heat illness in outdoor workers; 7.11 organophosphate pesticides and farmworker health; 11.4 organophosphate pesticides and acetylcholinesterase; 11.5 almonds and vitamin E; 12.9 PCR testing for Valley fever; 13.9 diabetes and how a glucose meter works; 14.4 carbon monoxide and Complex IV; 15.5 diabetic ketoacidosis. The Valley Connection boxes are original to this book and are licensed CC BY 4.0, including where they appear on pages adapted from CC BY-NC-SA sources.

How to use it

  1. Read the section pages before lecture, including the Lecture Connection at the bottom of each one; use the Chapter Guide's Key Ideas to check what you should be able to do.
  2. Work the worked examples with the solution covered, then open it and compare.
  3. Do the Try It sets as you go. Progress is saved only in your browser; nothing is graded or tracked.
  4. Work the Practice Problems. They are not graded, so use them to find what you still need to review.
  5. Then do the chapter’s homework in LibreTexts ADAPT. This is the graded, auto-checked homework; the box at the top of each Practice Problems page names the assignment. If your instructor uses it, open it from your course in Canvas so your score reaches the gradebook.
  6. Use the Practice Games page for extra practice. If a problem is hard, the matching game gives you unlimited fresh versions of the same skill.

If you work, care for family, or are still learning English. You do not have to read a whole chapter at once. Each section page is short enough to read in one sitting, so do one or two sections at a time and the Try It set for each. When time is short, start with the Chapter Guide’s Key Ideas, the short version of the chapter. Every term in the Glossary has a plain-English definition and its Spanish equivalent (Español). Every video has captions (use the CC button), and YouTube can translate the captions into Spanish. Your browser’s read-aloud and translate tools also work on these pages.

Professor Tip

The clinical boxes are not extra reading. Exam questions in a health-science organic course are increasingly written as scenarios, so practice translating a story about a patient into the functional group, property or pathway it is really asking about.

What was changed (adaptation log). Compared with the source texts, this book:

  • reorders the sections into the fifteen-chapter sequence of the CHEM 3B outline and adds a bonding-review chapter at the start;
  • appends a Lecture Connection block to every adapted section (course emphasis, worked examples, figures, Professor Tips, nursing and dental-hygiene connections);
  • edits the adapted section text (November 2026) so it matches this course: errors in the sources were corrected in place (for example the reversed transcription directions in Section 12.4, the outdated advice to induce vomiting after poisoning in Section 10.10, and the description of phenylalanine → tyrosine as a reduction in Section 15.11); older conventions were updated (2.5 ATP per NADH and 1.5 per FADH2 throughout the ATP tables, “cytosol,” IUPAC names alongside common names, course definitions of cofactor, point mutation and protein); and material beyond the course outline was removed or shortened (reaction mechanisms, oxidation numbers, LiAlH4 reductions, pKa values and the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, E/Z names and conformers). Every edited page says so in its attribution note;
  • also: some source figures were replaced by original structure and pathway figures drawn for this book (glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, β-oxidation, the urea cycle, the electron transport chain, ATP synthase and key reactions in Chapters 13–15), and every source figure that carried a ShareAlike (CC BY-SA) license was replaced with an original figure (for example in Sections 5.3, 5.4, 9.4, 10.7 and 12.4), a few errors in the sources were corrected and are noted where they occur (for example the reversed succinate and fumarate labels in Section 13.1), and source headings were renumbered so heading levels run in order for screen readers;
  • replaces ten empty placeholder sections with new, original sections;
  • adds a Chapter Guide, a Practice Problems page and a Practice Games page to every chapter, all original;
  • adds original figures (lecture-slide figures, structure and reaction drawings, pathway diagrams) with text descriptions, and embeds the instructor's own videos and practice tools;
  • standardizes conventions used on CHEM 3B exams where the source books differ (for example ATP yields of 2.5 per NADH and 1.5 per FADH2, “cytosol” rather than “cytoplasm”, and the enzyme-class and inhibitor vocabulary used in lecture); these conventions are now written into the section text itself.

No content from commercial textbooks, test banks, homework sets or exams was used. All practice problems and examples are original.

Accessibility statement

This book is built to meet the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.1 at level AA. Every figure, including every drawn structure and pathway diagram and all 875 figures inherited from the source textbooks (whose descriptions were rewritten for this book), carries a text description for screen readers and a visible caption; tables have header rows and captions; headings follow a consistent outline; formulas are written as text, not images; meaning is never carried by color alone; links are descriptive; and hidden answers use a standard disclosure control that works with the keyboard and with screen readers. Every page was checked with an automated WCAG 2.1 A/AA scan (axe-core) before publication, and the dense lecture-slide figures have longer descriptions that give a screen-reader user the same information a sighted student gets. The Try It widgets and games are operated with ordinary buttons and text fields, so they work from the keyboard, and each one has an “Open in a new tab” link for a larger view; the games have no required timed mode.

If you find a barrier — a figure without a description, a control you cannot reach from the keyboard, a video without accurate captions — tell your instructor, or report it at github.com/ItsDrDan/textbook/issues, so it can be fixed in the book rather than worked around. Students who need materials in another format can also contact Fresno City College's Disabled Students Programs and Services.

A note on medical content

The clinical connections explain the chemistry behind things you will see in practice. They are written at the level of a first chemistry course, not as clinical guidance, and they do not give treatment decisions or doses. Your health-program courses and your clinical instructors are the authority on patient care.
