Learning Objectives

  • To identify the different molecules that combine to form nucleotides.

The repeating, or monomer, units that are linked together to form nucleic acids are known as nucleotides. The deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) of a typical mammalian cell contains about 6 billion base pairs: two sets of about 3 billion, one set from each parent. Each nucleotide is made of a phosphate group (it becomes phosphoric acid, H3PO4, when a nucleotide is hydrolyzed in acid, but in the cell it is a negatively charged phosphate ester), a pentose sugar (a sugar with five carbon atoms), and a nitrogenous base (a base containing nitrogen atoms).

\[\mathrm{nucleic\: acids \underset{down\: into}{\xrightarrow{can\: be\: broken}} nucleotides \underset{down\: into}{\xrightarrow{can\: be\: broken}} H_3PO_4 + nitrogen\: base + pentose\: sugar} \nonumber \]

If the pentose sugar is ribose, the nucleotide is more specifically referred to as a ribonucleotide, and the resulting nucleic acid is ribonucleic acid (RNA). If the sugar is 2-deoxyribose, the nucleotide is a deoxyribonucleotide, and the nucleic acid is DNA.

The nitrogenous bases found in nucleotides are classified as pyrimidines or purines. Pyrimidines are heterocyclic amines with two nitrogen atoms in a six-member ring and include uracil, thymine, and cytosine. Purines are heterocyclic amines consisting of a pyrimidine ring fused to a five-member ring with two nitrogen atoms. Adenine and guanine are the major purines found in nucleic acids (Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\)).

The formation of a bond between C1′ of the pentose sugar and N1 of the pyrimidine base or N9 of the purine base joins the pentose sugar to the nitrogenous base. In the formation of this bond, a molecule of water is removed. Table \(\PageIndex{1}\) summarizes the similarities and differences in the composition of nucleotides in DNA and RNA.

The numbering convention is that primed numbers designate the atoms of the pentose ring, and unprimed numbers designate the atoms of the purine or pyrimidine ring.

Table \(\PageIndex{1}\): Composition of Nucleotides in DNA and RNA
Composition DNA RNA
purine bases adenine and guanine adenine and guanine
pyrimidine bases cytosine and thymine cytosine and uracil
pentose sugar 2-deoxyribose ribose
inorganic acid phosphoric acid (H3PO4) H3PO4

The names and structures of the major ribonucleotides and one of the deoxyribonucleotides are given in Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\).

Apart from being the monomer units of DNA and RNA, the nucleotides and some of their derivatives have other functions as well. Adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and adenosine triphosphate (ATP), shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{3}\), have a role in cell metabolism. Moreover, a number of coenzymes, including flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD), nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+), and coenzyme A, contain adenine nucleotides as structural components.

Summary

Nucleotides are composed of a phosphate group, a pentose sugar (ribose or deoxyribose), and a nitrogen-containing base (adenine, cytosine, guanine, thymine, or uracil). Ribonucleotides contain ribose, while deoxyribonucleotides contain deoxyribose.

Lecture Connection

A nucleotide is three parts, base + sugar + phosphate, and every lecture question about one comes down to three questions: which base (purine or pyrimidine), which sugar (ribose or deoxyribose), and how many phosphates. The chemistry is all review: heterocyclic amines (Chapter 7), cyclic hemiacetal sugars (Chapter 8) and phosphate esters (Chapter 6).

The five bases and the two sugars

  • Purines (two fused rings, one six-membered and one five-membered): adenine (A), guanine (G). You saw the purine ring among the heterocyclic amines in Chapter 7.
  • Pyrimidines (one six-membered ring): cytosine (C), thymine (T, DNA only), uracil (U, RNA only). Thymine is simply uracil with a methyl group on C5. Two lecture mnemonics: "Pure As Gold" (purines are A and G) and "Pyramids are built for U To C" (pyrimidines are U, T and C).
  • The sugar is a five-membered cyclic hemiacetal (a furanose, Chapter 8). DNA uses 2-deoxyribose (an H in place of the OH on C2′). RNA uses ribose (an OH on C2′). The sugar carbons are numbered with primes (1′ to 5′) so they are never confused with the ring atoms of the base.

Nucleoside versus nucleotide, and how to name them

Join a base to the anomeric carbon (C1′) of the sugar through the base's nitrogen (N9 of a purine, N1 of a pyrimidine), a condensation that loses water, and you have a nucleoside. The bond is a β-N-glycosidic bond, the nitrogen cousin of the glycosidic bond in disaccharides (Chapter 8). Add one or more phosphate groups to the C5′-OH as a phosphate ester (Chapter 6) and you have a nucleotide. The spelling helps: nucleoside has the S of base and sugar; nucleotide adds the T of phosphate. The names follow a pattern:

  • A nucleoside from a pyrimidine base ends in -idine (cytidine, thymidine, uridine); one from a purine base ends in -osine (adenosine, guanosine).
  • If the sugar is deoxyribose, add the prefix deoxy- (deoxyadenosine) and a lowercase d to the abbreviation (dAMP).
  • For the nucleotide, add 5′-monophosphate (or di-, tri-) to the nucleoside name: adenosine 5′-monophosphate, AMP.

Adding a second and third phosphate gives the diphosphate and triphosphate: AMP → ADP → ATP. The phosphate–phosphate links in ADP and ATP are anhydride bonds that release a large amount of energy when hydrolyzed; that is why ATP is the cell's energy currency (Chapter 13). The same nucleoside triphosphates are also the building blocks that polymerases use to make DNA (dATP, dGTP, dCTP, dTTP) and RNA (ATP, GTP, CTP, UTP).

Professor Tip

Three parts, three questions. For any nucleotide ask: (1) Which base, purine or pyrimidine? (2) Which sugar, ribose (2′-OH present) or deoxyribose? (3) How many phosphates? Those three answers name it. "Deoxyguanosine triphosphate" tells you guanine, deoxyribose, three phosphates: dGTP, a DNA building block.

Worked example

Example 1: Name the nucleotide

Problem: A molecule contains guanine, a sugar with no OH on C2′, and three phosphate groups on C5′. Name it, give its abbreviation, and say what it is used for.

Solution

Guanine on a sugar is guanosine; no 2′-OH means the sugar is deoxyribose, so the nucleoside is deoxyguanosine. Three phosphates make it a triphosphate: deoxyguanosine triphosphate, dGTP. It is one of the four building blocks DNA polymerase uses during replication; the two outer phosphates leave as pyrophosphate when the nucleotide is added to a growing strand.

Check: Base, sugar, phosphate count: all three are in the name. If the sugar had a 2′-OH, the answer would be GTP, an RNA building block (and an energy carrier like ATP).
