Problem 6.8

Esters can hydrogen bond with each other, which gives them high boiling points.

Answer
False. Esters have no O–H, so they cannot hydrogen bond with each other.

Problem 6.9

In water, a carboxylic acid ionizes only slightly, making it a weak acid.

Answer
True. Only a small fraction (often about 1%) of the molecules give up H+ to water to form the carboxylate ion; most stay as the neutral acid, which is why it is a weak acid.

Problem 6.49

An acyl group is R–C(=O)–. Which compound contains an acyl group bonded to an –OR group, making it an ester?

Problem 6.50

What is the IUPAC name of CH3CH(OCH3)CH2COOH?

  1. 2-methoxybutanoic acid
  2. 3-methoxybutanoic acid
  3. 3-hydroxypentanoic acid
  4. methyl 3-hydroxybutanoate
Answer
B. The COOH carbon is C1, so the –OCH3 group sits on C3 of a four-carbon chain. An –OCH3 group is an alkoxy (methoxy) substituent, not a hydroxy group, and its carbon is not part of the chain, so C and D are wrong; D would also require an ester, and this compound has a free COOH.

Problem 6.51

A student heats benzoic acid with ethanol and a drop of sulfuric acid to make ethyl benzoate, but the yield is poor. Which change would raise the amount of ester at equilibrium?

  1. Add more sulfuric acid, because the catalyst is used up during the reaction.
  2. Add water to dissolve the benzoic acid.
  3. Use a large excess of ethanol.
  4. Replace the sulfuric acid with NaOH.
Answer
C. Fischer esterification is reversible, so by Le Chatelier's principle adding excess alcohol (or removing water) shifts the equilibrium toward the ester. A catalyst is not consumed, so more of it does not change the equilibrium position (A). Water is a product, so adding it pushes the reaction backward toward hydrolysis (B). NaOH would simply neutralize the benzoic acid to sodium benzoate, and the carboxylate ion does not form esters (D).

Problem 6.52

Four unlabeled samples are each shaken with sodium bicarbonate solution. Only one fizzes. Which one?

  1. phenol
  2. 1-butanol
  3. butanoic acid
  4. butanone
Answer
C. Carboxylic acids are strong enough to protonate bicarbonate, giving carbonic acid that breaks down to CO2 gas and water: CH3CH2CH2COOH + NaHCO3 → CH3CH2CH2COO−Na+ + H2O + CO2. Phenol is a much weaker acid and does not react with bicarbonate; an alcohol and a ketone are not acids in water at all.

Problem 6.53

Compare and contrast. Methyl salicylate (oil of wintergreen) is hydrolyzed two ways: (1) heated with water and a trace of HCl; (2) heated with excess NaOH. For each, give the organic products and state whether the reaction is reversible. Then explain why only one of the two methods needs a second step to obtain salicylic acid itself.

Answer
(1) Acid-catalyzed hydrolysis: methyl salicylate + H2O \(\rightleftharpoons\) salicylic acid (2-hydroxybenzoic acid) + methanol. The H+ is a catalyst and the reaction is reversible (it is Fischer esterification run backward), so excess water is used to drive it. (2) Saponification: the NaOH is a reactant, consumed, and the products are sodium salicylate (the carboxylate salt) + methanol; the reaction is not reversible because a carboxylate ion will not react with an alcohol. Only method 2 needs a second step: adding a strong acid such as HCl protonates the carboxylate, RCOO−Na+ + HCl → RCOOH + NaCl, to give the free salicylic acid. (With excess NaOH the phenol –OH is deprotonated too, so the acid step restores both groups.)

Problem 6.54

Predict. Three compounds each contain five carbons: pentanoic acid, sodium pentanoate, and ethyl propanoate. (a) Rank them by water solubility, least to most, and explain each placement in terms of intermolecular forces. (b) Which has the highest boiling point among the two that are molecular (not ionic), and why?

Answer
(a) ethyl propanoate < pentanoic acid < sodium pentanoate. The ester has two oxygens that can accept hydrogen bonds from water but no O–H to donate, and most of the molecule is hydrocarbon, so it is only slightly soluble. Pentanoic acid can both donate and accept hydrogen bonds, so it is moderately soluble (about 5 g per 100 mL), although its four-carbon tail limits it. Sodium pentanoate is an ionic compound; ion–dipole attractions between Na+, the carboxylate ion, and water make it freely soluble, which is why acids are converted to their salts for aqueous products. (b) Pentanoic acid. It forms hydrogen-bonded dimers, which the ester cannot do, so the acid boils far higher (187 °C versus 99 °C for ethyl propanoate, which has the same molar mass of 102 g/mol).

Analyze the table. The table lists several acids that appear in this chapter.

Problem 6.56

Analyze the data. Four compounds have nearly the same molar mass (86–88 g/mol).

Boiling points of four compounds of nearly the same molar mass
CompoundFunctional groupMolar mass (g/mol)Boiling point (°C)

(a) Explain the order of the last three in terms of the strongest intermolecular force each compound can form with itself. (b) Methyl propanoate also has a molar mass of 88 g/mol. Predict its boiling point to within about 10 °C and justify your estimate.

Answer
(a) Pentanal has a polar C=O but no O–H, so its molecules attract one another only by dipole–dipole forces (plus dispersion). 1-Pentanol has one O–H and forms single hydrogen bonds, raising the boiling point by 35 °C. More Practice: Clinical and Integrative

Problem 6.57

Nursing. While restocking a medication cart, you open a bottle of aspirin tablets that has been stored in the humid bathroom of a patient's home. It smells sharply of vinegar. (a) Aspirin is acetylsalicylic acid. Identify the two oxygen-containing functional groups in the molecule. (b) Write a word equation for the reaction that produced the smell and name the type of reaction. (c) Which product is responsible for the smell, and what does the other product look like structurally? (d) Why should the tablets be discarded even though the drug has only partly reacted?

Answer
(a) A carboxylic acid (–COOH on the ring) and an ester (the ring oxygen bonded to an acetyl group, –O–C(=O)CH3). (b) Aspirin + water → salicylic acid + acetic acid. It is a hydrolysis of the ester; moisture from the air supplied the water, and the acid groups present catalyze it slowly. (c) Acetic acid, the acid in vinegar, is volatile and causes the smell. The other product, salicylic acid (2-hydroxybenzoic acid), has a phenol –OH where the ester used to be, next to the carboxylic acid. (d) The amount of active drug per tablet is no longer what the label states, and salicylic acid is more irritating to the stomach lining than aspirin; a vinegar smell is the standard sign that the product has degraded.

Problem 6.58

Nursing. A patient in septic shock has a blood lactate of 6 mmol/L (normal is under 2) and an arterial pH of 7.21. (a) Draw the condensed structure of lactic acid and give its IUPAC name. (b) The lab reports "lactate," not "lactic acid." Explain why. (c) Write the ionization equation and use it to explain how producing lactic acid lowers blood pH. (d) The body's main blood buffer is bicarbonate. Write the equation showing how HCO3− takes up the proton released by lactic acid, and name the condition the patient has.

Answer
(a) CH3CH(OH)COOH, 2-hydroxypropanoic acid. (b) Lactic acid is a carboxylic acid, and at blood pH (7.2–7.4) a carboxylic acid is ionized: essentially all of the molecules have lost their carboxyl proton and exist as the lactate ion, CH3CH(OH)COO−; that is the species the assay measures. (c) CH3CH(OH)COOH + H2O \(\rightleftharpoons\) CH3CH(OH)COO− + H3O+. Each lactic acid molecule made by oxygen-starved tissue releases one H+ into the blood; more H+ means a lower pH. (d) HCO3− + H+ → H2CO3 → CO2 + H2O (the CO2 is breathed off). When acid is produced faster than the buffer and lungs can handle, the pH falls below 7.35: metabolic acidosis, here specifically lactic acidosis.

Problem 6.59

Dental hygiene. A patient with a new acrylic denture reports a burning sensation on the palate. The denture base is PMMA, made by polymerizing methyl methacrylate, CH2=C(CH3)COOCH3, and a small amount of unreacted monomer can remain in a freshly processed appliance. (a) Name the two functional groups in methyl methacrylate. (b) What carboxylic acid and alcohol would give this ester by Fischer esterification? (c) Which functional group is used up when the monomer becomes PMMA, and what type of reaction is that (Chapter 3)? (d) The usual advice is to soak the new denture in water for 24 hours before wearing it. Explain, using what you know about ester solubility, why this helps.

Answer
(a) An alkene (C=C) and an ester (–COOCH3). (b) 2-Methylpropenoic acid (methacrylic acid), CH2=C(CH3)COOH, and methanol, CH3OH. (c) The C=C is consumed; the monomers join by addition polymerization, giving a long carbon chain with the ester groups hanging off it. The ester groups are unchanged in the polymer. (d) Methyl methacrylate is a small ester whose C=O oxygens accept hydrogen bonds from water, so it is slightly water soluble (about 1.5 g per 100 mL). Soaking lets the leftover monomer leach out of the acrylic into the water instead of into the patient's saliva and mucosa, which reduces the irritation; the polymer itself is insoluble and stays put.

Problem 6.60

Dental hygiene. Two patients show acid damage to enamel. Patient A sips a sports drink that contains citric acid (pH about 3) throughout every workout. Patient B has heavy plaque and a high-sugar diet. (a) For each patient, name the carboxylic acid attacking the enamel and state where it comes from. (b) Enamel begins to dissolve below the critical pH of about 5.5. Explain, using the behavior of weak acids, why a drink at pH 3 keeps dissolving enamel even after saliva has neutralized some of its acid. (c) Chewing sugar-free gum after eating stimulates saliva, which contains bicarbonate. Write the reaction between lactic acid and bicarbonate ion. (d) Why is the gum sweetened with xylitol rather than sucrose?

Answer
(a) Patient A: citric acid, added to the drink (dental erosion, no bacteria needed). Patient B: lactic acid, produced by plaque bacteria that ferment sugars by glycolysis (dental caries). (b) A weak acid is a reservoir of H+: only a small fraction is ionized at any moment, and as saliva removes H+, more acid molecules ionize to replace it (Le Chatelier's principle). A drink at pH 3 contains far more total acid than its free H+ suggests, so the pH stays below 5.5 for many minutes after each sip, and frequent sipping keeps enamel below the critical pH almost continuously. (c) CH3CH(OH)COOH + HCO3− → CH3CH(OH)COO− + H2O + CO2; the acid is converted to lactate and the pH rises back above 5.5, allowing remineralization. (d) Xylitol is a sugar alcohol that plaque bacteria cannot ferment to lactic acid, so it sweetens without feeding the acid-producing bacteria; sucrose would.

Problem 6.61

Integrative (Chapters 4–6). Start with 1-butanol. (a) Write the product of oxidizing it with excess oxidizing agent (Chapter 4), and classify the alcohol as 1°, 2°, or 3°. (b) Heat the product of (a) with ethanol and an acid catalyst; draw and name the ester. (c) Treat the ester from (b) with NaOH and heat; name the products. (d) Could 2-methyl-2-propanol have been used as the starting alcohol in (a) to make a different acid? Explain.

Answer
(a) 1-Butanol is a 1° alcohol; with excess oxidizing agent it is oxidized through butanal to butanoic acid, CH3CH2CH2COOH. (b) Fischer esterification: CH3CH2CH2COOH + CH3CH2OH \(\rightleftharpoons\) CH3CH2CH2COOCH2CH3 + H2O, ethyl butanoate (the pineapple-scented ester). (c) Saponification gives sodium butanoate, CH3CH2CH2COO−Na+, and ethanol. (d) No. 2-Methyl-2-propanol is a 3° alcohol; the carbon bearing the –OH has no hydrogen to lose, so it cannot be oxidized to an aldehyde or acid. Only 1° alcohols give carboxylic acids on oxidation.

Problem 6.62

Integrative and clinical. A dental patient reports a past allergic reaction to a "-caine" numbing gel. Benzocaine is ethyl 4-aminobenzoate; procaine (an injectable anesthetic no longer in common use) is also an ester of 4-aminobenzoic acid; lidocaine is an amide. (a) Draw benzocaine from its name and label the ester. (b) Write the products of its hydrolysis by esterase enzymes in the tissues. (c) Explain why a patient who reacted to benzocaine might also react to procaine but is unlikely to react to lidocaine for the same reason. (d) Nitroglycerin, used for chest pain, is often called an "ester" too. What acid is it an ester of, and how does that differ from the esters in this chapter?

Answer
(a) A benzene ring with –NH2 on C4 and –COOCH2CH3 on C1; the ester is the C(=O)–O–CH2CH3 unit attached to the ring (condensed: H2N–C6H4–COOCH2CH3). (b) 4-Aminobenzoic acid (PABA), H2N–C6H4–COOH, and ethanol. At body pH the PABA is present mainly as its carboxylate. (c) Both benzocaine and procaine are esters of PABA, so both release PABA when hydrolyzed, and PABA (or a derivative of it) is the usual trigger of true allergy to ester anesthetics. Lidocaine is an amide of a different acid; it is hydrolyzed by different enzymes, more slowly, and never produces PABA, so the PABA-based allergy does not carry over. (d) Nitroglycerin is the triester of glycerol with nitric acid (HNO3), so its ester linkages are O–NO2 rather than O–C(=O)R. It is an ester in the general sense (an acid condensed with an alcohol, like the phosphate esters) but not a carboxylic ester, so it is not hydrolyzed to a carboxylic acid.

Problem 6.65

Classify each pair as skeletal isomers, positional isomers, functional group isomers, or the same compound: (a) hexanoic acid and 4-methylpentanoic acid; (b) propyl ethanoate and ethyl propanoate; (c) butanoic acid and ethyl ethanoate; (d) CH3COOCH2CH3 and CH3CH2OOCCH3; (e) 2-methylbutanoic acid and 3-methylbutanoic acid.

Answer
(a) Skeletal: both C6H12O2 acids, straight vs. branched chain. (b) Positional: both are five-carbon C5H10O2 esters with the ester link in a different place along the chain. (c) Functional group: same formula, C4H8O2, but one is an acid and the other an ester. (d) Same compound: ethyl ethanoate written from either end. (e) Positional: both are C5H10O2 acids with a four-carbon chain ending in the COOH group (always C1); only the position of the methyl branch differs (C2 vs. C3).

Problem 6.66

Which statement about isomers of carboxylic acids is correct?

  1. Carboxylic acids can have positional isomers because the COOH can be on any carbon.
  2. Carboxylic acids cannot have skeletal isomers.
  3. A carboxylic acid and an ester with the same molecular formula are functional group isomers.
  4. A carboxylic acid can be part of a ring, like a cyclic ester.
Answer
C. Butanoic acid and methyl propanoate, for example, are both C4H8O2. A is wrong because the COOH is always terminal (C1); B is wrong because branching gives skeletal isomers once there are four or more carbons; D is wrong because a terminal group cannot sit inside a ring, whereas the single-bonded O of an ester can.

Problem 6.67

Name each compound: (a) a benzene ring with a COOH on one carbon and a chlorine on the carbon directly across the ring; (b) a benzene ring with COOH on one carbon and a methyl group on the carbon next to it; (c) CH3CH2CH(Br)COOCH3; (d) CH3COOCH2CH2CH2Cl.

Problem 6.69

Problem 6.71

Cumulative Problem 6.2 (Chapters 1 and 6)

Cumulative Problem 6.4 (Chapters 1, 4 and 6)

Butanoic acid, 1-pentanol and methyl propanoate all have a mass of about 88 g/mol. Rank them by boiling point and explain.

Answer
Butanoic acid (163 °C) > 1-pentanol (138 °C) > methyl propanoate (80 °C). (c) The CH3CH2C(=O)O– part comes from the acid; the CH3 on the oxygen comes from methanol. (d) Hydrolysis: heating the ester with water and acid reverses the reaction, and heating it with NaOH (saponification) gives sodium propanoate and methanol. Condensation and hydrolysis are the pair you will use for fats, peptides and nucleic acids.

Cumulative Problem 6.4 (Chapters 1, 4 and 6)

Butanoic acid (163 °C) > 1-pentanol (138 °C) > methyl propanoate (80 °C). A carboxylic acid can hydrogen bond at both its O–H and its C=O and forms hydrogen-bonded pairs (dimers), so it boils highest; the alcohol hydrogen bonds through one O–H; the ester has polar bonds but no O–H, so only dipole–dipole forces hold it together. Esters are the low-boiling, fruity-smelling members of the family, which is why they reach your nose from a ripe pear or banana.

Cumulative Problem 6.5 (Chapters 4, 5 and 6)

(a) A carboxylic acid and a 2° alcohol (the OH carbon is bonded to the CH3 carbon and the COOH carbon). (b) Yes. The middle carbon holds H, OH, CH3 and COOH, four different groups; the body makes and uses only the L form. (c) Oxidizing the 2° alcohol gives a ketone: CH3COCOOH, pyruvic acid, which has a ketone and a carboxylic acid. This single oxidation (lactate → pyruvate) is a real reaction in your liver, run by lactate dehydrogenase (Chapter 13).

Cumulative Problem 6.6 (Chapters 3, 4 and 6)

(a) An aromatic ring (Chapter 3), a carboxylic acid and an ester (an acetate ester of a phenol). (b) Moisture hydrolyzes the ester: aspirin + H2O → salicylic acid (the ring with COOH and a phenol OH) + acetic acid. Acetic acid is the vinegar smell; salicylic acid irritates the stomach, which is one reason to discard old aspirin. (c) Converting the carboxylic acid to its carboxylate salt makes it far more water soluble, so the tablet dissolves and is absorbed faster.
