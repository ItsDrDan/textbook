Learning Objectives

  • Define and understand the structure of sphingolipids, their subclasses, and their role in the nervous system.
  • Understand the role of sphingolipids in multiple sclerosis.

Sphingolipids and their subclasses

Sphingolipids are lipids built on a long-chain amino alcohol, usually sphingosine, shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\). When the amino (\(\ce{-NH2}\)) group is attached to a fatty acid by an amide bond, it is called ceramide. When the primary alcohol of ceramide is linked to a phosphorylcholine or phosphorylethanolamine group, it is called sphingomyelin. When the primary alcohol of ceramide is bound to glucose or galactose by a glycosidic bond, it is called cerebroside. When a glycosidic bond connects the primary alcohol of ceramide to an oligosaccharide with one or more sialic acids, it is called ganglioside.

Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\) illustrates two examples of sphingomyelin. Like glycerophospholipids, these sphingolipids are an essential component of the lipid bilayer. They are especially abundant in the white matter of the brain and in the myelin sheath, the coating that wraps around nerve-cell axons. The myelin sheath speeds up nerve impulses and protects nerve cells.

Multiple sclerosis

Multiple sclerosis is a nervous system disease in which the myelin sheaths wrapped around axons of nerve cells are damaged, as illustrated in Figure \(\PageIndex{3}\). Damaged myelin slows or blocks nerve signals. This can reduce sensation, coordination, movement and thinking, and can cause muscle weakness and vision loss. The severity of the effects depends on the amount of damage. Studies indicate that vitamin D may lessen the severity of the disease. Nearly 1 million people in the US and about 2.8 million worldwide have multiple sclerosis.

Lecture Connection

In lecture a sphingolipid is the membrane lipid with the other backbone: sphingosine instead of glycerol, and its one fatty acid attached by an amide bond instead of an ester. That amide is the giveaway on an exam drawing, and it is still hydrolyzable.

Sphingomyelin and the myelin sheath

The backbone is sphingosine, an 18-carbon amino alcohol, instead of glycerol. One fatty acid is attached through an amide bond (not an ester), and a polar head sits on the terminal OH. In sphingomyelin the head is phosphate + choline (or ethanolamine), so it looks and behaves much like lecithin even though it contains no ester; it is the main lipid of the myelin sheath, the insulating wrap around nerve axons that speeds nerve impulses. In multiple sclerosis sphingomyelin is lost from the sheath, the sheath deteriorates, and scarring of the neurons impairs signal transmission. When the head is a sugar instead of a phosphate, the molecule is a glycolipid: a cerebroside carries one monosaccharide (glucose or galactose) and is abundant in brain and nerve membranes. Glycolipids have no phosphate; that is the quickest way to tell them from sphingomyelin.

Professor Tip

For exams you need two sphingolipids: sphingomyelin (phosphate–choline head; myelin; multiple sclerosis) and the cerebroside (one sugar as the head; a glycolipid). Ceramide and ganglioside are names you may read but will not be asked to define. Classify any sphingolipid as a hydrolyzable lipid: the amide and the phosphate or glycosidic bonds all split with water.

Professor Tip

A common mistake is to write "ester" for the bond holding the fatty acid in sphingomyelin. Sphingosine has an amine on C2, so the fatty acid's C=O is bonded to N: an amide, the same link as in proteins and the local anesthetics of Chapter 7. Find the C=O, look at the neighbor: N means sphingolipid, O means glycerolipid.

Summary

  • Sphingolipids are built on sphingosine, an amino alcohol, with a fatty acid joined by an amide bond.
  • Sphingomyelin has a phosphate–choline head and is a major lipid of the myelin sheath around nerve cells; damage to myelin occurs in multiple sclerosis.
  • A cerebroside has a single sugar as its head and is a glycolipid.
  • All sphingolipids are hydrolyzable: their amide and phosphate or glycosidic bonds split with water.

Practice 9.4

Lipid X is hydrolyzed completely. The products are sphingosine, one fatty acid, phosphate and choline. Lipid Y gives sphingosine, one fatty acid and galactose.

  1. Name lipid X. Where in the body is it most abundant?
  2. What type of bond holds the fatty acid in lipid X? How is this different from the bond that holds the fatty acids in a triacylglycerol?
  3. Name lipid Y and the larger group of lipids it belongs to. What is the quickest way to tell Y from X?
Answer
  1. Sphingomyelin. The backbone is sphingosine and the head is phosphate plus choline. It is the main lipid of the myelin sheath that wraps nerve axons.
  2. An amide bond. Sphingosine has an amine on C2, so the C=O of the fatty acid is bonded to N. In a triacylglycerol the C=O is bonded to an O of glycerol, an ester.
  3. A cerebroside, which is a glycolipid. Its head is one sugar (galactose) and it has no phosphate. Sphingomyelin has a phosphate.

Check. Count the parts. X has four (sphingosine, fatty acid, phosphate, choline) and Y has three (sphingosine, fatty acid, sugar). Both have only one fatty acid and no glycerol, so neither is a glycerophospholipid. Both were split by water, so both are hydrolyzable lipids.
