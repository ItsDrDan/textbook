Learning Objectives

  • Name the typical reactions that take place with carboxylic acids.
  • Describe how carboxylic acids react with basic compounds.

Water-soluble carboxylic acids ionize slightly in water to form moderately acidic solutions.

\[\mathrm{RCOOH + H_2O\rightleftharpoons RCOO^{-}+H_3O^+} \nonumber\]

Their aqueous solutions exhibit the typical properties of acids, such as changing litmus from blue to red.

The anion formed when a carboxylic acid dissociates is called the carboxylate anion (RCOO−).

Whether soluble in water or not, carboxylic acids react with aqueous solutions of sodium hydroxide (NaOH), sodium carbonate (Na2CO3), and sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3) to form salts:

RCOOH + NaOH(aq) → RCOO−Na+(aq) + H2O

2RCOOH + Na2CO3(aq) → 2RCOO−Na+(aq) + H2O + CO2(g)

RCOOH + NaHCO3(aq) → RCOO−Na+(aq) + H2O + CO2(g)

In these reactions, the carboxylic acids act like inorganic acids: they neutralize basic compounds. With solutions of carbonate (\(CO_3^{2–}\)) and bicarbonate (\(HCO_3^{–}\)) ions, they also form carbon dioxide gas.

Carboxylic acid salts are named in the same manner as inorganic salts: the name of the cation is followed by the name of the organic anion. The name of the anion is obtained by dropping the -ic ending of the acid name and replacing it with the suffix -ate. This rule applies whether we are using common names or International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) names:

The salts of long-chain carboxylic acids are called soaps. We discuss the chemistry of soaps elsewhere.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Write an equation for each reaction.

  1. the ionization of propionic acid in water (H2O)
  2. the neutralization of propionic acid with aqueous sodium hydroxide (NaOH)

Solution

Propionic acid has three carbon atoms, so its formula is CH3CH2COOH.

  1. Propionic acid ionizes in water to form a propionate ion and a hydronium (H3O+) ion. CH3CH2COOH(aq) + H2O(ℓ) \(\rightleftharpoons\) CH3CH2COO−(aq) + H3O+(aq)
  2. Propionic acid reacts with NaOH(aq) to form sodium propionate and water. CH3CH2COOH(aq) + NaOH(aq) → CH3CH2COO−Na+(aq) + H2O(ℓ)

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Write an equation for the reaction of decanoic acid with each compound.

  1. aqueous sodium hydroxide (NaOH)
  2. aqueous sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3)
Solution
  1. Decanoic acid has 10 carbon atoms. It reacts with NaOH to form a salt and water (H2O). CH3(CH2)8COOH + NaOH(aq) → CH3(CH2)8COO−Na+(aq) + H2O(ℓ)
  2. With NaHCO3, the products are a salt, H2O, and carbon dioxide (CO2). CH3(CH2)8COOH + NaHCO3(aq) → CH3(CH2)8COO−Na+(aq) + H2O(ℓ) + CO2(g)

To Your Health: Organic Salts as Preservatives

Some organic salts are used as preservatives in food products. They prevent spoilage by inhibiting the growth of bacteria and fungi. Calcium and sodium propionate, for example, are added to processed cheese and bakery goods; sodium benzoate is added to cider, jellies, pickles, and syrups; and sodium sorbate and potassium sorbate are added to fruit juices, sauerkraut, soft drinks, and wine. Look for them on ingredient labels the next time you shop for groceries.

Key Takeaways

  • Soluble carboxylic acids are weak acids in aqueous solutions.
  • Carboxylic acids neutralize bases to form salts.

Lecture Connection

Two reactions here, and both are acid–base chemistry from CHEM 3A: a carboxylic acid ionizes slightly in water (weak acid), and it is neutralized completely by a base to give a carboxylate salt. The lecture's payoff is practical: the salt dissolves in water when the acid does not, which is how many drugs are formulated.

Weak acid, strong neutralization

  • Weak acid in water: RCOOH + H2O \(\rightleftharpoons\) RCOO− + H3O+. Carboxylic acids are proton donors, but weak ones compared with inorganic acids such as HCl or H2SO4: only a small percentage is ionized in aqueous solution, so the equation gets equilibrium arrows. Carboxylic acids are the most acidic organic group you have met (Section 6.10 compares them with phenols and alcohols), but still far weaker than the mineral acids.
  • Neutralization: RCOOH + NaOH → RCOO−Na+ + H2O. Carboxylic acids react with bases to form salts, and the acid–base reaction turns a water-insoluble carboxylic acid into a water-soluble carboxylate salt. This one goes to completion (a single arrow).
  • Two bases are used in this course. A strong base such as NaOH or KOH gives the salt and water. Sodium bicarbonate, NaHCO3, also works, and it gives off carbon dioxide gas: RCOOH + NaHCO3 → RCOO−Na+ + H2O + CO2(g). The fizz is a quick lab test for a carboxylic acid, because phenols and alcohols are too weak to react with bicarbonate.

Naming the salt. The carboxylate ion, R–COO−, is named a lot like an ester: metal first, then the acid name with -ic acid changed to -ate: sodium ethanoate (sodium acetate), potassium propanoate, sodium benzoate. The salts of long-chain acids are soaps (Section 6.8).

Carboxylate salts dissolve in water; the acids often do not

A carboxylic acid with more than about five carbons is mostly nonpolar and has low water solubility, but neutralizing it with a base makes an ionic compound, and ion–dipole attractions to water pull the salt into solution. Pharmaceutical and food chemists use this constantly, which is what the section's preservative examples are about:

  • Sodium benzoate is added to soft drinks and liquid medicines as a preservative because it dissolves readily, whereas benzoic acid itself barely dissolves (0.29 g/100 g in the Section 6.3 table).
  • Sodium salicylate and sodium citrate are the water-soluble forms used in solutions and IV preparations.
  • Ibuprofen is a carboxylic acid that is nearly insoluble in water. The salt formed with the basic amino acid lysine, ibuprofen lysine, dissolves well enough to be given intravenously and to be absorbed faster from fast-acting oral products. The drug molecule is unchanged; only its charge has.

Professor Tip

Adding strong acid reverses the neutralization: RCOO−Na+ + HCl → RCOOH + NaCl. That is what happens to a sodium-salt tablet when it reaches stomach acid, and it is also how you recover the free acid after a saponification (Section 6.8). Base makes the salt; acid gives back the acid.

In the Lab

In the carboxylic acids and esters experiment you compare the solubility and acidity of carboxylic acids with their sodium salts: the salts dissolve far better, and the acid turns blue litmus red while the salt solution does not. Bicarbonate fizz tells an acid from a phenol or an alcohol.

Carboxylates in the clinic

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Citrate in blood tubes. The light-blue-top tube used for coagulation tests contains sodium citrate. Citrate, with its three carboxylate groups, binds calcium ions (Ca2+) tightly, and because clotting requires free Ca2+, the blood stays liquid. The same chemistry is why rapid transfusion of citrate-preserved blood can lower blood calcium (hypocalcemia). Citrate is the carboxylate ion of citric acid; at blood pH the acid has already given up its protons (Section 6.10).

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Acid erosion and the critical pH. Enamel is the mineral hydroxyapatite, Ca5(PO4)3OH. Below a mouth pH of about 5.5 it starts to dissolve: Ca5(PO4)3OH + 4 H+ → 5 Ca2+ + 3 HPO42− + H2O. The H+ comes from weak carboxylic acids: lactic acid from plaque bacteria causes caries, while citric acid in juices and sports drinks causes erosion without any bacteria. Weak acids are still acids. Softened enamel is easily brushed away, which is why brushing right after an acidic drink is discouraged; saliva's bicarbonate neutralizes the acid, the same reaction as the bicarbonate test above.
