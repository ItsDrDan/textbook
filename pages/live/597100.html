Learning Objectives

  • Identify and name alcohols, phenols, ethers, and thiols.
  • Classify alcohols as primary (1°), secondary (2°), or tertiary (3°).
  • Use IMFs to compare boiling points and water solubility of alcohols, ethers, and hydrocarbons.
  • Predict products of dehydration and oxidation of alcohols, and oxidation of thiols to disulfides.

Chapter Summary

The big ideas of the chapter, one paragraph each. Each paragraph ends with the section where it is taught in full.

1. The functional groups

Alcohols (R–OH), phenols (OH on a benzene ring), ethers (R–O–R′) and thiols (R–SH) all have an oxygen or sulfur single-bonded to carbon; a C=O belongs to a different family. Learn the endings: -ol = alcohol, -thiol = thiol, phenol = the parent name for an OH on a benzene ring, -oxy = the ether prefix. Naming checklist for alcohols: longest chain containing the OH carbon, drop -e add -ol, number to give the OH the lower number, then prefixes. Cycloalkanols put the OH on C1 (the "1-" is omitted). Phenol is the parent for a ring OH (never "hydroxybenzene"). Ethers: common name "alkyl alkyl ether" (alphabetical); IUPAC name uses the shorter group as an alkoxy prefix on the longer chain. Thiols keep the -e: butanethiol. Details and examples: Sections 4.1, 4.2, 4.7, 4.8 and 4.9.

2. Classifying alcohols

Go to the carbon the OH is on and count the C–C bonds on that carbon: 1 = primary (1°), 2 = secondary (2°), 3 = tertiary (3°). Do not count the oxygen. The class decides the oxidation product (Key Idea 4). Watch for isomers: positional (1- vs 2-butanol), skeletal (2-methyl-1-propanol) and functional group (an alcohol vs an ether, Key Idea 7). Details and examples: Section 4.2.

3. Physical properties

The oxygen is bent (about 109.5°), so all four families are polar. Alcohols and phenols hydrogen bond with each other; ethers and thiols have only dipole–dipole forces, but ethers can accept a hydrogen bond from water or an R–OH. Boiling point at similar mass: alkane < ether < alcohol; stronger forces = higher melting and boiling points. Solubility: if the IMFs look like water's, it dissolves; four carbons or fewer = soluble, and each extra OH helps. Hydrogen bonding with itself controls boiling point; hydrogen bonding with water controls solubility. Details, data tables and examples: Sections 4.3, 4.6 and 4.8.

4. Reactions

Alcohols burn (combustion to CO2 and H2O; count the alcohol's own oxygen), lose water (dehydration, an elimination: H+ and heat give an alkene) and lose hydrogen (oxidation, [O]). Zaitsev's rule: when two alkenes are possible, the major product has the most alkyl groups on the C=C; the more substituted alkene wins (the opposite of Markovnikov). Oxidation follows the class: 1° → aldehyde → carboxylic acid; 2° → ketone; 3° → no reaction. Reduction [H] runs it backward. Thiols oxidize to disulfides: 2 R–SH → R–S–S–R. Details, figures and examples: Sections 4.5 and 4.9.

5. Acidity: alcohols vs. phenols

Alcohol solutions have about the pH of water; alcohols do not react with NaOH. Phenols are weak acids because the ring stabilizes the phenoxide ion, so phenol dissolves in NaOH as sodium phenoxide, a lab test that tells the two apart. Thiols are much more acidic than alcohols, close to phenols, and also react with NaOH; all three are far weaker than carboxylic acids. Details, the ranking table and the NaOH test: Sections 4.7 and 4.10.

6. Hydration and dehydration are reversible

Alkene + H2O \(\rightleftharpoons\) alcohol, with the same H+ catalyst in both directions. By Le Chatelier's principle, excess water and dilute acid push toward the alcohol (hydration); concentrated acid, heat and removal of the alkene pull toward the alkene (dehydration). "H+, heat, concentrated H2SO4" signals dehydration; "H+, H2O" signals hydration (Markovnikov's rule picks the alcohol). Details: Sections 4.4 and 4.5.

7. Functional group isomers

Ethanol and dimethyl ether are both C2H6O: same formula, different functional group. Ask "same functional group?" (no → functional group isomers), then "same carbon skeleton?" (no → skeletal; yes → positional). The ether isomer has no O–H, so it boils far lower and does not oxidize or dehydrate. Details and five of the C4H10O isomers: Sections 4.2 and 4.8.

8. Glycols and glycerol

Glycols are diols with OH groups on adjacent carbons (ethylene glycol, propylene glycol); glycerol is a triol. Each extra OH adds hydrogen bonding, so they are syrupy, miscible with water and high-boiling (ethanol 78 °C, ethylene glycol 198 °C, glycerol 290 °C). Ethylene glycol is toxic because alcohol dehydrogenase oxidizes it to oxalic acid; propylene glycol oxidizes to normal metabolites. Details and the dental and nursing uses: Section 4.6.

Health Career Connection

  • Nursing: 70% isopropyl alcohol swabs denature bacterial proteins (Section 4.4). The liver's alcohol dehydrogenase oxidizes ethanol, methanol and ethylene glycol by the same 1° alcohol pattern; methanol's and ethylene glycol's products are the poisons, and the antidotes work by keeping the enzyme busy (Sections 4.5 and 4.6).
  • Dental hygiene: eugenol and thymol are phenolic antiseptics, xylitol and sorbitol are noncariogenic sugar alcohols, and halitosis is largely methanethiol and other volatile sulfur compounds (Sections 4.6, 4.7 and 4.9).

Worked Examples

Example 1: Name and classify

Problem. Name and classify CH3CH(OH)CH2CH2CH3.

Solution. Five-carbon chain, numbered so the OH is on C2: 2-pentanol. The OH carbon is bonded to 2 other carbons, so it is a secondary (2°) alcohol.

Check. Numbering from the other end would put the OH on C4; 2 is lower, so 2-pentanol is correct.

Example 2: Oxidation

Problem. Give the oxidation product(s) of (a) 1-propanol, (b) cyclohexanol, (c) 2-methyl-2-butanol.

Solution.

(a) 1°: CH3CH2CH2OH → CH3CH2CHO (propanal) → CH3CH2COOH (propanoic acid)

(b) 2°: cyclohexanol → cyclohexanone

(c) 3°: no reaction

Check. Classify first, then predict: 1° goes two steps, 2° stops at the ketone, 3° has no H on the OH carbon to lose.

Example 3: Dehydration

Problem. What is the major product when 3-methyl-2-butanol, CH3CH(OH)CH(CH3)CH3, is dehydrated?

Solution. H can be removed from C1 (giving 3-methyl-1-butene) or from C3 (giving 2-methyl-2-butene). 2-Methyl-2-butene, (CH3)2C=CHCH3, has the more substituted double bond and is major.

Check. Count alkyl groups on each C=C: 2-methyl-2-butene has three, 3-methyl-1-butene has one. Both are C5H10, the alcohol minus H2O.

Example 4: Ranking by boiling point and solubility

Problem. Rank from lowest to highest boiling point: 1-propanol, ethyl methyl ether, 1,2-propanediol, and butane. Then state which are soluble in water.

Solution. All four have 58–76 g/mol, so look at the IMFs. Butane: dispersion only. Ethyl methyl ether: a weak dipole, no O–H. 1-Propanol: one O–H, hydrogen bonding. 1,2-Propanediol: two O–H groups, more hydrogen bonding per molecule. Ranking: butane < ethyl methyl ether < 1-propanol < 1,2-propanediol (actual values: −1, 7, 97, and 188 °C). Solubility: the ether, the alcohol, and the diol all have oxygen atoms that accept hydrogen bonds from water and short carbon chains, so all three are soluble (the diol and 1-propanol are miscible). Butane is insoluble.

Check. Each added O–H group raised the boiling point by roughly 90 °C in this series, a bigger effect than the few grams per mole of mass difference, which is the sign that hydrogen bonding dominates.

Example 5: Which reagent makes which product?

Problem. Starting from cyclohexanol, which set of conditions gives (a) cyclohexanone, (b) cyclohexene, (c) CO2 and H2O? Choose from: [O]; H+ and heat; O2 and a flame.

Solution. (a) A C=O in place of the C–OH is an oxidation: [O]. Cyclohexanol is a 2° alcohol, so the product is the ketone and the reaction stops there. (b) Losing H and OH from neighboring carbons to make a C=C is dehydration, an elimination: H+ and heat. Cyclohexanol is symmetric, so only one alkene (cyclohexene) is possible. (c) Complete breakdown to CO2 and H2O is combustion: O2 and a flame.

Check. Read the product first, then pick the reagent: a new C=O means [O]; a new C=C and a lost water means H+/heat; CO2 means burning. Reverse the arrows and you have the Chapter 3 and Chapter 5 reactions: hydration (H2O, H+) turns cyclohexene back into cyclohexanol, and reduction ([H]) turns cyclohexanone back into cyclohexanol.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Choosing a longer carbon chain that does not contain the OH carbon as the parent. The parent chain must include the carbon bonded to the OH, even if a longer chain exists elsewhere.
  • Naming a phenol as "hydroxybenzene" or "cyclohexenol." The parent name is phenol, and the OH carbon is C1.
  • Counting the oxygen when classifying an alcohol. Only count carbons on the C–OH carbon.
  • Oxidizing a 2° alcohol "all the way" to a carboxylic acid. Ketones stop.
  • Claiming ethers hydrogen bond with each other. They have no O–H.
  • Calling C6H5CH2OH a phenol. The OH is on a CH2, not on the ring, so it is an alcohol (benzyl alcohol).
  • Choosing the Zaitsev product by counting which neighbor has more hydrogens. Count the alkyl groups on the product's double bond instead; the more substituted alkene wins.
  • Dropping the "e" in thiol names ("butanthiol"). It is butanethiol; only -ol replaces the -e.
  • Forgetting the oxygen inside the alcohol when balancing a combustion equation. Count it before you count the O2.
  • Writing an IUPAC ether name with the alkoxy group on the longer chain. The shorter group becomes the alkoxy prefix; the longer chain is the parent (1-methoxypropane, not propoxymethane).
  • Treating ethylene glycol as harmless because it is "just a diol." Its oxidation products, not the glycol itself, are the toxic species.

Clinical Update: Rosa’s Story, Part 4 of 15

Bad breath and mouthwash

Rosa’s story for this chapter is on the Chapter 4 opening page. Quick recap for the questions below: bacteria in Rosa’s gum pockets release methanethiol, CH3SH, which causes bad breath; her mouthwash contains ethanol, and the hygienist suggests alcohol-free rinses, one with thymol (an –OH bonded directly to a benzene ring) and one that oxidizes sulfur compounds.

Case Question 4.1

Classify CH3SH and explain why such compounds are noticed at very low concentrations.

Answer
It is a thiol (an –SH group on a carbon). Thiols have strong, unpleasant odors that the nose detects at extremely low concentrations, which is why a small amount of bacterial methanethiol causes noticeable bad breath.

Case Question 4.2

Ethanol and thymol both have an –OH group. Which is an alcohol and which is a phenol?

Answer
Ethanol, CH3CH2OH, is an alcohol: the –OH is on a saturated carbon. Thymol is a phenol: the –OH is bonded directly to an aromatic ring.

Case Question 4.3

When two thiol molecules are oxidized, what kind of bond forms between them?

Answer
A disulfide bond, –S–S–: 2 CH3SH → CH3S–SCH3 (two hydrogens are removed, so it is an oxidation). A disulfide is only the first oxidation product; oxidizing rinses push sulfur compounds further, to odorless products, which is one way they freshen breath.

Where this chemistry is taught: Section 4.5, Section 4.7, Section 4.9. Rosa’s story continues at the start of Chapter 5.

Name and classify (CH3)2C(OH)CH2CH3.

Answer

Give the common name of CH3OCH2CH3.

Answer

What alcohol would you oxidize to make butanone, CH3COCH2CH3?

Answer
2-butanol, CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3 (a 2° alcohol gives a ketone)

Exercise 4

Write the product when two molecules of ethanethiol are oxidized.

Answer
CH3CH2–S–S–CH2CH3 (diethyl disulfide)

Exercise 5

Which is more soluble in water: 1-hexanol or 1,2-propanediol? Why?

Answer
1,2-Propanediol. It has two OH groups for hydrogen bonding and a much smaller nonpolar chain (it is fully miscible with water).

Exercise 6

Which reacts with aqueous NaOH: cyclohexanol or phenol? Write the reaction.

Answer
Phenol: C6H5OH + NaOH → C6H5O−Na+ + H2O. Cyclohexanol is an alcohol and does not react.

Exercise 7

Classify each pair as skeletal, positional, or functional group isomers: (a) 1-butanol and 2-butanol; (b) 1-butanol and diethyl ether.

Answer
(a) Positional; (b) functional group isomers (both C4H10O)
