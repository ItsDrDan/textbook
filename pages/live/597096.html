Learning Objectives

  • To describe the structure and uses of some phenols

Compounds in which an OH group is attached directly to an aromatic ring are designated ArOH and called phenols. Phenols differ from alcohols in that they are slightly acidic in water. They react with aqueous sodium hydroxide (NaOH) to form salts.

\[\ce{ArOH (aq) + NaOH (aq) \rightarrow ArONa (aq) + H_2O} \nonumber \]

The parent compound, C6H5OH, is itself called phenol. (An old name, emphasizing its slight acidity, was carbolic acid.) Phenol is a white crystalline compound that has a distinctive (“hospital smell”) odor.

To Your Health: Phenols and Us

Phenols are widely used as antiseptics (substances that kill microorganisms on living tissue) and as disinfectants (substances intended to kill microorganisms on inanimate objects such as furniture or floors). The first widely used antiseptic was phenol. Joseph Lister used it for antiseptic surgery in 1867. Phenol is toxic to humans, however, and can cause severe burns when applied to the skin. In the bloodstream, it is a systemic poison—that is, one that is carried to and affects all parts of the body. Its severe side effects led to searches for safer antiseptics, a number of which have been found.

An operation in 1753, painted by Gaspare Traversi, of a surgery before antiseptics were used.

One safer phenolic antiseptic is 4-hexylresorcinol (4-hexyl-1,3-dihydroxybenzene; resorcinol is the common name for 1,3-dihydroxybenzene, and 4-hexylresorcinol has a hexyl group on the fourth carbon atom of the resorcinol ring). It is much more powerful than phenol as a germicide and has fewer undesirable side effects. Indeed, it is safe enough to be used as the active ingredient in some mouthwashes and throat lozenges.

 

Summary

Phenols are compounds in which an OH group is attached directly to an aromatic ring. Many phenols are used as antiseptics.

Molecule in the Clinic: Eugenol (oil of cloves)

Eugenol is the main compound in clove oil. Mixed with zinc oxide it makes zinc oxide–eugenol (ZOE), a temporary filling and sedative dressing that soothes an irritated tooth pulp. Eugenol is also why a clove can numb a toothache.

Your turn: Name every functional group in eugenol.

Answer
A phenol (OH directly on the benzene ring), an ether (the methoxy group, OCH3), an alkene (the C=C in the side chain) and the aromatic ring itself.

Lecture Connection

Two things about phenols are tested: how to name them (phenol is the parent name, and the OH carbon is C1) and the fact that they are weak acids while alcohols are neutral. The acidity comparison and the NaOH lab test are worked out in full in Section 4.10; this block covers naming, the lab tests you will see, and the phenols you will meet in the clinic.

Naming phenols

Phenol is the IUPAC name for an OH bonded directly to a benzene ring. Assign C1 to the ring carbon holding the OH, number the ring to give the other substituents the lowest numbers, and use either numbers or the ortho/meta/para prefixes from Chapter 3: 3-methylphenol is m-cresol. Do not name a phenol as a "hydroxybenzene" or as a "cyclohexenol"; the parent word is phenol. And check where the OH sits: C6H5CH2OH has its OH on a CH2, not on the ring, so it is an alcohol (benzyl alcohol), not a phenol.

Practice (lecture style). A benzene ring with an OH, an ethyl group on the ring carbon next to it, and a methyl group two carbons further around. Of the names "2-ethyl-1-hydroxy-4-methylbenzene," "6-ethyl-4-methylphenol," "2-ethyl-4-methyl-cyclohexenol" and "2-ethyl-4-methylphenol," only the last is correct: phenol is the parent, the OH carbon is C1, and numbering toward the ethyl group gives 2 and 4 rather than 6 and 4.

Example: Naming a disubstituted phenol

Problem. Name a benzene ring that carries an OH on one carbon, a chlorine on the next carbon around the ring, and a methyl group on the carbon directly across from the OH.

Solution. An OH directly on a benzene ring makes the parent phenol, so the OH carbon is C1. Number toward the nearer substituent: Cl on C2, CH3 on C4. Prefixes in alphabetical order: 2-chloro-4-methylphenol.

Check. Numbering the other way would put Cl on C6, so 2 is correct. Any name that calls the ring a "hydroxybenzene" or a "cyclohexenol" is wrong on the parent.

Phenols are weak acids; alcohols are not

  • Alcohol solutions have about the same pH as water, so for our purposes alcohols are neutral. They do not react with NaOH.
  • Phenols are more acidic than alcohols in water: a phenol solution has pH below 7. Phenol is a weak acid because the benzene ring stabilizes the negative charge left behind when the O–H loses H+, so phenol reacts with NaOH to give sodium phenoxide, the salt shown in the section text. Section 4.10 gives the full ranking and the reasoning.

In the Lab

Two bench tests tell a phenol from an alcohol in the alcohols and phenols experiment. NaOH test: phenol dissolves in 5% NaOH (it is neutralized to sodium phenoxide, an ionic salt); an alcohol such as cyclohexanol does not react and stays as a separate layer (details in Section 4.10). Ferric chloride (FeCl3) test: phenols form strongly colored complexes with Fe3+ (from yellow to purple). Ordinary alcohols do not.

Phenols in health care

The section text tells the story of phenol as Lister's surgical antiseptic in the 1860s and of 4-hexylresorcinol as a milder successor. Today phenolic rinses compete with chlorhexidine, an amine-containing antiseptic (Chapter 7). Propofol, a common IV anesthetic, is a substituted phenol. Triclosan, an antibacterial once common in toothpastes, is a chlorinated phenol that also contains an ether link, two functional groups from this chapter in one molecule; it has since been removed from most U.S. toothpastes.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Phenols in the operatory. Eugenol, the phenol from clove oil, is mixed with zinc oxide to make zinc oxide–eugenol (ZOE) temporary cement and dressings; its mild antiseptic and soothing action comes with the phenol group. Thymol, another phenol (its structure is in the first figure of Section 4.2's Lecture Connection), is one of the essential oils in antiseptic mouthrinses, alongside eucalyptol (an ether) and menthol (an alcohol), so one rinse contains three of this chapter's functional groups. Chlorhexidine rinse is the stronger prescription alternative but can stain teeth.
