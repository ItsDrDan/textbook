Learning Objectives

  • Define the quaternary structure of proteins and explain why some proteins need more than one chain.

Several proteins function as a single polypeptide with primary, secondary, and tertiary structures. Some proteins require more than one polypeptide with their primary, secondary, and tertiary structures combined to function.

Quaternary structure of proteins

The arrangement of two or more polypeptide chains packed together into one functional protein is called the quaternary structure of proteins.

The individual chains are called subunits. The subunits are held together by the same interactions that hold the tertiary structure of individual subunits, i.e., hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, salt bridges, etc.

A protein with two subunits is called a dimer; three, a trimer; four, a tetramer; five, a pentamer. For example, hemoglobin, shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\), is a tetramer of two \(\alpha\) subunits of 141 amino acid residues each and two \(\beta\) subunits of 146 amino acid residues each. These subunits are structurally similar, about the same size, and work cooperatively in the hemoglobin tetramer.

Lecture Connection

Quaternary structure exists only when a protein is made of two or more separate chains. Same forces as tertiary structure, just acting between chains instead of within one. If a protein is a single chain, it has no quaternary structure, no matter how big it is.

Hemoglobin, insulin, myoglobin

  • Hemoglobin is the classic case: two α subunits (141 residues each) and two β subunits (146 residues each), each cradling a heme group whose Fe2+ binds one O2 and carries it through the blood. Carbon monoxide is poisonous because it binds that same Fe2+ about 200 times more tightly than O2 does, so a small amount of CO takes a large share of the oxygen-carrying sites out of service; the patient looks pink and the pulse oximeter can read normal while the tissues starve.
  • Insulin has two chains (A and B), but they are held together by covalent –S–S– (disulfide) bonds, and they start out as one chain (proinsulin) that is cut. So many textbooks treat insulin as a special case rather than true quaternary structure: unlike true subunits, its chains are not pulled apart by agents that disrupt only the weak interactions.
  • A single-chain protein such as myoglobin, the oxygen store in muscle, has primary, secondary and tertiary structure but no quaternary structure. Its fold looks like one hemoglobin subunit.

Because the subunits are held by the same weak R-group forces as tertiary structure, anything that denatures a protein (Section 10.10) also pulls its subunits apart.

Valley Connection: Nitrate in Well Water and “Blue Baby” Hemoglobin

Many rural Valley families drink water from private wells, and those wells are not routinely tested. In the eastern San Joaquin Valley, about one in three domestic wells has nitrate above the public health limit,1 and a UC Davis study for the state estimated that about 254,000 people in the Tulare Lake Basin and Salinas Valley have drinking water at risk.2 Most of the nitrate (NO3−) comes from crop fertilizer and dairy manure that soaks down into the groundwater.3

The chemistry link. Nitrate itself does little harm. Bacteria in the gut reduce it to nitrite (NO2−), and nitrite oxidizes the iron in each heme group of hemoglobin from Fe2+ to Fe3+. The result, methemoglobin, cannot carry oxygen.4 Remember that hemoglobin is four subunits, each with its own heme: every subunit whose iron is oxidized is one fewer O2 carrier. When enough of the blood’s hemoglobin is converted, tissues run short of oxygen and the skin, lips and nail beds turn bluish (cyanosis), the “blue baby syndrome.”

Why babies? Infants under about 6 months are most at risk. Their fetal hemoglobin is oxidized to methemoglobin more easily than adult hemoglobin, and their red blood cells have only about half the adult activity of the enzyme that reduces Fe3+ back to Fe2+.4,5 The federal limit of 10 mg/L nitrate-nitrogen was set to protect infants. Boiling does not help; it concentrates the nitrate.6 A nurse who asks a new parent “What water do you use to mix the formula?” is asking a chemistry question.

Think about it: Carbon monoxide and nitrite both keep hemoglobin from carrying O2, but in different ways. Compare what each one does at the iron of the heme group. In either case, has the quaternary structure of hemoglobin been taken apart?

Answer
CO leaves the iron as Fe2+ but binds to it about 200 times more tightly than O2, so the site is occupied. Nitrite oxidizes the iron from Fe2+ to Fe3+ (the iron loses an electron), and Fe3+ cannot carry O2. No: in both cases the four subunits are still together. The protein is intact, but the heme sites are out of service.

Summary

  • Quaternary structure is the arrangement of two or more polypeptide chains (subunits) in one functional protein.
  • Hemoglobin has four subunits, each with a heme group; myoglobin is a single chain and has no quaternary structure.
  • The subunits are held by the same four interactions as tertiary structure.

Practice 10.9

Hemoglobin has two α chains of 141 amino acids each and two β chains of 146 amino acids each. Myoglobin is one chain of 153 amino acids.

  1. How many subunits does hemoglobin have? What is the name for a protein with that number of subunits? How many amino acids does it contain in all?
  2. Which of the two proteins has quaternary structure? Explain.
  3. What holds the subunits of hemoglobin together? How many O2 molecules can one hemoglobin carry, and why?
Answer
  1. Four subunits, so hemoglobin is a tetramer. Total: (2 × 141) + (2 × 146) = 282 + 292 = 574 amino acids.
  2. Only hemoglobin. Quaternary structure exists only when two or more separate chains pack into one functional protein. Myoglobin is a single chain, so it stops at tertiary structure, however large it is.
  3. The same R-group interactions that hold tertiary structure, acting between chains instead of within one chain. In hemoglobin these are the weak ones: hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds and salt bridges. Hemoglobin carries four O2: each of the four subunits has one heme, and each Fe2+ binds one O2.

Check. 282 + 292 = 574, and 574 is larger than either chain alone, as it must be. Count chains, not amino acids, to decide on quaternary structure: four chains, yes; one chain, no.
