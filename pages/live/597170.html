Learning Objectives

  • To explain why vitamins are necessary in the diet.

Many enzymes are simple proteins consisting entirely of one or more amino acid chains. Other enzymes contain a nonprotein component called a cofactor that is necessary for the enzyme’s proper functioning. There are two types of cofactors: inorganic ions [e.g., zinc or Cu(I) ions] and organic molecules known as coenzymes. Most coenzymes are vitamins or are derived from vitamins.

Vitamins are organic compounds that are essential in very small (trace) amounts for the maintenance of normal metabolism. They generally cannot be synthesized at adequate levels by the body and must be obtained from the diet. The absence or shortage of a vitamin may result in a vitamin-deficiency disease. In the first half of the 20th century, a major focus of biochemistry was the identification, isolation, and characterization of vitamins. Despite accumulating evidence that people needed more than just carbohydrates, fats, and proteins in their diets for normal growth and health, it was not until the early 1900s that research established the need for trace nutrients in the diet.

Because organisms differ in their synthetic abilities, a substance that is a vitamin for one species may not be so for another. Over the past 100 years, scientists have identified and isolated 13 vitamins required in the human diet and have divided them into two broad categories: the fat-soluble vitamins, which include vitamins A, D, E, and K, and the water-soluble vitamins, which are the B complex vitamins and vitamin C. All fat-soluble vitamins contain a high proportion of hydrocarbon structural components. There are one or two oxygen atoms present, but the compounds as a whole are nonpolar. In contrast, water-soluble vitamins contain large numbers of electronegative oxygen and nitrogen atoms, which can engage in hydrogen bonding with water. Most water-soluble vitamins act as coenzymes or are required for the synthesis of coenzymes. The fat-soluble vitamins are important for a variety of physiological functions. Vitamins C and E, as well as the provitamin β-carotene can act as antioxidants in the body. Antioxidants prevent damage from free radicals, which are molecules that are highly reactive because they have unpaired electrons. Free radicals are formed not only through metabolic reactions involving oxygen but also by such environmental factors as radiation and pollution.

β-carotene is known as a provitamin because it can be converted to vitamin A in the body.

Free radicals react most commonly react with lipoproteins and unsaturated fatty acids in cell membranes, removing an electron from those molecules and thus generating a new free radical. The process becomes a chain reaction that finally leads to the oxidative degradation of the affected compounds. Antioxidants react with free radicals to stop these chain reactions by forming a more stable molecule or, in the case of vitamin E, a free radical that is much less reactive (vitamin E is converted back to its original form through interaction with vitamin C).

Summary

Vitamins are organic compounds that are essential in very small amounts for the maintenance of normal metabolism. Vitamins are divided into two broad categories: fat-soluble vitamins and water-soluble vitamins. Most water-soluble vitamins are needed for the formation of coenzymes, which are organic molecules needed by some enzymes for catalytic activity.

Lecture Connection

Two lecture definitions to carry into the metabolism chapters: a cofactor is an inorganic helper (a metal ion) and a coenzyme is an organic helper, almost always built from a water-soluble vitamin. Then the pairing you will use over and over: niacin → NAD+, riboflavin → FAD, pantothenic acid → coenzyme A, thiamine → TPP, B6 → PLP.

Cofactor or coenzyme?

In this course

The section text uses "cofactor" as the umbrella term for any non-protein helper and "coenzyme" for the organic ones. Some textbooks do the same. On exams, follow the lecture definitions: an inorganic substance that binds to an enzyme, such as a metal ion (Zn2+, Mg2+, Fe2+, Cu2+), is a cofactor; an organic molecule that binds to an enzyme, usually made from a vitamin (NAD+, FAD, coenzyme A), is a coenzyme. Protein + cofactor or coenzyme = the active enzyme.

  • Metal-ion cofactors form coordinate covalent bonds with nitrogen, oxygen, or sulfur atoms of R groups (His, Cys, Asp, Glu); some are held tightly and some enter and leave the active site. They help hold the substrate or carry charge: Zn2+ in carboxypeptidase steadies the carbonyl oxygen while a C-terminal peptide bond is hydrolyzed. Zn2+ sits in carbonic anhydrase, alcohol dehydrogenase, and the matrix metalloproteinases of gum tissue; Mg2+ pairs with ATP in every kinase; Fe2+/Fe3+ in heme carries electrons in catalase and the cytochromes of the electron transport chain; Cu2+ in cytochrome oxidase; Mn2+, Se, and Mo in a few others.
  • Coenzymes are made from water-soluble vitamins, mostly the B group. The coenzyme is usually a slightly built-up version of the vitamin, and it does the chemistry the protein's twenty amino acids cannot: carrying hydrogen, electrons, acyl groups, CO2, amino groups, or one-carbon units from one reaction to the next.
  • Water-soluble vitamins (the B vitamins and C) are carried dissolved in the blood, are rapidly eliminated in the urine, must be constantly replenished, and are unlikely to be toxic. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) are nonpolar lipids (Chapter 9): they are absorbed with dietary fat, carried in the blood by protein carriers (lipoproteins), stored in fat tissue and the liver, needed only in periodic doses, and more likely to be toxic in overdose. The fat-soluble vitamins are not coenzymes.

Worked example

Example 1: From symptom to vitamin to coenzyme

Problem. A patient with a long history of heavy alcohol use is confused and has trouble walking and moving his eyes. Which vitamin is most likely deficient, which coenzyme is missing, and which step of metabolism stalls?

Solution. The picture is Wernicke encephalopathy, caused by lack of thiamine (B1). Its coenzyme is thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP), needed by pyruvate dehydrogenase, so pyruvate from glycolysis cannot become acetyl-CoA. Brain cells, which depend on glucose, run short of energy.

Check. Thiamine is water-soluble, so stores are small and a few weeks of poor intake can empty them; that fits the rapid onset. Beriberi is the same deficiency with heart and peripheral-nerve symptoms.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Vitamins on the ward. Patients with alcohol use disorder are often thiamine-deficient (poor diet, poor absorption), and giving IV glucose without thiamine can precipitate Wernicke encephalopathy (confusion, unsteady gait, eye-movement problems), because pyruvate cannot be converted to acetyl-CoA without TPP; thiamine is given first. Vitamin B12 needs intrinsic factor from the stomach to be absorbed, so pernicious anemia (autoimmune loss of intrinsic factor), gastric surgery, long-term metformin, and a strict vegan diet (no animal foods) all lead to deficiency and are treated with injections or supplements. Folate before and during early pregnancy prevents neural-tube defects such as spina bifida, since the embryo's rapidly dividing cells need THF to make DNA. In the dental chair, bleeding gums on probing can signal vitamin C deficiency (weak collagen), and vitamin D is needed for the mineralization of developing teeth.
