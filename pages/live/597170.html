Learning Objectives

  • To explain why vitamins are necessary in the diet.

Many enzymes are simple proteins consisting entirely of one or more amino acid chains. Other enzymes contain a nonprotein component called a cofactor that is necessary for the enzyme’s proper functioning. Many books use “cofactor” for any nonprotein helper. In this course, a cofactor is an inorganic ion such as Zn2+, Mg2+, Fe2+ or Cu2+, and a coenzyme is an organic molecule, usually made from a vitamin (NAD+, FAD, coenzyme A). Protein + cofactor or coenzyme = the active enzyme.

Vitamins are organic compounds that are essential in very small (trace) amounts for the maintenance of normal metabolism. They generally cannot be synthesized at adequate levels by the body and must be obtained from the diet. The absence or shortage of a vitamin may result in a vitamin-deficiency disease. In the first half of the 20th century, a major focus of biochemistry was the identification, isolation, and characterization of vitamins. Despite accumulating evidence that people needed more than just carbohydrates, fats, and proteins in their diets for normal growth and health, it was not until the early 1900s that research established the need for trace nutrients in the diet.

Because organisms differ in their synthetic abilities, a substance that is a vitamin for one species may not be so for another. Over the past 100 years, scientists have identified and isolated 13 vitamins required in the human diet and have divided them into two broad categories: the fat-soluble vitamins, which include vitamins A, D, E, and K, and the water-soluble vitamins, which are the B complex vitamins and vitamin C. All fat-soluble vitamins contain a high proportion of hydrocarbon structural components. There are one or two oxygen atoms present, but the compounds as a whole are nonpolar. In contrast, water-soluble vitamins contain large numbers of electronegative oxygen and nitrogen atoms, which can engage in hydrogen bonding with water. Most water-soluble vitamins act as coenzymes or are required for the synthesis of coenzymes. The fat-soluble vitamins are important for a variety of physiological functions. The key vitamins and their functions are listed in the tables in the Lecture Connection below.

Vitamins C and E, as well as the provitamin β-carotene can act as antioxidants in the body. Antioxidants prevent damage from free radicals, which are molecules that are highly reactive because they have unpaired electrons. Free radicals are formed not only through metabolic reactions involving oxygen but also by such environmental factors as radiation and pollution.

β-carotene is known as a provitamin because it can be converted to vitamin A in the body.

Free radicals most commonly react with lipoproteins and unsaturated fatty acids in cell membranes, removing an electron from those molecules and thus generating a new free radical. The process becomes a chain reaction that finally leads to the oxidative degradation of the affected compounds. Antioxidants stop these chain reactions by reacting with the free radicals. Vitamin E, for example, gives up a hydrogen atom and becomes a much less reactive radical; vitamin C then converts it back to its original form.

Summary

Vitamins are organic compounds that are essential in very small amounts for the maintenance of normal metabolism. Vitamins are divided into two broad categories: fat-soluble vitamins and water-soluble vitamins. Most water-soluble vitamins are needed for the formation of coenzymes, which are organic molecules needed by some enzymes for catalytic activity.

Lecture Connection

Two lecture definitions to carry into the metabolism chapters: a cofactor is an inorganic helper (a metal ion) and a coenzyme is an organic helper, almost always built from a water-soluble vitamin. Then the pairing you will use over and over: niacin → NAD+, riboflavin → FAD, pantothenic acid → coenzyme A, thiamine → TPP, B6 → PLP.

Cofactor or coenzyme?

  • Metal-ion cofactors form coordinate covalent bonds with nitrogen, oxygen, or sulfur atoms of R groups (His, Cys, Asp, Glu); some are held tightly and some enter and leave the active site. They help hold the substrate or carry charge: Zn2+ in carboxypeptidase steadies the carbonyl oxygen while a C-terminal peptide bond is hydrolyzed. Zn2+ sits in carbonic anhydrase, alcohol dehydrogenase, and the matrix metalloproteinases of gum tissue; Mg2+ pairs with ATP in every kinase; Fe2+/Fe3+ in heme carries electrons in catalase and the cytochromes of the electron transport chain; Cu2+ in cytochrome oxidase; Mn2+, Se, and Mo in a few others.
  • Coenzymes are made from water-soluble vitamins, mostly the B group. The coenzyme is usually a slightly built-up version of the vitamin, and it does the chemistry the protein's twenty amino acids cannot: carrying hydrogen, electrons, acyl groups, CO2, amino groups, or one-carbon units from one reaction to the next.
  • Water-soluble vitamins (the B vitamins and C) are carried dissolved in the blood, are rapidly eliminated in the urine, must be constantly replenished, and are unlikely to be toxic. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) are nonpolar lipids (Chapter 9): they are absorbed with dietary fat, carried in the blood by protein carriers (lipoproteins), stored in fat tissue and the liver, needed only in periodic doses, and more likely to be toxic in overdose. The fat-soluble vitamins are not coenzymes.

Fat-soluble vitamins

None of the fat-soluble vitamins acts as a coenzyme in the usual sense; A, D, E and K work as a light sensor, a hormone, an antioxidant and a helper in blood clotting.

Worked example

Example 1: From symptom to vitamin to coenzyme

Problem. A patient with a long history of heavy alcohol use is confused and has trouble walking and moving his eyes. Which vitamin is most likely deficient, which coenzyme is missing, and which step of metabolism stalls?

Solution. The picture is Wernicke encephalopathy, caused by lack of thiamine (B1). Its coenzyme is thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP), needed by pyruvate dehydrogenase, so pyruvate from glycolysis cannot become acetyl-CoA. Brain cells, which depend on glucose, run short of energy.

Check. Thiamine is water-soluble, so stores are small and a few weeks of poor intake can empty them; that fits the rapid onset. Beriberi is the same deficiency with heart and peripheral-nerve symptoms.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Vitamins on the ward. Patients with alcohol use disorder are often thiamine-deficient (poor diet, poor absorption), and giving IV glucose without thiamine can precipitate Wernicke encephalopathy (confusion, unsteady gait, eye-movement problems), because pyruvate cannot be converted to acetyl-CoA without TPP; thiamine is given first. Vitamin B12 needs intrinsic factor from the stomach to be absorbed, so pernicious anemia (autoimmune loss of intrinsic factor), gastric surgery, long-term metformin, and a strict vegan diet (no animal foods) all lead to deficiency and are treated with injections or supplements. Folate before and during early pregnancy prevents neural-tube defects such as spina bifida, since the embryo's rapidly dividing cells need THF to make DNA. In the dental chair, bleeding gums on probing can signal vitamin C deficiency (weak collagen), and vitamin D is needed for the mineralization of developing teeth.

Valley Connection: Almonds and Vitamin E

California grows about 80% of the world’s almonds,1 and most of the orchards are in San Joaquin Valley counties from Stanislaus and Merced through Madera, Fresno and Kern.2 A one-ounce handful of almonds supplies about 7.3 mg of vitamin E (as α-tocopherol), roughly half of the 15 mg an adult needs each day.3

The chemistry link. Vitamin E is a fat-soluble vitamin: a long, nonpolar hydrocarbon tail attached to a ring that carries one phenol –OH. The tail lets it dissolve in the lipid bilayer of cell membranes (Chapter 9), next to the polyunsaturated fatty acid chains it protects. When a reactive oxygen species attacks a C=C in one of those chains, vitamin E gives up the H of its phenol –OH and stops the chain of oxidation before it spreads. That makes it an antioxidant, not a coenzyme, which is why it is not in the B-vitamin coenzyme table. It also explains why almonds, rich in oleic acid, keep well: the oil carries its own antioxidant.

In the clinic. Deficiency is rare and is usually seen in people who cannot absorb fat, for example with Crohn’s disease or cystic fibrosis. Food sources are safe, but very high doses from supplements can interfere with vitamin K and blood clotting, so patients on blood thinners are often asked about vitamin E supplements.4

Think about it: Vitamin E has an –OH group, and –OH groups can hydrogen bond with water. Why is vitamin E fat-soluble anyway? Use your answer to explain why a person who cannot absorb fat can run low on vitamin E.

Answer
One polar –OH is far outweighed by the long nonpolar hydrocarbon tail and ring, so the molecule as a whole is nonpolar. Like dissolves like: it dissolves in fats and lipid membranes, not in water. Because it travels dissolved in dietary fat, a person who absorbs fat poorly also absorbs vitamin E poorly.

Valley Connection: Folic Acid in the Tortilla

The folate row of the vitamin table lists neural-tube defects. As THF, folate carries the one-carbon units used to make nucleotides, so the fast-dividing cells of an embryo need plenty of it very early in pregnancy. CDC notes that Hispanic/Latina women have a high risk of having a baby with a neural-tube defect.1 In 2016 the FDA allowed folic acid to be added to corn masa flour on a voluntary basis,2 but CDC reports that, in data from 2011–2020, voluntary fortification had not yet improved folate levels among Hispanic/Latina women.1

California’s law. AB 1830, signed September 28, 2024, made fortification required: starting January 1, 2026, corn masa flour made, sold or used in California must contain 0.7 mg of folic acid per pound, and wet corn masa products may contain 0.4 mg per pound.3 Masa is the dough behind corn tortillas and tamales, so a staple food now carries the vitamin.

Think about it: Folate is turned into THF in the body. By the definitions in this section, is THF a cofactor or a coenzyme? Explain your choice, and explain why the cells of an early embryo need so much of it.

Answer
THF is a coenzyme. It is an organic molecule made from a vitamin. A cofactor in this course is an inorganic ion such as Zn2+ or Mg2+. THF carries the one-carbon units used to build nucleotides. An embryo’s cells divide fast, and every division needs a full new copy of the DNA, so they need a steady supply of nucleotides.

Practice 11.5

Five enzyme helpers are Zn2+, NAD+, Mg2+, coenzyme A and FAD.

  1. Classify each helper as a cofactor or a coenzyme.
  2. For each coenzyme, name the vitamin it is made from.
  3. A patient takes large daily supplements of vitamin C and of vitamin A. Which one is more likely to build up to a toxic level? Explain.
Answer
  1. Cofactors: Zn2+ and Mg2+. They are inorganic metal ions. Coenzymes: NAD+, coenzyme A and FAD. They are organic molecules.
  2. NAD+ comes from niacin (B3). FAD comes from riboflavin (B2). Coenzyme A comes from pantothenic acid (B5).
  3. Vitamin A. It is fat-soluble, so the excess is stored in the liver and in fat tissue. Vitamin C is water-soluble, so the excess leaves quickly in the urine.

Check. Two cofactors plus three coenzymes accounts for all five helpers. Every cofactor on the list is a metal ion with a charge and no carbon. Every coenzyme traces back to a B vitamin, and the B vitamins are water-soluble.
