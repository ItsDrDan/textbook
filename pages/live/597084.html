Learning Objectives

  • To describe the bonding in benzene and the way typical reactions of benzene differ from those of the alkenes.

Next we consider a class of hydrocarbons with molecular formulas like those of unsaturated hydrocarbons, but which, unlike the alkenes, do not readily undergo addition reactions. These compounds comprise a distinct class, called aromatic hydrocarbons, with unique structures and properties. We start with the simplest of these compounds. Benzene (C6H6) is of great commercial importance, but it also has noteworthy health effects.

The formula C6H6 seems to indicate that benzene has a high degree of unsaturation. (Hexane, the saturated hydrocarbon with six carbon atoms, has the formula C6H14—eight more hydrogen atoms than benzene.) However, despite the seeming low level of saturation, benzene is rather unreactive. It does not, for example, react readily with bromine, which is a test for unsaturation.

Benzene is a liquid that smells like gasoline, boils at 80°C, and freezes at 5.5°C. It is the aromatic hydrocarbon produced in the largest volume. It was formerly used to decaffeinate coffee and was a significant component of many consumer products, such as paint strippers, rubber cements, and home dry-cleaning spot removers. It was removed from many product formulations in the 1950s, but others continued to use benzene in products until the 1970s when it was associated with leukemia deaths. Benzene is still important in industry as a precursor in the production of plastics (such as Styrofoam and nylon), drugs, detergents, synthetic rubber, pesticides, and dyes. It is used as a solvent for such things as cleaning and maintaining printing equipment and for adhesives such as those used to attach soles to shoes. Benzene is a natural constituent of petroleum products, but because it is a known carcinogen, its use as an additive in gasoline is now limited.

To explain the surprising properties of benzene, chemists suppose the molecule has a cyclic, hexagonal, planar structure of six carbon atoms with one hydrogen atom bonded to each. However, these structures do not explain the unique properties of benzene. Furthermore, experimental evidence indicates that all the carbon-to-carbon bonds in benzene are equivalent, and the molecule is unusually stable. Chemists often represent benzene as a hexagon with an inscribed circle:

The inner circle indicates that the valence electrons are shared equally by all six carbon atoms (that is, the electrons are delocalized, or spread out, over all the carbon atoms). It is understood that each corner of the hexagon is occupied by one carbon atom, and each carbon atom has one hydrogen atom attached to it. Any other atom or groups of atoms substituted for a hydrogen atom must be shown bonded to a particular corner of the hexagon. Many chemists still draw the earlier structure with alternating double and single bonds, and either drawing is accepted in this course.

To Your Health: Benzene and Us

Most of the benzene used commercially comes from petroleum. It is employed as a starting material for the production of detergents, drugs, dyes, insecticides, and plastics. Once widely used as an organic solvent, benzene is now known to have both short- and long-term toxic effects. The inhalation of large concentrations can cause nausea and even death due to respiratory or heart failure, while repeated exposure leads to a progressive disease in which the ability of the bone marrow to make new blood cells is eventually destroyed. This results in a condition called aplastic anemia, in which there is a decrease in the numbers of both the red and white blood cells.

Key Takeaway

  • Aromatic hydrocarbons appear to be unsaturated, but they have a special type of bonding and do not undergo addition reactions.

Lecture Connection

Lecture treats the benzene ring as its own functional group for one reason: its six shared electrons make it stable, so it substitutes where an alkene would add. Everything in this section is a consequence of that one idea.

Why benzene is its own functional group

  • Aromatic compounds are cyclic and planar with conjugated (alternating) double bonds. Benzene (C6H6) is drawn with three alternating double bonds, but its six double-bond electrons are delocalized, shared equally by all six ring carbons; the real molecule is a hybrid of the two resonance drawings. That makes it unusually stable and not very reactive, so it does not undergo the addition reactions above. Aromatic rings are treated as a separate functional group precisely because they react differently from ordinary alkenes.

The structure of benzene. Benzene is a flat, regular hexagon of six carbons, each carrying one hydrogen. If you draw it with three C=C bonds you can place them in two equally good ways (the two Kekulé structures in the figure below). Neither drawing is right on its own: every C–C bond in benzene is the same length, partway between a single and a double bond, because the six electrons that the drawing puts into "double bonds" are actually shared around the whole ring. Chemists show this with a circle inside the hexagon. A ring that shares its electrons this way is called aromatic (the name is historical; it has nothing to do with smell), and the shared electron cloud is what makes the ring so much harder to break open than an alkene.

Professor Tip

The games (Bond Voyage!, Organic Origins) draw benzene in the Kekulé form with three C=C bonds because it lets you count bonds and hydrogens; when you count a formula from it, every ring carbon still has exactly one H (C6H6). Whichever way it is drawn, do not treat the ring as three alkenes: it does not add H2, Br2, or H2O under the conditions of Section 3.4.

Benzene reacts by substitution, not addition

In electrophilic aromatic substitution an H on the ring is replaced, so the stable aromatic ring survives:

  • Halogenation: benzene + Br2 (FeBr3 catalyst) → bromobenzene + HBr
  • Nitration: benzene + HNO3 (H2SO4 catalyst) → nitrobenzene + H2O
  • Sulfonation: benzene + SO3 (H2SO4) → benzenesulfonic acid

Why substitution? If Br2 added across one "double bond" of benzene the way it adds to cyclohexene, the ring would lose its shared electron cloud and most of the stability that goes with it. Replacing a hydrogen keeps the cloud intact, so that is the path benzene takes. The reagent that attaches to the ring is an electrophile ("electron-lover"): an electron-poor species that is attracted to the electron-rich ring. On its own Br2 is not a strong enough electrophile, which is why benzene gives no reaction in the bromine test. The catalyst (FeBr3 for bromine, H2SO4 for nitric acid) makes the reagent a much stronger electrophile, and the substitution proceeds. You do not need the step-by-step mechanism; you need to recognize the three reactions, their reagents, and their products, which are summarized in the table and figure below.

The three products above matter later in the course: nitrobenzene is reduced to aniline, the parent of many amine drugs (Chapter 7), and the sulfonic acid group is the strongly acidic, water-solubilizing group in detergents, while its close relative, the sulfonamide group (–SO2NH2), is the core of the sulfa antibiotics.

Example 1: Addition or substitution? Cyclohexene versus benzene

Problem. Both cyclohexene (C6H10) and benzene (C6H6) are six-membered rings. Write the organic product, its molecular formula, and the reaction type for (a) cyclohexene + Br2 and (b) benzene + Br2 with FeBr3.

Solution. (a) Cyclohexene has an ordinary C=C, so Br2 adds: one Br on each former double-bond carbon. Product 1,2-dibromocyclohexane, C6H10Br2; both bromine atoms end up in the product and nothing else forms. Addition. (b) Benzene keeps its aromatic ring. One Br replaces one ring H: product bromobenzene, C6H5Br, plus HBr. Substitution (electrophilic aromatic substitution).

Check. Addition makes one product and uses up the double bond; the formula grows by the whole reagent (C6H10 + Br2). Substitution makes two products and the ring formula changes by swapping one H for one Br (C6H6 → C6H5Br). Without the FeBr3 catalyst, (b) does not happen at all, which is the basis of the bromine test in the lab.

In the Lab

  • Experiment 3 – Reactions of Hydrocarbons
  • Bromine test: the red color of Br2 disappears quickly with an alkene (addition across the C=C). With an alkane the color lingers and fades only slowly in light (substitution, which also releases HBr). Benzene gives no reaction.
  • KMnO4 test: purple permanganate adds to a C=C to make a diol and turns into a muddy brown MnO2 solid. Alkanes and aromatic rings stay purple.
  • You will use these two tests to identify an unknown hydrocarbon as saturated or unsaturated.
