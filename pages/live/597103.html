Learning Objectives

  • Explain why the boiling points of aldehydes and ketones are higher than those of ethers and alkanes of similar molar masses but lower than those of comparable alcohols.
  • Compare the solubilities in water of aldehydes and ketones of four or fewer carbon atoms with the solubilities of comparable alkanes and alcohols.
  • Name the typical reactions that take place with aldehydes and ketones.
  • Describe some of the uses of common aldehydes and ketones.

The carbon-to-oxygen double bond is quite polar, more polar than a carbon-to-oxygen single bond. The electronegative oxygen atom has a much greater attraction for the bonding electron pairs than does the carbon atom. The carbon atom has a partial positive charge, and the oxygen atom has a partial negative charge:

In aldehydes and ketones, this charge separation leads to dipole-dipole interactions that are great enough to significantly affect the boiling points. Table \(\PageIndex{1}\) shows that the polar single bonds in ethers have little such effect, whereas hydrogen bonding between alcohol molecules is even stronger.

Table \(\PageIndex{1}\): Boiling Points of Compounds Having Similar Molar Masses but Different Types of Intermolecular Forces
Compound Family Molar Mass Type of Intermolecular Forces Boiling Point (°C)
CH3CH2CH2CH3 alkane 58 dispersion only –1
CH3OCH2CH3 ether 60 weak dipole 7
CH3CH2CHO aldehyde 58 strong dipole 49
CH3COCH3 ketone 58 strong dipole 56
CH3CH2CH2OH alcohol 60 hydrogen bonding 97

Formaldehyde is a gas at room temperature. Acetaldehyde boils at 20°C; in an open vessel, it boils away in a warm room. Most other common aldehydes are liquids at room temperature.

Although the lower members of the homologous series have pungent odors, many higher aldehydes have pleasant odors and are used in perfumes and artificial flavorings. As for the ketones, acetone has a pleasant odor, but most of the higher homologs have rather bland odors.

The oxygen atom of the carbonyl group engages in hydrogen bonding with a water molecule.

The solubility of aldehydes is therefore about the same as that of alcohols and ethers. Formaldehyde, acetaldehyde, and acetone are soluble in water. As the carbon chain increases in length, solubility in water decreases. The borderline of solubility occurs at about four carbon atoms per oxygen atom. All aldehydes and ketones are soluble in organic solvents and, in general, are less dense than water.

Oxidation of Aldehydes and Ketones

Aldehydes and ketones are much alike in many of their reactions, owing to the presence of the carbonyl functional group in both. They differ greatly, however, in one most important type of reaction: oxidation. Aldehydes are readily oxidized to carboxylic acids, whereas ketones resist oxidation.

The aldehydes are, in fact, among the most easily oxidized of organic compounds. They are oxidized by oxygen (\(\ce{O2}\)) in air to carboxylic acids.

\[\ce{2RCHO + O_2 -> 2RCOOH} \nonumber \]

The ease of oxidation helps chemists identify aldehydes. A sufficiently mild oxidizing agent can distinguish aldehydes not only from ketones but also from alcohols. Tollens’ reagent, for example, is an alkaline solution of silver (\(\ce{Ag^{+}}\)) ion complexed with ammonia (NH3), which keeps the \(\ce{Ag^{+}}\) ion in solution.

\[\ce{H_3N—Ag^{+}—NH_3} \nonumber \]

When Tollens’ reagent oxidizes an aldehyde, the \(\ce{Ag^{+}}\) ion is reduced to free silver (\(\ce{Ag}\)).

\[\underbrace{\ce{RCHO(aq)}}_{\text{an aldehyde}} + \ce{2Ag(NH3)2^{+}(aq) + 3OH^{-}(aq) -> RCOO^{-} +} \underbrace{\ce{2Ag(s)}}_{\text{free silver}} + \ce{4NH3(aq) + 2H2O} \nonumber \]

Deposited on a clean glass surface, the silver produces a mirror (Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\)). Ordinary ketones do not react with Tollens’ reagent.

Although ketones resist oxidation by ordinary laboratory oxidizing agents, they undergo combustion, as do aldehydes.

Some Common Carbonyl Compounds

Formaldehyde has an irritating odor. Because of its reactivity, it is difficult to handle in the gaseous state. For many uses, it is therefore dissolved in water and sold as a 37% to 40% aqueous solution called formalin. Formaldehyde denatures proteins, rendering them insoluble in water and resistant to bacterial decay. For this reason, formalin is used in embalming solutions and in preserving biological specimens.

Large quantities of formaldehyde are used to make phenol-formaldehyde resins for gluing the wood sheets in plywood and as adhesives in other building materials. Sometimes the formaldehyde escapes from the materials and causes health problems in some people. While some people seem unaffected, others experience coughing, wheezing, eye irritation, and other symptoms.

The odor of green leaves is due in part to a carbonyl compound, cis-3-hexenal, which with related compounds is used to impart a “green” herbal odor to shampoos and other products.

Acetaldehyde is an extremely volatile, colorless liquid. It is a starting material for the preparation of many other organic compounds. Acetaldehyde is formed as a metabolite in the fermentation of sugars and in the detoxification of alcohol in the liver. Aldehydes are the active components of many other familiar materials (Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\)).

Acetone is the simplest and most important ketone. Because it is miscible with water as well as with most organic solvents, its chief use is as an industrial solvent (for example, for paints and lacquers). It is also the chief ingredient in some brands of nail polish remover.

To Your Health: Acetone in Blood, Urine, and Breath

Acetone is formed in the human body as a by-product of lipid metabolism. Normally, acetone does not accumulate to an appreciable extent because it is oxidized to carbon dioxide and water. The normal concentration of acetone in the human body is less than 1 mg/100 mL of blood. In certain disease states, such as uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, the acetone concentration rises to higher levels. It is then excreted in the urine, where it is easily detected. In severe cases, its odor can be noted on the breath.

Ketones are also the active components of other familiar substances, some of which are noted in the accompanying figure.

Certain steroid hormones have the ketone functional group as a part of their structure. Two examples are progesterone, a hormone secreted by the ovaries that stimulates the growth of cells in the uterine wall and prepares it for attachment of a fertilized egg, and testosterone, the main male sex hormone. These and other sex hormones affect our development and our lives in fundamental ways.

Valley Connection: The Aldehydes in Wildfire Smoke

Late summer and fall often bring wildfire smoke into the Valley. During the 2020 Creek Fire in the Fresno and Madera County foothills, the Valley Air District warned that smoke was affecting every county in the Valley air basin and told residents to stay indoors.1 Most smoke warnings focus on tiny particles (PM2.5), but smoke is also a mix of gases, and some of the most irritating ones are aldehydes.

The chemistry. When wood burns incompletely, small carbonyl compounds form. EPA’s wildfire smoke guide names formaldehyde (methanal, HCHO), acetaldehyde (ethanal, CH3CHO), and acrolein (propenal, CH2=CHCHO) among the hazardous air pollutants of concern in smoke.2 The properties in this section explain why they are a problem. With only one to three carbons, these aldehydes have low boiling points; formaldehyde is a nearly colorless gas at room temperature.3 Their polar C=O group also makes them very water-soluble (formaldehyde is about 55% soluble in water at 20 °C),3 so they dissolve readily in the moist surfaces of the eyes, nose, and throat. Formaldehyde vapor causes immediate irritation of these mucous membranes,3 and breathing acrolein can irritate the nose and throat and slow the breathing rate.4 Formaldehyde is also classified as a known human carcinogen by the International Agency for Research on Cancer and the U.S. National Toxicology Program. Smoke studies have identified acrolein and formaldehyde, along with benzene, as the main gas-phase contributors to health risk in wildfire smoke.5

In the clinic. People with asthma, COPD, or heart disease, older adults, children, and pregnant people are especially vulnerable to smoke.1,2 Respiratory therapists and nurses often recommend N95 respirators, but there is a catch: N95 and P100 respirators remove only particles, not gases or vapors like aldehydes.2 Respirators with organic-vapor cartridges can reduce aldehyde exposure,2 but for most people the best protection is staying indoors in filtered air.1

Think about it: Formaldehyde and ethane have nearly the same molar mass (30 g/mol), but formaldehyde is far more irritating to the eyes and airways. Which property of the carbonyl group helps explain this?

Answer
The C=O group is polar, and its oxygen can accept hydrogen bonds from water. That makes formaldehyde very soluble in water, so it dissolves quickly into the watery lining of the eyes, nose, and throat, where it reacts with and irritates tissue. Ethane is nonpolar, barely dissolves in water, and passes through without that effect.

Summary

The polar carbon-to-oxygen double bond causes aldehydes and ketones to have higher boiling points than those of ethers and alkanes of similar molar masses but lower than those of comparable alcohols that engage in intermolecular hydrogen bonding. Aldehydes are readily oxidized to carboxylic acids, whereas ketones resist oxidation.

Molecule in the Clinic: Glutaraldehyde (a cold sterilant)

Glutaraldehyde is a high-level disinfectant used for instruments that cannot be heated in an autoclave, such as endoscopes. Its two aldehyde groups react with the amino groups of microbial proteins and cross-link them, which kills bacteria, viruses and spores.

Your turn: The IUPAC name of glutaraldehyde is pentanedial. What does each part of the name tell you about the structure?

Answer
Pentanedial: five carbons, two aldehyde groups (the ending “-dial”), both on chain ends so no numbers are needed.

Your turn: Why must staff wear gloves and work with good ventilation when they use it?

Answer
The same reaction that cross-links microbial proteins also attacks the proteins of skin and airways, so glutaraldehyde irritates them and can cause allergic sensitization.

Lecture Connection

Same routine as Chapter 4: geometry first, then the strongest intermolecular force, then rank. Aldehydes and ketones are one category for ranking purposes, sitting between hydrocarbons and alcohols. The oxidation the section mentions at the end is worked out in Section 5.4.

Geometry, forces and ranking

The carbonyl carbon has three electron groups (two single bonds and the double bond), so its geometry is trigonal planar with 120° angles. The C=O bond is polar, so aldehydes and ketones have dipole–dipole forces. Can two aldehyde (or two ketone) molecules hydrogen bond with each other? No: there is no H bonded to the oxygen. Their boiling and melting points are therefore higher than hydrocarbons of comparable size and shape (they are polar) but lower than alcohols (no hydrogen bonding). Can they hydrogen bond with water? Yes: the carbonyl oxygen is a proton acceptor, so the length of the alkyl chain decides solubility: 4 or fewer carbons, soluble; 5 carbons, sparingly soluble; 6 or more, insoluble. The table compares three compounds of nearly the same molar mass.

Two reminders. First, as the carbon chain grows, the nonpolar part wins: hexanal and 2-hexanone dissolve only slightly in water (a few grams per liter), which is what “insoluble” means in the rule above. Second, an isomeric aldehyde and ketone (propanal, 49 °C, and propanone, 56 °C, both in the section’s table) have very similar boiling points, because both rely on the same C=O dipole. For boiling-point rankings on an exam, treat aldehydes and ketones as one category that sits between hydrocarbons and alcohols. The section's own table adds the ether (ethyl methyl ether, 7 °C) just above the alkane, giving the full order alkane < ether < aldehyde or ketone < alcohol.

Example: Ranking by boiling point and solubility

Problem. Pentane, butanal, 1-butanol and 2-butanone all have molar masses of 72–74 g/mol. (a) Rank them from lowest to highest boiling point. (b) Which are appreciably soluble in water?

Solution. (a) Identify the strongest force each can use with its own kind. Pentane: dispersion only. Butanal and 2-butanone: dipole–dipole from the C=O, no O–H. 1-Butanol: hydrogen bonding. Ranking: pentane < butanal ≈ 2-butanone < 1-butanol (actual values 36, 75, 80 and 118 °C). (b) All three oxygen compounds can accept hydrogen bonds from water, and each has only four carbons, so butanal, 2-butanone and 1-butanol are all soluble (2-butanone and 1-butanol: roughly 7–27 g per 100 mL). Pentane is insoluble.

Check. The aldehyde and ketone land between the hydrocarbon and the alcohol, and the small difference between the two carbonyl isomers is not something you need to predict.

Common carbonyl compounds in the clinic and the lab

In the Lab

In the aldehydes and ketones experiment you will meet three of the section's compounds by smell: formaldehyde (specimen preservative), acetone (nail-polish remover) and benzaldehyde (almond). The chemical tests you then run on an unknown (Tollens', iodoform) are described in Section 5.4.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Ketones you can smell. The section's note on acetone in blood, urine and breath is the chemistry behind a bedside observation. When cells cannot use glucose, as in untreated type 1 diabetes, the liver turns fatty acids into ketone bodies, two of which are acids. The result is diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), a dangerous drop in blood pH. The third ketone body is the ketone acetone, which is volatile (small, dipole–dipole forces only) and leaves through the lungs, giving the fruity "acetone breath" you may notice on a patient. Urine ketone strips detect the ketone bodies; details come in Chapter 15. Formalin. A water solution of formaldehyde (formalin) preserves biopsy and tissue samples by cross-linking proteins, which is the same reactivity that makes free formaldehyde an irritant.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Aldehydes as sterilants. Glutaraldehyde, a five-carbon dialdehyde (OHC–CH2CH2CH2–CHO), is a cold-sterilant for heat-sensitive instruments. Its two aldehyde groups react with amino groups on microbial proteins, cross-linking them so the proteins stop working; formaldehyde fixes tissue specimens the same way. That reactivity is also why these solutions irritate eyes, skin and airways and have a sharp odor, and why instruments must be rinsed thoroughly after soaking. Flavor. Two of the section's aldehydes flavor toothpaste and mouth rinse: vanillin (vanilla) and benzaldehyde (almond, cherry). Acetone. The same ketone that appears in DKA breath is a lab and clinic solvent, used for removing nail polish and in some denture and appliance cleaning; it dissolves both polar and nonpolar residues because the C=O is polar while the two methyl groups are not. Curing lights. The photoinitiator in most light-cured composites is camphorquinone, a compound with two ketone groups; it absorbs blue light and starts the polymerization you saw in Chapter 3.
