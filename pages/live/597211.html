Learning Objectives

  • Describe how dietary fat is digested and absorbed: emulsification by bile salts, hydrolysis by pancreatic lipase to a monoacylglycerol and two fatty acids, and repackaging into chylomicrons.
  • Describe the difference in composition and function between the five classes of lipoproteins.

During times of feasting, lipids enter the body's metabolic pathways through the process of digestion. Any excess energy will be stored in adipose tissues. If food has not been eaten for a while, fasting, lipids mobilized from stored adipose tissue and synthesized in the liver can be used for energy. Transportation of nonpolar lipids through the bloodstream requires binding to water-soluble proteins like serum albumin or packaging in lipoproteins. There are five categories of lipoprotein defined by their composition and density, or the ratio of lipid to protein. Chylomicrons, which have the highest lipid to protein ratio and the lowest density, are responsible for transporting lipids from digestion into the blood and eventually to the liver. Following is a list of the remaining four lipoproteins and their functions:

  • Very low-density lipoproteins (VLDLs) (0.96-1.006 g/cm3) are made in the liver from remnants of chylomicrons and transport triglycerides from the liver to various tissues in the body for energy or storage. As the VLDLs travel through the circulatory system, the lipoprotein lipase strips the VLDL of triglycerides. As triglyceride removal persists, the VLDLs become intermediate-density lipoproteins.
  • Intermediate-density lipoproteins (IDLs) (1.007-1.019 g/cm3) transport a variety of fats and cholesterol left from VLDLs from the tissues to the liver where they are transformed into low-density lipoprotein.
  • Low-density lipoproteins (LDLs) (1.020-1.062 g/cm3) carry cholesterol and other lipids from the liver to tissue throughout the body. LDLs are comprised of very small amounts of triglycerides, and house over 50 percent cholesterol and cholesterol esters. Once inside the cell, the LDL is taken apart and its cholesterol is released and used for membrane formation, steroid synthesis, or if in excess forming arterial plaques.
  • High-density lipoproteins (HDLs) (1.063-1.210 g/cm3) are responsible for carrying cholesterol out of the bloodstream and into the liver, where it is either reused or removed from the body with bile.

Note To Your Health: Good vs. Bad Cholesterol

HDL and LDL are often called “good cholesterol” and “bad cholesterol.” That is a shortcut: the cholesterol molecule is the same in both. What differs is the particle that carries it. LDL delivers cholesterol to the tissues, and when there is too much LDL, cholesterol can build up in artery walls (atherosclerosis). HDL carries cholesterol back to the liver.

A total blood cholesterol under 200 mg/dL is generally considered desirable, and a lower LDL with a higher HDL goes with a lower risk of heart disease. Today, clinicians set each patient’s LDL goal from that person’s overall risk of heart disease, so the target differs from patient to patient.

Diet matters. Saturated fats and trans fats raise LDL, and trans fats also lower HDL. Replacing saturated fats with unsaturated fats (for example olive or canola oil, nuts, and fish) lowers LDL. Omega-3 fatty acids from fish mainly lower triglycerides.

Lecture Connection

The lecture mentions lipoproteins only where the digestion story needs them: chylomicrons carry the triacylglycerol rebuilt in the intestinal wall, and a lipase at the tissues takes it apart again. The five classes above are background for the lipid panel you will read as a health professional, not a list to memorize for the exam.

Digesting fats

About 98% of dietary lipids are triacylglycerols (TAG), the triesters of glycerol and three fatty acids from Chapter 9. Excess energy from carbohydrates and proteins is also stored as TAG in adipocytes (fat cells). TAGs are insoluble in water, so the water-soluble enzymes of saliva have little effect on them, and only about 10% are hydrolyzed by gastric lipase in the stomach. Digesting the rest is a two-part problem: the fat globules must be broken into small particles so that a water-soluble enzyme can reach them, and then the ester bonds must be hydrolyzed.

  • Emulsification. Bile salts, made in the liver from cholesterol and secreted from the gallbladder into the small intestine, act like soaps: a steroid ring that dissolves in fat plus an ionic group that dissolves in water. They break the fat globules into small droplets, giving a water-soluble enzyme far more fat surface to reach. (After lipase acts, bile salts and the products form even smaller clusters called micelles, which carry them to the intestinal cell.) No bonds are broken yet; this is a physical change.
  • Incomplete hydrolysis. Pancreatic lipase (a hydrolase) hydrolyzes the ester linkages between glycerol and the fatty acid units. In the intestine this hydrolysis is incomplete: it removes the two outer fatty acids and leaves a monoacylglycerol. Set up the table: reaction type, hydrolysis (water splits an ester); how can you tell, an ester became a carboxylic acid plus an alcohol; enzyme class, hydrolase; coenzyme, none. Compare saponification in Chapter 9: same ester hydrolysis, but at body pH with an enzyme instead of hot NaOH.
  • Repackaging and absorption. Micelles of free fatty acids and monoacylglycerols pass through the membranes of intestinal cells. Inside, the fatty acids recombine with the monoacylglycerols to re-form TAG, which is coated with phospholipids and proteins to make chylomicrons, water-soluble particles that carry TAG through the lymphatic system into the bloodstream (see the lipoprotein classes above).
  • Storage, then complete hydrolysis. Stored TAG sits in adipocytes, whose cytoplasm is mostly one large fat droplet, beneath the skin and around the organs. When carbohydrate cannot meet energy needs, the hormones glucagon and epinephrine activate a lipase in adipose tissue that completely hydrolyzes stored TAG to glycerol + 3 fatty acids. The fatty acids bind the plasma protein albumin for transport; glycerol dissolves in blood and goes to the liver (Section 15.4). Section 15.2 covers storage and mobilization in detail.

Professor Tip

Know three things. (1) Chylomicrons carry dietary fat from the intestine, through the lymph, to the blood. (2) VLDL carries fat made in the liver (lipogenesis, Section 15.6) out to adipose tissue. (3) Lipoprotein lipase, a hydrolase on the capillary walls of muscle and adipose tissue, hydrolyzes the ester bonds of the triacylglycerol in both, and the free fatty acids enter the cells to be burned or stored. The density ranges, IDL, and the detailed cholesterol traffic of LDL and HDL are not assessed.

Delivery and storage

At capillaries in muscle and adipose tissue, lipoprotein lipase hydrolyzes the triacylglycerol in chylomicrons and VLDL. This is the same ester hydrolysis that pancreatic lipase performed in the gut (see Digesting fats, above). The difference is that now the products go into cells instead of across the intestinal wall. Muscle burns the fatty acids by β-oxidation (Section 15.4); adipocytes re-esterify them with glycerol 3-phosphate and park the triacylglycerol in their single large droplet (Section 15.2). Between meals the traffic reverses. Hormone-sensitive lipase inside the fat cell hydrolyzes the stored fat. The fatty acids then travel bound to albumin, not in a lipoprotein, because free fatty acids are too insoluble to travel alone.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Reading a lipid panel. A fasting lipid panel reports total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, and triglycerides (the clinical word for triacylglycerols). The “fasting” matters because of this section’s chemistry: after a meal the blood is full of chylomicrons, so the triglyceride number would reflect breakfast rather than the patient. High LDL and low HDL together are the pattern linked to hyperlipidemia and atherosclerosis (cholesterol-rich plaque in artery walls). High triglycerides often mean that extra carbohydrate is being turned into fat by lipogenesis. They can also point to poorly controlled diabetes, where too little insulin leaves lipoprotein lipase switched off. Statins lower LDL by inhibiting the liver’s cholesterol synthesis, a pathway that, like fatty acid synthesis, starts from acetyl CoA (Section 15.10); omega-3 fatty acids (EPA and DHA, Chapter 9) mainly lower triglycerides. A nurse who understands which particle carries which lipid can explain why the diet advice for high triglycerides (less sugar and alcohol) differs from the advice for high LDL (less saturated and trans fat).

Summary

  • Bile salts emulsify dietary fat, and pancreatic lipase hydrolyzes triacylglycerols to fatty acids and monoglycerides, which are reassembled and carried in chylomicrons.
  • Lipids travel in the blood packaged in lipoproteins: particles with a coat of protein, phospholipid and cholesterol around a core of triacylglycerols and cholesterol esters.
  • Chylomicrons carry dietary fat from the intestine; VLDL carries fat made in the liver; LDL delivers cholesterol to tissues; HDL returns cholesterol to the liver.
  • Lipoprotein lipase on capillary walls hydrolyzes the triacylglycerols so fatty acids can enter cells.
  • High LDL (“bad”) and low HDL (“good”) cholesterol are linked to atherosclerosis.
