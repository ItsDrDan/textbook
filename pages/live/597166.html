Learning Objectives

  • Explain the functions of enzymes.
  • Explain how enzymes are classified and named.

A catalyst is any substance that increases the rate or speed of a chemical reaction without being changed or consumed in the reaction. Enzymes are biological catalysts, and nearly all of them are proteins. The reaction rates attained by enzymes are truly amazing. In their presence, reactions occur at rates that are a million (106) or more times faster than would be attainable in their absence. Even more amazing, enzymes do this at body temperature (about 37°C) and near-neutral pH (about 7). In the laboratory, speeding up a reaction usually takes high temperature or pressure, strong oxidizing or reducing agents, or strong acids or bases. In addition, enzymes are highly specific in their action; that is, each enzyme catalyzes only one type of reaction in only one compound or a group of structurally related compounds. The compound or compounds on which an enzyme acts are known as its substrates.

Hundreds of enzymes have been purified and studied in an effort to understand how they work so effectively and with such specificity. The resulting knowledge has been used to design drugs that inhibit or activate particular enzymes. HIV treatment is a good example: today’s HIV drugs block enzymes made by the virus (reverse transcriptase, integrase and protease) without blocking the enzymes of the human body, and they let people with HIV live long, healthy lives (Section 12.8).

The first enzymes to be discovered were named according to their source or method of discovery. The enzyme pepsin, which aids in the hydrolysis of proteins, is found in the digestive juices of the stomach (Greek pepsis, meaning “digestion”). Papain, another enzyme that hydrolyzes protein (in fact, it is used in meat tenderizers), is isolated from papayas. As more enzymes were discovered, chemists recognized the need for a more systematic and chemically informative identification scheme. In the current numbering and naming scheme, under the oversight of the Nomenclature Committee of the International Union of Biochemistry and Molecular Biology (IUBMB), enzymes are arranged into classes according to the general type of reaction they catalyze (Table \(\PageIndex{1}\)), with subgroups and secondary subgroups that specify the reaction more precisely.

Each enzyme is assigned a four-digit number, preceded by the prefix EC—for Enzyme Commission—that indicates its group, subgroup, and so forth (you do not need to learn the numbers; you do need the six class names). Thus urease is the enzyme that catalyzes the hydrolysis of urea.

Summary

An enzyme is a biological catalyst, a substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without being changed or consumed in the reaction. A systematic process is used to name and classify enzymes.

Lecture Connection

Two things from lecture live in this section: what a catalyst does to an energy diagram (lower the hill, leave the start and end alone), and how to read an enzyme's name and put it in one of six classes. The six classes come back in every step of metabolism in Chapters 13–15, so this is the table to learn now.

What an enzyme does to the energy diagram

Review from CHEM 3A: a catalyst speeds up a reaction without being used up, and it does so by providing a different route with a lower activation energy (Ea), the energy hill between reactants and products. At body temperature most reactions of metabolism would take years without an enzyme; with one they take milliseconds.

  • Enzymes lower Ea by binding the reactant, called the substrate, in a pocket called the active site and holding it in exactly the right position and shape for the reaction (Section 11.2).
  • Enzymes do not change the overall energy of the reaction (ΔG or ΔH) and do not change the position of equilibrium. They change only how fast equilibrium is reached. A reaction that is uphill in energy stays uphill; the enzyme just makes it happen faster in both directions.
  • Enzymes are not consumed: a catalyst is unchanged by the reaction and is regenerated after each turn. One molecule of carbonic anhydrase can hydrate about a million CO2 molecules per second, over and over. Enzymes typically speed a reaction 106 to 1012 times, which makes them the most effective catalysts known.
  • Most enzymes are globular proteins (Chapter 10): compact, water-soluble, and folded so that a few R groups come together to form the active site. Some enzymes also need a non-protein part, a cofactor or coenzyme (Section 11.5), before they work.

Reading an enzyme's name

Almost every enzyme name ends in -ase, and the rest of the name tells you the substrate, the reaction, or both:

  • Substrate + -ase: lactase acts on lactose, lipase on lipids, urease on urea, sucrase on sucrose, protease on proteins.
  • Substrate + reaction + -ase: lactate dehydrogenase removes hydrogen from lactate; pyruvate decarboxylase removes CO2 from pyruvate; glucose-6-phosphate isomerase rearranges glucose-6-phosphate.
  • A few old names do not follow the rule: pepsin, trypsin, chymotrypsin (digestive proteases) and lysozyme. Learn these as exceptions.

Professor Tip

Read the enzyme's name like a sentence. "Glucose-6-phosphate isomerase" rearranges glucose-6-phosphate. "Lactate dehydrogenase" removes hydrogen from lactate. The name tells you the class and the substrate, which makes the metabolism chapters much easier. When you have only the equation, ask three questions in order: Is NAD+/FAD involved (oxidoreductase)? Is water a reactant that splits a bond (hydrolase) or ATP a reactant that joins two molecules (ligase)? Does a group move between two molecules (transferase), does one molecule split or gain CO2/H2O at a double bond (lyase), or does one molecule simply rearrange (isomerase)?

Two traps worth naming now. Water that adds across a double bond (fumarase, any "hydratase") is a lyase reaction, not hydrolysis; water that splits one molecule into two is hydrolysis. And not every ATP-using enzyme is a ligase: a kinase uses ATP but transfers the phosphate (transferase), while a ligase uses ATP to join two molecules into one. As a general rule, "synthase" (no ATP) is a lyase and "synthetase" (ATP) is a ligase. The one exception in this course is citrate synthase (Chapter 14): it is classified as a ligase.

Worked example

Example 1: Classifying reactions you will meet in metabolism

Problem. Each reaction below appears in Chapters 13–15. Classify the enzyme and explain the clue you used.

  1. glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate + NAD+ + Pi → 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate + NADH + H+
  2. fructose-1,6-bisphosphate → dihydroxyacetone phosphate + glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate
  3. 3-phosphoglycerate → 2-phosphoglycerate
  4. fumarate + H2O → malate
  5. glutamate + oxaloacetate → α-ketoglutarate + aspartate
  6. fatty acid + CoA-SH + ATP → fatty acyl-CoA + AMP + PPi

Solution.

  1. NAD+ becomes NADH: an oxidoreductase (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase). The aldehyde is oxidized.
  2. One six-carbon molecule splits into two three-carbon molecules with no water and no NAD+: a lyase (aldolase).
  3. One reactant, one product, same formula; the phosphate has simply moved from C3 to C2: an isomerase (phosphoglycerate mutase).
  4. Water is a reactant, but nothing is split in two: it adds across the C=C of fumarate (a hydration, Chapter 3). That makes fumarase a lyase, not a hydrolase.
  5. An amino group moves from glutamate to oxaloacetate: a transferase (aspartate transaminase, the AST of a liver panel).
  6. ATP is spent and two molecules (fatty acid and CoA) become one: a ligase (acyl-CoA synthetase).

Check. The two reactions that use water, (d) and a hydrolase such as maltase (maltose + H2O → 2 glucose), look alike until you ask whether the product count went up. Hydrolysis splits one molecule into two; hydration adds water to one molecule and keeps it whole.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Enzymes in the mouth, by class. Saliva carries salivary amylase, a hydrolase that starts starch digestion by cutting α(1→4) links to maltose, which is why starchy snacks that linger on teeth can still feed acid-producing bacteria; lysozyme, a hydrolase that breaks the polysaccharide backbone of bacterial cell walls; and peroxidases (oxidoreductases). Lactoferrin, an iron-binding protein, limits bacterial growth. Plaque bacteria have their own enzymes: Streptococcus mutans uses glucosyltransferase, a transferase, to build sticky glucan chains from sucrose that glue the plaque biofilm to enamel, one reason sucrose is the most cariogenic (cavity-causing) sugar. In periodontitis, bacterial collagenases and proteases, together with the host's own matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs, zinc-dependent hydrolases), break down collagen in the gingiva and periodontal ligament. Fluoride's main anticavity effect is not on an enzyme at all: it is catalytic, speeding the regrowth (remineralization) of calcium phosphate crystals in softened enamel. In sodium monofluorophosphate toothpastes the F− is released by hydrolysis of a P–F bond, which can be chemical or enzyme-catalyzed.

Further reading: M. Epple and J. Enax, "The Chemistry of Dental Care," ChemistryViews (Part 1, Part 3).

Practice 11.1

Classify the enzyme that catalyzes each reaction into one of the six enzyme classes. State the clue you used.

  1. lactose + H2O → glucose + galactose
  2. pyruvate + NADH + H+ → lactate + NAD+
  3. fructose-6-phosphate + ATP → fructose-1,6-bisphosphate + ADP
  4. pyruvate → acetaldehyde + CO2
Answer
  1. Hydrolase (lactase). Water is a reactant and one molecule is split into two.
  2. Oxidoreductase (lactate dehydrogenase). NADH becomes NAD+, so pyruvate is reduced.
  3. Transferase (a kinase). A phosphate group leaves ATP and shows up on the sugar. It is not a ligase, because two molecules do not become one.
  4. Lyase (pyruvate decarboxylase). CO2 is removed with no water and no NAD+.

Check. Ask the three questions in order. NAD+ or FAD in the equation? Only (b). Water splitting a bond, or ATP joining two molecules? Water in (a); ATP appears in (c), but two reactants give two products, so nothing was joined. What is left is a group moving between molecules (c) or one molecule losing CO2 (d).
