Learning Objectives

  • Describe how electron transport and ATP synthase together carry out oxidative phosphorylation, and name the two mobile electron carriers, coenzyme Q (ubiquinone) and cytochrome c.
  • Understand the electron transport process basics, including basic redox reactions and proton pumping in complexes I through IV.
  • Understand the ATP synthesis process, accounting for the ATP yield and body heating.

What is oxidative phosphorylation?

In food catabolism up to this point, the energy is transferred from food to high-energy molecules, such as energetic electron carriers \(\ce{NADH}\) and \(\ce{FADH2}\). Recall that these electron carrier molecules do not deliver electrons alone; they deliver protons and electrons, i.e., \(\ce{2H^{+} + 2e^{-}}\). The electrons in \(\ce{NADH}\) and \(\ce{FADH2}\) are transported through a series of enzymes located in the inner membrane of mitochondria, and ultimately, the \(\ce{2H^{+} + 2e^{-}}\) reduce \(\ce{O2}\) into \(\ce{H2O}\) by the reaction shown below.

\(\ce{4H^{+} + 4e^{-} + O2 -> 2H2O}\)

Energy is released at each step. The energy is converted to electrochemical potential energy by pumping protons (\(\ce{H^{+}}\)) from the matrix to intermembrane spaces through sets of enzymes called complexes I, III, & IV. The electrochemical energy is harnessed by \(\ce{ATP}\) synthase, which allows protons to flow back from the intermembrane space to the matrix and couples the energy released to synthesize higher energy \(\ce{ATP}\) by phosphorylating lower energy \(\ce{ADP}\) with inorganic phosphate (\(\ce{P_i}\)). These interconnected processes of electron transport and \(\ce{ATP}\) synthesis are collectively called oxidative phosphorylation. It happens in the mitochondria of cells as illustrated in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\) and is explained in the following sections.

Electron and proton transport molecules

In addition to \(\ce{NAD^{+}}\) and \(\ce{FAD}\), two mobile electron carriers move electrons between the complexes. Coenzyme Q (ubiquinone, \(\ce{Q}\)) is a small, fat-soluble molecule with a long hydrocarbon tail. It picks up two electrons and two H+ to become \(\ce{QH2}\) and moves freely inside the lipid bilayer of the inner membrane, carrying electrons from Complex I and Complex II to Complex III.

Cytochrome c (Cyt c) is a small, water-soluble protein with a heme group: an iron atom held in a ring, like the heme of hemoglobin. Its iron accepts one electron (\(\ce{Fe^{3+}}\) becomes \(\ce{Fe^{2+}}\)) and then passes it on. Cytochrome c moves along the outer surface of the inner membrane, carrying electrons one at a time from Complex III to Complex IV.

Electron transport chain

In this part 2 of stage 3, electrons are removed from \(\ce{NADH}\) and \(\ce{FADH2}\) and passed on to reduce \(\ce{O2}\) into \(\ce{H2O}\) through a series of enzyme complexes, i.e., complexes I through IV and mobile electron carriers like ubiquinone (\(\ce{Q}\)) and cytochrome c (\(\ce{Cyt \; c}\)) by a process called electron transport chain. At each step, electrons pass to a carrier that holds them more tightly (a higher, more positive reduction potential); O2 at the end of the chain holds them most tightly of all. Energy is released at each step, and more is released when the difference between the two carriers is larger.

The complex I, III, and IV extend from the matrix to the intermembrane space through the inner membrane, as illustrated in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\). These three complexes utilize the energy released to pump protons (\(\ce{H^{+}}\)) from the matrix to the intermembrane space. Complex II extends to the matrix from the inner membrane but does not cross the membrane to intermembrane space. Therefore complex II transports electrons but does not pump protons. The enzyme complexes and the mobile electron carriers involved in the electron transport chain and \(\ce{ATP}\) synthesis are described in the following sub-sections, where the chemical equations and figures are taken from the public domain source via Wikipedia.

Complex I

Complex I, also known as NADH-coenzyme Q oxidoreductase or NADH dehydrogenase, is the first enzyme complex in the electron transport chain. Complex I is illustrated in the figure. NADH gives two electrons to Complex I, which passes them through several iron-containing centers to coenzyme Q, making \(\ce{QH2}\). The energy released is used to pump four H+ from the matrix into the intermembrane space. The overall reaction in Complex I is the following.

\(\ce{NADH + Q +5H^{+}_{matrix} -> NAD^{+} + QH_{2} + 4H^{+}_{intermembrane}}\)

\(\ce{NAD^{+}}\) liberated in this reaction becomes available to oxidize more substrates in the metabolic pathways.

Complex II

Complex II, also known as succinate-Q oxidoreductase or succinate dehydrogenase, is a second entry point to the electron transport chain. It is part of the citric acid cycle and the electron transport chain. Succinate is oxidized to fumarate in the citric acid cycle at the expense of reduction of \(\ce{FAD}\) to \(\ce{FADH2}\) in complex II. The electrons pass from \(\ce{FADH2}\) to coenzyme Q by the following overall reaction.

\(\ce{Succinate + Q -> Fumarate + QH2}\)

Recall that \(\ce{FADH2}\) is less energetic than \(\ce{NADH}\). Since oxidation of \(\ce{FADH2}\) to \(\ce{FAD}\) at complex II releases less energy than oxidation of \(\ce{NADH}\) to \(\ce{NAD^{+}}\) at complex I, complex II does not pump protons from matrix to intermembrane space. The energy released by electron transport is utilized to pump protons in complex III and IV, as described in the next section.

Complex III

Complex III, also known as cytochrome c reductase, takes electrons from \(\ce{QH2}\) and passes them, one at a time, to cytochrome c. Its iron-containing cytochromes do the passing: each iron atom accepts one electron (\(\ce{Fe^{3+}}\) becomes \(\ce{Fe^{2+}}\)) and gives it to the next carrier. Because \(\ce{QH2}\) brings two electrons and cytochrome c takes only one, two cytochrome c molecules are reduced for each \(\ce{QH2}\). Complex III also pumps H+ into the intermembrane space. The overall reaction is the following.

\(\ce{QH2 + 2Cyt \; c_{ox} + 2H^{+}_{matrix} -> Q + 2Cyt \; c_{red} + 4H^{+}_{intermembrane}}\)

Complex IV

Complex IV, also known as cytochrome c oxidase, is the final complex in the electron transport chain. It contains iron (in heme groups) and copper atoms that pass the electrons on to oxygen. Complex IV reduces oxygen into water at the expense of oxidation of \(\ce{Cyt~c}\) and pumps \(\ce{4H^{+}}\) from matrix to intermembrane space as illustrated in the figure and shown in the following overall reaction.

\(\ce{4Cyt \; c_{red} + O2 + 8H^{+}_{matrix} -> 4Cyt \; c_{ox} + 2H2O + 4H^{+}_{intermembrane}}\)

\(\ce{ATP}\)-synthase

\(\ce{ATP}\)-synthase, also known as complex V is the final complex in the oxidative phosphorylation process, i.e., part 3 of stage 3 of food catabolism. It comprises two parts in the shape of a mushroom, as illustrated in the figure. The first part, FO, is the hydrophobic part embedded in the inner membrane (see Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\) a). The FO acts as a pore for the flow of protons (\(\ce{H^{+}}\)) across the membrane. The second part is F1 which is hydrophilic and spheroidal and protrudes into the matrix. \(\ce{ATP}\) synthesis takes place in the F1 part of \(\ce{ATP}\)-synthase. It operates on the principle of chemiosmosis, which is described below.

Chemiosmosis

Water movement from the direction of higher to lower concentration across a semipermeable membrane is called osmosis. When protons (\(\ce{H^{+}}\)) are pumped from the matrix to intermembrane space by complex I, III, & IV, a proton concentration gradient is developed across the membrane. Since protons are positive charge ions (\(\ce{H^{+}}\)), there is also an electrical gradient; collectively, it is called an electrochemical gradient. Chemiosmosis is the flow of ions (\(\ce{H^{+}}\)) across a semipermeable membrane down their electrochemical gradient.

An electrochemical gradient developed due to protons (\(\ce{H^{+}}\)) is a form of electrochemical potential energy. \(\ce{ATP}\)-Synthase harnesses the electrochemical potential energy to synthesize \(\ce{ATP}\) as the protons flow through it, just as electricity is generated as water from a dam flows through a turbine. The higher energy \(\ce{ATP}\) is synthesized by condensing lower energy \(\ce{ADP}\) and phosphate \(\ce{P_{i}}\); this is the phosphorylation half of oxidative phosphorylation. It takes three to four \(\ce{H^{+}}\) to synthesize one \(\ce{ATP}\) by the following overall reaction.

\(\ce{ADP + P_{i} +4H^{+}_{intermembrane} <=> ATP + H2O + 4H^{+}_{matrix}}\)

This is an equilibrium reaction, i.e., when the electrochemical gradient is low, \(\ce{ATP}\)-synthase consumes \(\ce{ATP}\) and returns protons from the matrix to the intermembrane space.

Structure and mechanisms of operation of \(\ce{ATP}\)-synthase

\(\ce{ATP}\)-synthase comprises several protein sub-units. Its two major parts, FO and F1, are illustrated in Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\) a. Note: the subscript in FO is the letter O, not the number zero.

FO portion of \(\ce{ATP}\)-synthase

As protons flow through FO, they turn a ring of proteins in the membrane, the way water turns a turbine in a hydroelectric dam (Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\) a and b).

F1 portion of \(\ce{ATP}\)-synthase

The turning ring spins a central stalk inside the F1 head. Each turn changes the shape of the F1 head step by step, so that ADP and Pi bind, are joined into ATP, and the ATP is released (Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\) c).

Heating the body

\(\ce{NADH}\) is oxidized in the electron transport chain at the expense of reduction of \(\ce{O2}\) by the following overall reaction.

\(\ce{1/2O2 + NADH + H^{+} -> H2O + NAD^{+}}\)

The potential difference between these redox pairs is 1.14 volts, equivalent to about −220 kJ/mol. Oxidation of one NADH produces about 2.5 ATP. Making ATP costs 30.5 kJ/mol, so 30.5 kJ/mol × 2.5 = 76 kJ is stored per mole of NADH. The percentage of energy conserved as ATP, the energy efficiency, is therefore \(\frac{76}{220}\times{100} \approx 35\text{%}\). The remaining 65% of the energy ends up heating the body.

Some compounds, known as uncouplers, uncouple electron transport from \(\ce{ATP}\) synthase, i.e., the electron transport pumps \(\ce{H^{+}}\) as usual but \(\ce{H^{+}}\) return to the matrix without producing \(\ce{ATP's}\). So, all energy released during the electron transport process becomes heat energy. Examples of uncouplers are 2,4-dinitrophenol and dicumarol that combine with \(\ce{H^{+}}\) and, being hydrophobic, carry them through the inner membrane. Some compounds, like oligomycin A prevent \(\ce{ATP}\) synthesis by blocking \(\ce{H^{+}}\)-channels in \(\ce{ATP}\) synthase. Salicylic acid (from aspirin, acetylsalicylic acid), if taken in extreme excess, also acts as an uncoupler: it carries \(\ce{H^{+}}\) back across the inner membrane so the protons bypass \(\ce{ATP}\) synthase.

Certain animals adapted to the cold environment have developed uncoupling systems to generate more heat for heating their body. They contain a large amount of brown fat, a tissue with many mitochondria, and are brown due to iron in the cytochrome in the mitochondria. The electron transport works in the brown fat. Still, the mitochondria have certain proteins embedded in the inner membrane that allow \(\ce{H^{+}}\) to return to the matrix without \(\ce{ATP}\) production. The body of newborn babies loses more heat per unit mass because of the larger surface area to mass ratio. Newborn babies have brown fat around the neck, between the shoulder blades, and around the kidneys and spine, and the heat it makes warms the blood flowing past. Adults keep only small deposits of brown fat, mainly around the neck and shoulders (Section 15.2).

Valley Connection: Carbon Monoxide, the Winter Poison That Jams Complex IV

A cold-weather danger. On cold Valley winter nights, homes run gas furnaces, wall heaters and fireplaces, and when the power goes out, some families turn to generators, grills or camp stoves. Anything that burns fuel makes carbon monoxide (CO).1 Winter raises the risk because people lean harder on heating appliances.3 In the U.S., CO poisoning not linked to fires kills more than 400 people a year and sends over 100,000 to emergency departments.1 In California it causes more than 600 ED visits a year, with the highest rates in people 65 and older.5 Generators, grills and camp stoves should never be run inside a home, basement or garage.2

Two targets, one result. CO binds the iron in hemoglobin’s heme about 200–250 times more tightly than O2 does, so less oxygen is delivered.3 But CO also binds cytochrome c oxidase (Complex IV), the last stop of the electron transport chain, where O2 accepts electrons and becomes water.3 With Complex IV blocked, electrons back up, protons stop being pumped, and ATP synthase loses its gradient. Cells switch to anaerobic glycolysis and make lactic acid. That is why CO disrupts oxygen use, not just oxygen delivery.2

Smoke from a house fire can add a second poison. Burning plastic, vinyl, wool or silk releases hydrogen cyanide, and fire victims often have both CO and cyanide in their blood.4 Cyanide blocks cytochrome c oxidase too (the Molecule in the Clinic box below explains how), and a high blood lactate is a clue that it is present.4

In the clinic. EMTs, ER nurses and respiratory therapists can’t trust a regular pulse oximeter here, because it misreads carboxyhemoglobin as oxyhemoglobin.2,3 Patients get 100% oxygen, usually for about 4–5 hours until symptoms clear, and hyperbaric oxygen is considered for severe cases and is preferred in pregnancy.2

The law. California’s Carbon Monoxide Poisoning Prevention Act of 2010 (SB 183) requires CO alarms in homes that have a fuel-burning heater or appliance, a fireplace or an attached garage: single-family homes by July 1, 2011, and other dwellings by January 1, 2013.6

At work. CO is also a job hazard. OSHA lists warehouses and forklift operators among the places and jobs where workers can breathe harmful levels of CO,7 and Iowa investigators traced a series of poisonings to propane-powered forklifts run in poorly ventilated warehouses and production areas.8 In Valley packing sheds, cold-storage rooms and warehouses, where doors are kept shut, the protections are the same: good ventilation, well-maintained engines and heaters, CO monitors, and electric or battery-powered equipment where it can be used safely.7,8

Think about it: A man ran a generator in his garage during an outage. He is confused, his pulse oximeter reads 98%, and his blood lactate is high. Explain both findings.

Answer
A standard pulse oximeter cannot tell carboxyhemoglobin from oxyhemoglobin, so it shows a falsely normal saturation even though much of his hemoglobin is carrying CO instead of O2. The lactate is high because CO also binds Complex IV. Electrons can’t reach O2, the proton gradient and ATP synthesis fall, and cells rely on anaerobic glycolysis, reducing pyruvate to lactate to regenerate NAD+.

Summary of glucose catabolism

Aerobic catabolism of a glucose molecule converts a \(\ce{6C's}\) glucose molecule to six carbon dioxide along with \(\ce{ATP's}\), \(\ce{GTP's}\), and reduced coenzyme \(\ce{NADH's}\), and \(\ce{FADH2{'s}}\), as shown in the figure.

Glucose first goes through glycolysis that produces two pyruvate and overall two \(\ce{ATP}\) and two \(\ce{NADH}\). Two pyruvates go through oxidative decarboxylation producing two acetyl-\(\ce{CoA}\) and two \(\ce{NADH}\). Each acetyl-\(\ce{CoA}\) goes through one turn of the citric acid cycle. So, the two turns of the citric acid cycles convert two acetyl-\(\ce{CoA}\) into four \(\ce{CO2}\), six \(\ce{NADH}\), two \(\ce{FADH2}\), and two \(\ce{GTP}\). These steps are summarized in Figure \(\PageIndex{3}\) and Figure \(\PageIndex{4}\) shown below.

Figure \(\PageIndex{3}\) illustrates the summary of aerobic catabolism of glucose and production of \(\ce{ATP's}\), \(\ce{GTP's}\), and reduced coenzyme \(\ce{NADH's}\), and \(\ce{FADH2{'s}}\) at different stages.

Comparison of \(\ce{ATP}\) yield in aerobic and anaerobic catabolism of glucose

Anaerobic catabolism, i.e., the two forms of fermentation shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{4}\) yields a net two \(\ce{ATP's}\) from a glucose molecule.

Aerobic catabolism yields 30–32 ATP per glucose (32 in the liver with the malate–aspartate shuttle, 30 in muscle and brain; see Section 14.5). Two \(\ce{ATP's}\) are produced before the citric acid cycle and two \(\ce{GTP's}\), six \(\ce{NADH's}\), and two \(\ce{FADH2{'s}}\) are produced during the citric acid cycle (ten \(\ce{NADH's}\) in all, counting the two from glycolysis and the two from pyruvate oxidation). \(\ce{GTP's}\) later convert to \(\ce{ATP's}\). The reduced coenzymes, i.e., \(\ce{NADH's}\) and \(\ce{FADH2{'s}}\) enter the electron transport chain and produce heat and \(\ce{ATP's}\) as explained in the next section.

Account of \(\ce{ATP}\) production from complete oxidation of glucose

The full ATP tally for one glucose, including the shuttle that decides between 32 and 30 ATP, is in Section 14.5. Each GTP counts as one ATP. Overall, with the malate–aspartate shuttle:

\(\ce{\underbrace{C6H12O6}_{Glucose} + 6O2 +32ADP + 32Pi -> 6CO2 + 6H2O + 32ATP}\)

Molecule in the Clinic: Cyanide and its antidote

Cyanide (CN−), released when plastics, wool or silk burn, binds the iron of cytochrome c oxidase (Complex IV). Electron transport stops, O2 can no longer be used, and ATP production collapses. The antidote hydroxocobalamin is a form of vitamin B12: its cobalt binds cyanide to form cyanocobalamin, which the kidneys excrete (turning the urine red).

Your turn: Blood lactate rises quickly in cyanide poisoning. Why?

Answer
With Complex IV blocked, NADH cannot pass its electrons to O2, so NAD+ is not regenerated by the chain. Cells keep glycolysis running by reducing pyruvate to lactate, which regenerates NAD+.

Lecture Connection

Think of the chain as four stations and two shuttles, then one turbine. The lecture wants the order the electrons visit the stations, which stations pump protons (and therefore why NADH is worth 2.5 ATP and FADH2 only 1.5), what ATP synthase’s two parts do, and what happens when a poison blocks a station or an uncoupler drains the gradient. The section above goes far deeper into the machinery; the parts you own are listed below.

Professor Tip

You are responsible for the F0/F1 division of labor, the chemiosmotic model, the stations and carriers below, and the inhibitor and uncoupler examples; the names of the metal centers inside each complex are background. You will see the membrane part of ATP synthase written FO (for the inhibitor oligomycin) or F0; both mean the same part.

Four stations, two shuttles

The chain sits in the inner mitochondrial membrane, which is folded into cristae to give it more surface area.

  • Complex I (NADH dehydrogenase, NADH–coenzyme Q reductase): takes 2 e− from NADH, passes them to CoQ, and pumps H+ out of the matrix. The NAD+ it regenerates goes back to oxidize more substrates in the cycle.
  • Complex II (succinate dehydrogenase, succinate–coenzyme Q reductase): the same enzyme as step 6 of the cycle. Its FADH2 never leaves the complex; the two electrons go straight to CoQ. No protons are pumped.
  • Coenzyme Q (ubiquinone): a small, fat-soluble molecule with a long hydrocarbon tail, so it dissolves in the lipid bilayer and diffuses between complexes, carrying electrons from I and II to III. It is not a protein.
  • Complex III (coenzyme Q–cytochrome c reductase): takes electrons from reduced CoQ, passes them to cytochrome c one at a time, and pumps H+.
  • Cytochrome c: a small water-soluble protein with an iron-containing heme group. It sits on the outer face of the inner membrane and carries one electron at a time from III to IV.
  • Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase): collects four electrons, hands them to one O2 molecule along with 4 H+, and makes 2 H2O. It pumps H+ too. This is where you actually “use” nearly all of the oxygen you breathe.

The chemiosmotic model and ATP synthase. Three of the complexes (I, III, IV) also work as proton pumps, moving H+ from the matrix side of the inner membrane to the intermembrane space; some of those H+ come from the reduced carriers and some from the matrix. The [H+] outside becomes higher than inside, creating a hydrogen ion gradient; this is the chemiosmotic model, which links the energy of electron transport to a proton gradient. To equalize the pH and charges, H+ returns to the matrix through a channel in ATP synthase. The enzyme has two parts: the F0 subunit is the channel for proton flow, and the F1 subunit is the site where ADP + Pi are joined into ATP. As protons pass through F0, energy is released and used to make ATP in F1. The ATP must then be moved out of the matrix to be used; for each ATP that leaves, an ADP, a Pi, and an H+ move in. The whole process, oxidation of NADH and FADH2 coupled to phosphorylation of ADP, is oxidative phosphorylation.

Why 2.5 and 1.5. Electrons from NADH pass through three proton pumps (I, III, IV); electrons from FADH2 enter at Complex II, which does not pump, and pass through only two (III, IV). Fewer protons pumped means less ATP: 2.5 per NADH, 1.5 per FADH2.

Proton bookkeeping: where 2.5 and 1.5 come from

  • For each pair of electrons: Complex I moves about 4 H+, Complex III about 4, and Complex IV about 2 (it moves 4 for each O2, which takes two electron pairs).
  • NADH (I, III and IV): 4 + 4 + 2 = about 10 H+. FADH2 (III and IV only): 4 + 2 = about 6 H+.
  • ATP synthase uses about 3 H+ to make one ATP, and about 1 more H+ brings ADP and Pi in as the ATP goes out. That is about 4 H+ per ATP delivered to the cell.
  • NADH: 10 ÷ 4 = 2.5 ATP. FADH2: 6 ÷ 4 = 1.5 ATP.

You do not need to memorize the proton counts. They show where the course values 2.5 and 1.5 come from.

Practice: (a) How many electrons are transferred when one FADH2 is oxidized at Complex II? (b) Arrange in the order they appear in electron transport: cytochrome c, FAD, coenzyme Q. (c) What is the final electron acceptor: H2O, FAD, NAD+, or O2?

Answer
(a) Two (FADH2 → FAD + 2 H+ + 2 e−). (b) FAD, coenzyme Q, cytochrome c. (c) O2, which is reduced to water at Complex IV.

Regulation of electron transport

Electron transport is regulated by the availability of ADP, Pi, O2, and NADH; low levels of any of these slow the chain and decrease ATP formation. The strongest dependence is on ADP: when a cell is active and consuming ATP rapidly, the elevated ADP activates ATP synthesis, and the chain runs only as fast as H+ can return through ATP synthase (“respiratory control”). A resting cell with lots of ATP and little ADP breathes slowly; a working cell breathes fast. This is the molecular reason your oxygen consumption rises when you exercise, and why, as Section 14.2 notes, working skeletal muscle can account for almost 90% of the body’s oxygen use.

Inhibitors and uncouplers

Because the chain is one line, blocking any station stops everything after it, and the carriers before the block pile up in their reduced form. Uncouplers do something different: they let H+ leak back across the membrane without going through ATP synthase, so the chain keeps running (fast, even) but the energy leaves as heat instead of ATP. Health Career Connection

  • Respiratory therapy and nursing – why oxygen matters: O2 is the final electron acceptor. Without it, electron transport stops, NADH cannot be recycled, and cells fall back to glycolysis alone (2 ATP instead of about 30–32 per glucose). This is the biochemistry behind hypoxia.
  • Nursing – newborn thermoregulation: Brown fat in infants contains the uncoupling protein thermogenin, which lets H+ leak back without making ATP. The energy is released as heat, which helps keep newborns warm; the section explains why their large surface-to-mass ratio makes this necessary.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Poisons that block Complex IV. A patient pulled from a house fire can have both carbon monoxide and cyanide in the blood. CO occupies hemoglobin, so O2 is not delivered; cyanide blocks cytochrome c oxidase, so the O2 that does arrive cannot be used (Molecule in the Clinic, above). Either way the tissues switch to anaerobic glycolysis and blood lactate rises, causing metabolic acidosis. High-flow oxygen helps displace CO from hemoglobin, but more oxygen alone cannot unblock the enzyme, which is why cyanide needs its own antidote.

Sepsis. In severe infection, inflammatory signals appear to impair mitochondria so that cells use oxygen poorly even when it is delivered. Lactate can rise despite a normal oxygen saturation, which is one reason serial lactate levels are tracked in sepsis.

Temperature and metabolic rate. Every rise of 1 °C in body temperature raises oxygen demand by roughly 10%, so a fever is costly for a patient with poor lungs or heart; therapeutic hypothermia after cardiac arrest does the opposite, lowering the brain’s ATP demand. Inherited mitochondrial diseases, caused by mutations in electron transport proteins, show up first in high-demand tissues such as muscle and brain, often with a high resting lactate.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Tissues need a constant oxygen supply. Cells store almost no ATP, only seconds’ worth, so any tissue whose blood flow stops loses oxidative phosphorylation at once. This is why ischemia (loss of blood supply) leads to necrosis (tissue death): glycolysis alone cannot meet the demand. Osteonecrosis of the jaw, a feared complication of some bone-active drugs and of radiation to the head and neck, involves a compromised blood supply to bone that cannot keep up with the oxygen demand of healing. The vasoconstrictor epinephrine in a local anesthetic cartridge deliberately reduces blood flow (and oxygen delivery) in a small area for a short time, which is useful for a cleaner field but is one reason the dose and the site are chosen carefully.

Plaque bacteria run on fermentation. The streptococci that start dental caries (tooth decay) lack a full electron transport chain; they make ATP by glycolysis and dump the electrons onto pyruvate to form lactic acid, the acid that demineralizes enamel below pH 5.5. The obligate anaerobes deep in periodontal pockets cannot tolerate oxygen at all. Chlorhexidine rinses work by disrupting bacterial membranes, which is where a bacterium’s energy-producing machinery sits. Finally, a patient with a fever or an active infection has a higher metabolic rate and oxygen demand, something to note before a long appointment.
