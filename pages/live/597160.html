Learning Objectives

  • Define the secondary structure of proteins and understand the structural features of major secondary structures, including \(\alpha\)-helix, \(\beta\)-pleated sheet, random coil, and triple helix structures.

The polypeptide backbone is composed of repeated \(\ce{-C-C-N{-}}\) units where the side chain (\(\ce{-R}\)) is hanging on the first \(\ce{C}\). The second carbon is the carbonyl (\(\ce{C=O}\)) group and the nitrogen is the amine (\(\ce{-NH{-}}\)) group in amide. The polymer chain acquires a conformation that:

  1. maximizes hydrogen bonding between the \(\ce{C=O}\) and \(\ce{-NH{-}}\) of the same chain (intramolecular) or among the neighboring chains (intermolecular), and
  2. minimizes the steric strain due to the \(\ce{-R}\) groups.

This way, the polymer backbone acquires repetitive patterns in portions of the backbone.

Secondary structure of proteins

The secondary structure of proteins comprises organized regions of polypeptide backbone stabilized by hydrogen bonds between atoms.

The two common secondary structures in proteins are the \(\alpha\)-helix and the \(\beta\)-pleated sheet. Portions of the backbone that do not follow a repeating pattern are called random coils. Triple-helix is another common secondary structure found in collagen proteins in connective tissues. These secondary structures are illustrated in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\) below and described in the following sections.

\(\alpha\)-Helix

In the \(\alpha\)-helix portion, the polypeptide chain acquires a coil shape spiraling clockwise from N-terminus to C-terminus, held by hydrogen bonds between the carbonyl groups and amine groups in the backbone and side chains protruding outwards, as illustrated in the model shown on the left.

Each \(\ce{N-H}\) is hydrogen bonded with a \(\ce{C=O}\) group four units away, shown by hashed lines in the model. Each side chain (\(\ce{-R}\)) group on an amino acid residue is protruding outwards from the helix.

The \(\alpha\)-helix is represented by a right-handed spiral shape ribbon, as shown by the red-color ribbon in the structure of glucagon shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\). The side chain on each amino acid can be seen protruding out from the helix. The random coil portion of glucagon is shown by a blue-color tube. Since the side chains hang out in free space, short and long-side chain amino acids can easily be accommodated in an \(\alpha\)-helix without steric strain. Keratin, a fibrous protein of hair, fingernails, horns, and wool, is composed of a major portion of \(\alpha\)-helix.

\(\beta\)-Pleated sheet

The \(\beta\)-Pleated sheet is a portion of polypeptide chains in which sections of polypeptide chains are aligned parallel or antiparallel and held together by hydrogen bonds between the carbonyl groups and amine groups of the neighboring polypeptide chains. The \(\beta\)-Pleated sheet is usually represented as a ribbon with an arrowhead pointing toward the C-terminus, as shown on the right.

Since the polypeptide backbone is in a zig-zag shape, multiple chains running parallel to each other in a \(\beta\)-pleated sheet is a pleated shape as shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{3}\). The two neighboring chains are parallel if both have the N-terminus on the same side, and antiparallel if the two adjacent chains have their N-termini on opposite sides; one drawing of each type is shown here. The neighboring chains may be different polypeptides or the same polypeptide chain with hairpin-like bends at the ends.

The alternate \(\ce{C=O}\) and \(\ce{N-H}\) on the backbone of neighboring chains point towards each other and hydrogen bond with each other, as shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{3}\). The other set of alternate \(\ce{C=O}\) and \(\ce{N-H}\) point to the other side and establish hydrogen bonds with the chains on the other sides. These hydrogen bonds give mechanical strength to the proteins. Silk is made of a fibrous protein fibroin with a significant portion as \(\beta\)-pleated sheets.

The alternate side chains (\(\ce{-R}\)) point above and below in a \(\beta\)-pleated sheet. The amino acids in \(\beta\)-pleated sheets usually have short side chains, such as glycine, valine, alanine, and serine, as the long chains cause more steric strain.

Random coil

Most of the proteins have a mix of \(\alpha\)-helix and \(\beta\)-pleated sheets and organized but not repeated structures between the two that are called random coils as illustrated in Figure \(\PageIndex{4}\). The random coils are represented by tubes.

Triple helix

The triple helix is a secondary structure found in collagen. Collagen is a strong, rope-like structural protein, present in connective tissues of the tendon, cartilage, blood vessels, skin, and bone. It is the most abundant protein of vertebrates making up 30% to 35% of their proteins by weight.

The triple helix is made of three collagen peptides which are left-handed helices that are placed together on a common axis and displaced (translated) along the axis, forming a right-handed triple helix, as shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\) and Figure \(\PageIndex{5}\).

The collagen peptide is composed of repeat units of Gly-X-Y, where X is usually proline, and Y is generally hydroxyproline - a proline with a hydroxyl (\(\ce{-OH}\)) group installed on the side chain. Proline has a secondary \(\alpha\)-amine group that causes a bend in the peptide chain, which is needed for the helical structure of collagen peptides. The extra (\(\ce{-OH}\)) in the side chain of hydroxyproline allows more hydrogen bonding in the collagen peptide, which makes connective tissues stronger.

Lecture Connection

Secondary structure is about the backbone, not the side chains. Both the α-helix and the β-pleated sheet are held by hydrogen bonds between a backbone C=O and a backbone N–H; the R groups just point out of the way. If a question mentions R groups, it is asking about tertiary structure, not secondary.

Helix, sheet, and what holds them

  • In an α-helix the chain coils like a spiral staircase, with each backbone C=O hydrogen-bonded to the N–H four residues further along; the R groups point outward. In a β-pleated sheet stretches of chain lie side by side, zig-zagging, with hydrogen bonds between the C=O of one strand and the N–H of the neighbor.
  • Both are held by backbone hydrogen bonds. When the N–H and C=O are on the same chain these are intramolecular hydrogen bonds (intra = within one molecule; inter would mean between different molecules). The R groups are not involved; which arrangement forms depends on which one lets the backbone hydrogen-bond best around the side chains it carries.
  • Keratin in hair is mostly α-helix; silk is mostly β-sheet; most globular proteins contain both, joined by the "random coil" stretches the section text describes.
  • The triple helix of collagen is the third kind of secondary structure you need: three chains wind around each other, each a repeat of Gly-X-Y with lots of proline and hydroxyproline. (Because it involves three separate chains, some textbooks call it quaternary structure; this course files it with secondary structure because it is held by hydrogen bonds between backbone groups.)

Professor Tip

The answer to "what holds secondary structure together" is always hydrogen bonds between backbone C=O and N–H groups. Call them intramolecular when the two strands belong to the same chain (a helix, or a sheet that folds back on itself), intermolecular only when two separate chains line up, as in silk.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Collagen, a triple-helix protein, makes up the periodontal ligament, gingival connective tissue, and the organic matrix of dentin and bone. Vitamin C is needed by the enzyme that converts proline residues in collagen to hydroxyproline, and the extra –OH groups are what let the three chains hydrogen-bond into a stable triple helix. Without vitamin C the helix is weak and the tissue falls apart: deficiency (scurvy) shows up first in the mouth as bleeding, swollen gums and loose teeth, because gingival collagen turns over quickly. Hair gets its shape from a different secondary structure: the α-helices of keratin, cross-linked by disulfide bonds (Section 10.8).

Summary

  • Secondary structure is the local folding of the backbone: the α-helix, the β-pleated sheet, the triple helix of collagen, and random coil.
  • Secondary structure is held by hydrogen bonds between backbone C=O and N–H groups, not by the side chains.
  • Keratin (hair, nails) is mostly α-helix, silk fibroin is β-sheet, and collagen is a triple helix of three chains.
