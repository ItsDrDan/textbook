Learning Objectives

  • Describe the peptide bond and the disulfide bond and their properties.
  • Define the primary structure of a protein, write it from the N-terminus to the C-terminus, and explain why it matters.

Peptide

An amine reacts with a carboxylic acid and makes an amide by eliminating water. Amino acids have both an amine and a carboxylic acid group. If the amine group of one amino acid reacts with the carboxylic acid of another, the two amino acids become bonded through an amide bond. For example, alanine and glycine become bonded through an amide bond, as illustrated below.

Peptide bond

An amide bond that links two amino acids is called a peptide bond or peptide linkage. For example, a peptide bond that links alanine and glycine is highlighted in a brown box in the above illustration.

Two amino acids linked by a peptide bond make a dipeptide; alanylglycine, shown above, is an example. A dipeptide still has a free –NH3+ at one end and a free –COO− at the other, so more amino acids can be added at either end. Three amino acids linked by peptide bonds make a tripeptide, four are called tetrapeptide, five are pentapeptide, six are hexapeptide, seven are heptapeptide, and so on. A tripeptide of glycine, histidine, and lysine is shown below.

Shorter chains of amino acids linked by peptide bonds are called peptides; longer ones are called polypeptides. Polypeptides and proteins are sometimes used interchangeably; in this course a chain of more than 40 amino acids is called a protein (Section 10.4). Amino acids in peptides are usually called residues.

Structure of a peptide backbone

The backbone of a peptide chain is \(\ce{-C-C-N{-}}\) where the middle \(\ce{C}\) is the carbonyl \(\ce{C=O}\) and \(\ce{C-N}\) is the peptide bond. The C–N peptide bond behaves partly like a double bond.

Because of this, the peptide bond is planar and does not rotate. Therefore, the four groups around the peptide bond (the carbonyl O and its α-carbon on the C side, the N–H hydrogen and its α-carbon on the N side) exist in the same plane as in the case of alkenes. Free rotation is around the other \(\sigma\)-bonds in the peptide backbone. Therefore, the structure of the peptide backbone is like cards connected by a swivel at the opposite corners, as shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\).

The peptide bond's rigidity limits the peptide backbone's possible orientations, affecting its secondary and tertiary structure. The \(\ce{N-H}\) groups can establish hydrogen bonds with the \(\ce{C=O}\) groups within the same chain or between the neighboring chains that play an essential role in determining the secondary and tertiary structures of proteins that are described in a later section.

Disulfide bond

Amino acid cysteine has a thiol (\(\ce{-SH}\)) group that can easily be oxidized to disulfide (\(\ce{-S-S{-}}\)) bond or disulfide linkage linking two cysteines into a dimer called cystine, as illustrated below.

When a cysteine residue makes a disulfide bond with another cysteine residue in the same chain or another chain, it provides a covalent linkage that binds parts of the same chain or two different chains, as shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\). Examples of both types are found in the structure of insulin, which is composed of two polypeptides joined by disulfide linkages, as shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\).

Naming peptides

Peptides are written and named from the N-terminus (the residue with the free –NH3+) to the C-terminus (the residue with the free –COO−). Every residue except the C-terminal one takes a -yl ending: the dipeptide shown above is alanylglycine (Ala-Gly, AG), and the tripeptide is glycylhistidyllysine (Gly-His-Lys, GHK). The full naming rules and practice are in Section 10.4.

What is the primary structure of proteins?

Primary structure of proteins

The primary structure of peptides or proteins is the sequence of amino acids linked together by peptide bonds. For example, the primary structures of the dipeptide and tripeptides shown above are Ala-Gly and Gly-His-Lys.

The primary structure of a protein is shown as a sequence of amino acids written from the N-terminus to the C-terminus. When the sequence of amino acids is known, three-letter abbreviations are separated by hyphens, e.g., Gly-His-Lys. When the sequence of amino acids in a peptide is not known, the three-letter abbreviations of the constituent amino acid are listed, separated by commas. For example, Ala, Gly could mean Ala-Gly or Gly-Ala, which are different compounds with different properties related to each other as constitutional isomers. Similarly, Gly, His, Lys could mean any one of the following six constitutional isomers: Gly-His-Lys, Gly-Lys-His, His-Lys-Gly, His-Gly-Lys, Lys-Gly-His, or Lys-His-Gly.

The number of constitutional isomers increases exponentially as the number of amino acids in the peptide increases. Constitutional isomers of a polypeptide of n amino acids chosen from 20 amino acids commonly found in proteins are given by 20n. For example, a polypeptide containing 60 amino acids selected from 20 amino acids found in proteins may have 2060, i.e., 1078, which is an enormous number of possibilities. This analysis shows that the number of proteins that can be synthesized using 20 amino acids is enormously large. An analogy is the entire English language, written with only the 26 letters of the alphabet.

When the primary structure of a polypeptide is modified, its function is affected. The extent of the effect depends on the number of amino acids replaced and their nature. For example, human insulin comprises two peptides: chain A of 21 amino acids and chain B of 30 amino acids, two chains joined by disulfide linkages, as shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\). The amino acids at positions 8, 9, and 10 in chain A (-Thr-Ser-Ile-) and position 30 in chain B (-Thr) in human insulin are replaced with -Ala-Ser-Val- and -Ala, respectively, in bovine insulin, but the two perform the same function. Bovine (cow) insulin was once used to treat people with diabetes, but it caused more allergic reactions. It is no longer sold in the United States; today people use human insulin or modified human insulins (insulin analogs) made by genetically engineered microorganisms.

As shown below, vasopressin and oxytocin are two nonapeptides that differ in two amino acid residues at positions 3 and 8. Their cysteine residues form a disulfide bond, and the carboxylate group (\(\ce{-COO^{-}}\)) on C-terminus is converted to a primary amide ((\(\ce{-CONH2}\)).

Vasopressin and oxytocin are both made in the hypothalamus and released by the pituitary gland, but they have different functions. Vasopressin is an antidiuretic hormone that regulates blood pressure by adjusting the water reabsorbed by the kidneys. Oxytocin stimulates uterine contractions in labor.

Another example where a slight change in the primary structure of a protein alters its function significantly is sickle cell anemia. Hemoglobin in the red blood cell is responsible for carrying oxygen. Hemoglobin comprises four polypeptides, two alpha chains, and two beta chains containing 574 amino acid residues. A change of glutamic acid (a hydrophilic amino acid) with valine (a hydrophobic amino acid) in the sixth position of the two beta-chains changes the structure of hemoglobin so much that it causes the red blood cells to change from a rounded shape to a sickle body. Sickled cells do not function properly and block the blood flow -a medical condition called sickle cell anemia, illustrated in The Figure on the right.

Some examples and uses of peptides

Peptides have different functions in the body of living things. Some of them are commercially important also. For example, aspartame, about 180 times sweeter than sucrose and used as a sugar substitute, is a methyl ester of a dipeptide Asp-Phe, shown below.

The tripeptide Gly-His-Lys shown before is a human copper-binding peptide with wound healing and skin remodeling activity. Met-enkephalin (Tyr-Gly-Gly-Phe-Met) is a pentapeptide related to pain signals in the body.

Vasopressin and oxytocin are two nonapeptide hormones released by the pituitary gland. Vasopressin is an antidiuretic hormone that regulates blood pressure by adjusting the water reabsorbed by the kidneys. Oxytocin stimulates uterine contractions in labor.

Substance P (SP) is an undecapeptide (Arg-Pro-Lys-Pro-Gln-Gln-Phe-Phe-Gly-Leu-Met) that is a neuropeptide acting as a neurotransmitter and as a neuromodulator. Insulin is a combination of two polypeptides linked by disulfide linkage that regulates glucose in the blood. Malfunctioning of insulin causes diabetes.

Molecule in the Clinic: Insulin

Insulin is a small protein of 51 amino acids in two chains: an A chain of 21 and a B chain of 30, linked by two disulfide bonds, with a third disulfide inside the A chain. Rapid-acting “insulin lispro” differs from human insulin only in that the amino acids at positions B28 and B29 (proline and lysine) are swapped, which makes it cluster less and absorb faster after injection.

Your turn: What bonds hold the A and B chains together, and which amino acid forms them?

Answer
Covalent disulfide bonds (–S–S–), formed between the side chains of two cysteine residues.

Your turn: Swapping two amino acids in lispro changes which level of protein structure directly?

Answer
The primary structure, the amino acid sequence. The change in how the molecules associate follows from it.

Lecture Connection

Primary structure is the sequence, written N-terminus to C-terminus, and it is the only level held by covalent peptide bonds. One wrong residue can change everything downstream, which is why the lecture spends time on sickle cell hemoglobin and on insulin.

Why one residue matters

  • Primary structure is covalent: it survives heat, alcohol, acid and heavy metals, and only hydrolysis (Section 10.10) breaks it.
  • Sickle cell hemoglobin differs from normal hemoglobin at one position out of 146 in the β chain: Glu → Val at position 6. A charged, hydrophilic side chain is swapped for a hydrophobic one on the surface of the protein. Deoxygenated hemoglobin molecules stick to one another through that hydrophobic patch, form fibers, and bend red cells into sickles that block capillaries. Because the Glu is gone, HbS carries one less negative charge than HbA and moves more slowly toward the positive electrode in electrophoresis (Section 10.2), which is how the lab tells them apart.
  • Insulin is a 51-residue peptide hormone: an A chain (21 residues) and a B chain (30) linked by two disulfide bonds, with a third inside the A chain. Swallowed insulin is just protein to the stomach; pepsin and trypsin hydrolyze it to amino acids, so it must be injected.
  • Aspartame, the sweetener in sugar-free gum, is the methyl ester of the dipeptide Asp-Phe. It is hydrolyzed in the gut to aspartate, phenylalanine and methanol, which is why products that contain it carry a warning for people with phenylketonuria (PKU).

Worked example

Example 1: Name a tripeptide and find its hydrolysis products

Problem. A tripeptide has the sequence Ala-Cys-Gly. (a) Which residue is the N-terminus and which is the C-terminus? (b) Give its full name and its one-letter form. (c) What products form when it is completely hydrolyzed?

Solution.

(a) Peptides are written from the N-terminus, so alanine (free –NH3+) is the N-terminus and glycine (free –COO−) is the C-terminus. Cysteine in the middle has used both of its groups in the two peptide bonds.

(b) Change the ending of every residue except the last to -yl: alanine → alanyl, cysteine → cysteinyl, glycine stays glycine. Run them together: alanylcysteinylglycine. One-letter form: A, C, G → ACG.

(c) Complete hydrolysis adds one water across each of the two peptide bonds and returns the three free amino acids as zwitterions: alanine + cysteine + glycine. No new compounds form; the residues simply get their –NH3+ and –COO− groups back.

Check. Three residues, two peptide bonds, two waters consumed in hydrolysis. Reversing the letters (GCA, glycylcysteinylalanine) would be a different tripeptide with glycine at the N-terminus, so the order in the name must match the order in the sequence.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Albumin, the most abundant globular protein in plasma (normal about 3.5–5.0 g/dL), is too large to leave the capillaries, so it holds water in the blood by oncotic pressure (the osmotic pull of plasma proteins). In liver disease, malnutrition or kidney loss, hypoalbuminemia (low albumin) lets fluid leak into tissues: edema. The urine dipstick protein pad detects mainly albumin; a positive result, proteinuria, means the kidney filter is leaking protein that should have stayed in the blood. In phenylketonuria (PKU) the enzyme that converts phenylalanine to tyrosine is missing, phenylalanine builds up and damages the developing brain, so newborns are screened and the diet (and aspartame) is restricted. A single-residue change is enough for disease: in sickle cell disease the Glu → Val swap described above causes vaso-occlusive pain crises and hemolytic anemia. Chapter 15 picks up nitrogen balance: protein intake versus the nitrogen lost as urea, negative after surgery, burns or starvation.

Summary

  • Amino acids are joined by peptide bonds (amide bonds) between the α-COOH of one and the α-NH2 of the next; a disulfide bond can link two cysteine side chains.
  • A peptide is written and named from the N-terminus to the C-terminus; every residue but the last takes a -yl ending (glycylalanine, Gly-Ala).
  • The primary structure is the sequence of amino acids, and it determines every higher level of structure.
  • Changing a single residue can change function, as the Glu → Val substitution in sickle cell hemoglobin shows.
