Learning Objectives

  • Trace fructose, galactose, and mannose into glycolysis and name the intermediate where each one enters.
  • Classify the enzymes of these entry routes (kinase, aldolase, epimerase, isomerase) by enzyme class and reaction type.
  • Explain why the liver’s fructose route bypasses the main control point of glycolysis, and predict what accumulates in hereditary fructose intolerance and galactosemia.
  • Describe the two phases of the pentose phosphate pathway, its products (NADPH and ribose-5-phosphate), and what each product is used for.
  • Relate G6PD deficiency to NADPH, glutathione, and hemolysis.

A glass of milk, a soda, and a slice of bread deliver three different monosaccharides to your liver, yet glycolysis (Section 13.5) is written for only one of them, glucose. This section follows the other sugars to their on-ramps, then turns to a side road that glucose-6-phosphate can take instead of glycolysis: the pentose phosphate pathway, the body’s source of NADPH and ribose. For a health-career student the payoff is practical: newborn screening for galactosemia, the fructose-and-triglycerides connection, and the drug list a patient with G6PD deficiency must avoid all come straight from this chemistry.

Three sugars, one pathway

Digestion (Section 13.3) ends with glucose, fructose, and galactose in the portal blood. Glucose walks straight into glycolysis at step 1. Fructose and galactose cannot: no glycolysis enzyme recognizes them. The solution is a short set of extra reactions that convert each sugar into an intermediate glycolysis already uses. Two facts are worth holding onto. First, the extra steps are of the same kinds you met in Section 13.5: phosphorylation by a kinase, isomerization, and cleavage by an aldolase. Second, where a sugar enters matters, because anything that enters below phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK-1, step 3) has skipped the main checkpoint.

Fructose: the liver’s fast lane

Fructose comes from sucrose (table sugar), from high-fructose corn syrup, and from fruit and honey. Most of it is taken up by the liver, which handles it in three steps:

  1. Fructokinase transfers a phosphate from ATP to carbon 1, making fructose-1-phosphate. (A kinase is a transferase; the reaction type is phosphorylation.) Notice the phosphate lands on C1, not C6, so this is not the fructose-6-phosphate of glycolysis.
  2. Aldolase B, a lyase, splits the six-carbon fructose-1-phosphate into two three-carbon pieces: dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP) and glyceraldehyde. Compare glycolysis step 4, where aldolase splits fructose-1,6-bisphosphate into DHAP and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate. The liver enzyme accepts the mono-phosphate, so one product comes out without a phosphate.
  3. Triose kinase phosphorylates glyceraldehyde with ATP to give glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (GAP). DHAP is converted to a second GAP by triose phosphate isomerase (glycolysis step 5). Both halves now continue through steps 6–10 to pyruvate.

Count the ATP: the liver spends 2 ATP (fructokinase and triose kinase) to make two GAP, exactly what glucose costs in steps 1 and 3, so the net yield is the same 2 ATP and 2 NADH per hexose. The difference is control. Glucose must pass PFK-1, which slows whenever ATP and citrate are high. Fructose enters below PFK-1, so a large fructose load keeps pouring carbon into the bottom of glycolysis no matter how much ATP the liver already has. Acetyl CoA the citric acid cycle cannot use is built into fatty acids (lipogenesis, Chapter 15) and shipped out as triglycerides: the biochemical reason a high-fructose diet raises blood triglycerides and promotes fat storage in the liver.

In muscle and adipose tissue, which lack fructokinase, hexokinase can phosphorylate fructose to fructose-6-phosphate directly, entering at step 3 under normal control; but hexokinase much prefers glucose, so this route carries little traffic. In hereditary fructose intolerance, aldolase B is missing, fructose-1-phosphate accumulates after any fructose or sucrose intake and ties up the liver’s phosphate and ATP, causing hypoglycemia, vomiting, and liver damage; management is dietary, keeping fructose and sucrose out of the diet.

Galactose: three steps to glucose

Galactose, from the lactose in milk, is the C4 epimer of glucose (Section 8.2): the two sugars differ only in which way the C4 hydroxyl points. The liver fixes that one stereocenter and then treats the product as glucose:

  1. Galactokinase (a transferase) phosphorylates galactose at C1 with ATP: galactose-1-phosphate.
  2. Galactose-1-phosphate uridylyltransferase (GALT) swaps the galactose onto a carrier nucleotide. It takes UDP-glucose, hands the UDP to galactose-1-phosphate, and releases glucose-1-phosphate and UDP-galactose. (UDP is uridine diphosphate, a nucleotide relative of ADP; the same UDP-glucose carrier builds glycogen in Section 13.7.)
  3. UDP-galactose 4-epimerase flips the C4 hydroxyl, converting UDP-galactose back into UDP-glucose. An epimerase is an isomerase: same atoms, one stereocenter inverted. The regenerated UDP-glucose goes back to step 2, so the carrier is recycled and the net result is simply galactose + ATP → glucose-1-phosphate + ADP.

Phosphoglucomutase (also an isomerase) moves the phosphate from C1 to C6, and glucose-6-phosphate enters glycolysis at step 2. The same reaction connects glycogen breakdown to glycolysis (Section 13.7), so you will see it again.

Galactosemia. Classic galactosemia is a missing GALT (step 2). A newborn fed milk cannot get past galactose-1-phosphate, which piles up in the liver, kidneys, and brain; excess galactose is also reduced (Section 8.5) to the sugar alcohol galactitol, which collects in the lens and causes cataracts. Within days the infant shows vomiting, poor feeding, jaundice (yellow skin from bilirubin the damaged liver cannot clear), and an enlarged liver, and is unusually prone to E. coli sepsis. Because every one of those symptoms traces back to the one blocked step, management means removing the substrate: a lactose-free, galactose-restricted diet, lifelong.

Mannose and the entry map

Mannose, a minor dietary sugar, is beyond this course; for the record, hexokinase and an isomerase (phosphomannose isomerase) turn it into fructose-6-phosphate, which enters glycolysis at step 3. The figure collects all the entry points.

Professor Tip

For every reaction on this page, set up the four-column table from lecture: What type of reaction is this? How can I tell? What class of enzyme is needed? What coenzyme is needed? Then read the enzyme name before you look at the reaction. -kinase: transferase, ATP in, phosphate on. -isomerase, -mutase, -epimerase: isomerase, same formula in and out. Aldolase, enolase, decarboxylase: lyase, a bond broken or made without hydrolysis and without oxidation (enolase removes a water molecule to make a C=C; it does not use water to split a bond). -dehydrogenase: oxidoreductase, look for NAD+, NADP+, or FAD. Only the GALT step above has an unusual name (a -transferase, which at least says its class out loud).

Example 1: Classify the entry enzymes

Problem. Assign each enzyme to one of the six enzyme classes and name the reaction type: (a) fructokinase, (b) aldolase B, (c) UDP-galactose 4-epimerase, (d) phosphoglucomutase.

Solution. (a) Fructokinase moves a phosphate from ATP to fructose: transferase; phosphorylation. (b) Aldolase B breaks the C3–C4 bond of fructose-1-phosphate without water or a redox change: lyase; cleavage. (c) The epimerase inverts one stereocenter and changes nothing else: isomerase; isomerization (epimerization). (d) Phosphoglucomutase moves a phosphate from C1 to C6 within one molecule: isomerase; isomerization.

Check: Count atoms. In (c) and (d) the product has the same molecular formula as the substrate, the hallmark of an isomerase. In (a) the product gained HPO3 and ATP lost it, the hallmark of a kinase.

Example 2: Trace the carbons

Problem. One fructose molecule is metabolized by the liver all the way to pyruvate. How many ATP are spent, how many are made, how many NADH are made, and how many pyruvate result?

Solution. Spent: 1 ATP at fructokinase and 1 ATP at triose kinase, total 2 ATP. The two glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate molecules then run glycolysis steps 6–10 twice: step 6 makes 2 NADH, steps 7 and 10 make 4 ATP, and the products are 2 pyruvate. Net: 2 ATP, 2 NADH, 2 pyruvate, identical to glucose.

Check: Both routes phosphorylate the hexose twice before splitting it, so the investment is the same; both produce two trioses that take the same payoff steps.

Example 3: Predict what accumulates

Problem. An infant with classic galactosemia is fed standard formula. Name the compound that accumulates directly behind the block, and explain why switching to a soy-based (lactose-free) formula helps even though the enzyme is still missing.

Solution. GALT is missing, so its substrate, galactose-1-phosphate, accumulates (galactokinase still works and keeps making it). Unconverted galactose also backs up and is reduced to galactitol. A soy-based formula supplies carbohydrate as sucrose or glucose polymers rather than lactose, so no galactose reaches the liver, nothing enters the blocked pathway, and the toxic intermediates are not formed. The enzyme defect is unchanged; the substrate is removed.

Check: This is the general strategy for inborn errors of metabolism, the same logic as the low-phenylalanine diet in PKU: you cannot fix the enzyme, so you control what reaches it.

The pentose phosphate pathway: making NADPH and ribose

Glucose-6-phosphate stands at a fork. Most of it continues down glycolysis for energy. A share, large in some tissues, turns instead into the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP; also called the hexose monophosphate shunt), which runs in the cytosol and makes no ATP at all. It exists to make two other things the cell cannot get from glycolysis:

  • NADPH, the reducing coenzyme for anabolism. Building fatty acids and cholesterol (Chapter 15) requires NADPH, which is why the liver, adipose tissue, adrenal cortex, and lactating mammary gland run the pathway hard. NADPH is also the cell’s antioxidant currency: it keeps the tripeptide glutathione in its reduced form so that glutathione can destroy hydrogen peroxide and other reactive oxygen species before they attack membranes, proteins, and hemoglobin.
  • Ribose-5-phosphate, the five-carbon sugar in every nucleotide: ATP, NAD+, coenzyme A, RNA, and (after reduction to deoxyribose) DNA. Any dividing cell needs it.

The pathway has two phases (in lecture, two stages: an oxidative stage of three reactions that takes glucose-6-phosphate to ribulose-5-phosphate and CO2, and a non-oxidative stage in which ribulose-5-phosphate is isomerized to ribose-5-phosphate).

Oxidative phase (irreversible). Two oxidations, each handing electrons to NADP+:

  1. Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD), an oxidoreductase, oxidizes C1 of glucose-6-phosphate (the hemiacetal carbon) and reduces NADP+ to NADPH. After a quick hydrolysis of the resulting lactone, the product is 6-phosphogluconate, the phosphate of the aldonic acid you met in Section 8.5. G6PD is the committed first step and is controlled by its product: when NADPH is plentiful, G6PD slows.
  2. 6-Phosphogluconate dehydrogenase oxidizes the C3 hydroxyl and the molecule loses C1 as CO2 (an oxidative decarboxylation), reducing a second NADP+ to NADPH and leaving the five-carbon ketose ribulose-5-phosphate.

Bookkeeping: per glucose-6-phosphate, the oxidative phase makes 2 NADPH and 1 CO2 and one pentose phosphate.

Non-oxidative phase (reversible). An isomerase converts ribulose-5-phosphate to ribose-5-phosphate for nucleotide synthesis. If the cell needs NADPH but not ribose, two enzymes (transketolase and transaldolase) rearrange the pentose phosphates into fructose-6-phosphate and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, which rejoin glycolysis. You need only the idea: the carbons are recycled, so a cell can keep making NADPH without piling up pentoses.

NADH versus NADPH. The two coenzymes differ by one phosphate far from the reactive nicotinamide ring, so their redox chemistry is identical. The phosphate is a label: catabolic dehydrogenases bind NAD+; anabolic and antioxidant enzymes bind NADPH. Separate pools let the cell keep NAD+ mostly oxidized, ready to accept electrons from fuel, and NADPH mostly reduced, ready to donate them.

Example 4: The first enzyme of the pathway

Problem. (a) Classify glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase by enzyme class and reaction type. (b) A red blood cell converts 5 molecules of glucose-6-phosphate through the oxidative phase. How many NADPH and CO2 result? (c) Predict the consequence if the cell has only 10% of normal G6PD activity and is exposed to an oxidizing drug.

Solution. (a) It removes hydrogen from C1 of the sugar and passes it to NADP+: an oxidoreductase; the reaction type is oxidation (of a hemiacetal to a lactone, then an acid). (b) Each glucose-6-phosphate gives 2 NADPH and 1 CO2, so 10 NADPH and 5 CO2. (c) With little G6PD, the cell makes little NADPH, so it cannot re-reduce glutathione. The oxidizing drug generates hydrogen peroxide faster than the cell can remove it; hemoglobin is oxidized and precipitates (Heinz bodies), the membrane is damaged, and the cell lyses: hemolysis.

Check: A red cell has no mitochondria and no other NADPH source, which is why G6PD deficiency shows up first and worst in red cells even though every tissue carries the mutation.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Galactosemia is on every U.S. state’s newborn screening panel because the first symptoms (vomiting, jaundice, lethargy, poor weight gain) arrive within days of the first milk feeding, and the response is simple: switch to a lactose-free formula and keep galactose out of the diet. G6PD deficiency is X-linked and common in people whose ancestry traces to Africa, the Mediterranean, the Middle East, and South and Southeast Asia. Between episodes the patient is well. Oxidative stress triggers hemolysis: certain drugs (some antimalarials, sulfonamide antibiotics, and others on the patient’s avoid list), fava beans, and infections. Within a day or two the patient may show dark urine, jaundice, pallor, fatigue, and back pain; the lab shows a falling hemoglobin and bite cells or Heinz bodies on the smear. Screening before prescribing a known trigger, and taking a new “dark urine after starting a drug” report seriously, are the nursing links. Fructose and triglycerides: because fructose enters glycolysis below PFK-1, a diet heavy in sugar-sweetened drinks pushes the liver toward lipogenesis; a rising triglyceride on a lipid panel is often the first lab sign.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

A patient on a galactose-free or lactose-free diet is no safer from dental caries: plaque bacteria ferment glucose, fructose, and sucrose just as readily, and the soy formulas and lactose-free products that replace milk often use sucrose or corn-syrup solids as the carbohydrate. The same is true for fructose itself. Agave nectar and “natural” fruit-juice sweeteners are marketed as gentler than sucrose, but fructose is a six-carbon sugar that oral bacteria feed into glycolysis and convert to lactic acid; a soda sweetened with high-fructose corn syrup holds plaque below the critical pH of 5.5 just as long as one sweetened with sucrose. Sucrose does carry one extra penalty, the sticky glucans bacteria build from it, so when a patient asks which sweetener is “safe,” the honest answer is that only the non-sugar sweeteners and the sugar alcohols (xylitol, which plaque bacteria cannot ferment; sorbitol and erythritol, which they ferment slowly if at all) are low-acid choices, and that frequency of exposure matters more than the brand of sugar.

Summary

  • Fructose, galactose, and mannose enter glycolysis by being converted to intermediates glycolysis already uses, with the same reaction types (phosphorylation, isomerization, cleavage) and enzyme classes (transferase, isomerase, lyase) seen in glycolysis itself.
  • Liver fructose: fructokinase → fructose-1-phosphate; aldolase B → DHAP + glyceraldehyde; triose kinase → glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate. Entry below PFK-1 means fructose escapes the main control point and feeds lipogenesis. Muscle uses hexokinase → fructose-6-phosphate. Hereditary fructose intolerance is a missing aldolase B.
  • Galactose: galactokinase → galactose-1-phosphate; GALT exchanges it onto UDP, releasing glucose-1-phosphate; UDP-galactose 4-epimerase recycles the carrier; phosphoglucomutase gives glucose-6-phosphate. Classic galactosemia is a missing GALT; galactose-1-phosphate and galactitol accumulate.
  • Mannose: hexokinase then phosphomannose isomerase → fructose-6-phosphate.
  • The pentose phosphate pathway makes no ATP. Its oxidative phase (G6PD first) produces 2 NADPH + CO2 per glucose-6-phosphate; its non-oxidative phase (isomerase, transketolase, transaldolase) supplies ribose-5-phosphate or returns carbons to glycolysis.
  • NADPH is for biosynthesis (fatty acids, cholesterol) and antioxidant defense (reduced glutathione); ribose-5-phosphate is for nucleotides. G6PD deficiency leaves red cells unable to make NADPH, so oxidative drugs, fava beans, or infection cause hemolysis.

Exercises

Exercise 1

Name the glycolysis intermediate that each sugar becomes on entry, and the step number at which it joins: (a) galactose, (b) mannose, (c) fructose in the liver.

Answer
(a) Glucose-6-phosphate, entering at step 2. (b) Fructose-6-phosphate, entering at step 3. (c) DHAP and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, entering at steps 5–6 (below PFK-1).

Exercise 2

Fructokinase and hexokinase both phosphorylate fructose. State the product of each, the enzyme class, and why the liver route is less regulated.

Answer
Fructokinase makes fructose-1-phosphate; hexokinase makes fructose-6-phosphate. Both are transferases (kinases) using ATP. Fructose-6-phosphate must still pass PFK-1, which ATP and citrate inhibit; fructose-1-phosphate is split by aldolase B into trioses that enter below PFK-1, so no high-energy signal can stop it.

Exercise 3

UDP-galactose 4-epimerase and phosphoglucose isomerase are both isomerases. Describe what each one changes and what stays the same.

Answer
Both keep the molecular formula unchanged. The epimerase inverts the configuration at one stereocenter (C4) of a sugar attached to UDP, converting galactose to glucose. Phosphoglucose isomerase converts an aldose (glucose-6-phosphate) to a ketose (fructose-6-phosphate), moving the carbonyl from C1 to C2.

Exercise 4

Compare and contrast glycolysis and the oxidative phase of the pentose phosphate pathway: starting material, coenzyme reduced, ATP made, CO2 released, and main purpose.

Answer
Both run in the cytosol. Glycolysis starts from glucose; the PPP branches off at glucose-6-phosphate. Glycolysis reduces NAD+ to NADH, makes 2 net ATP, releases no CO2, and exists to make ATP and pyruvate. The oxidative phase of the PPP reduces NADP+ to NADPH (2 per glucose-6-phosphate), makes no ATP, releases 1 CO2, and exists to make NADPH and ribose-5-phosphate.

Exercise 5

A liver cell is actively synthesizing fatty acids and has plenty of nucleotides. Which phase(s) of the pentose phosphate pathway are running, and what happens to the ribulose-5-phosphate?

Answer
The oxidative phase runs hard to make NADPH for fatty acid synthesis. Because ribose is not needed, the non-oxidative phase (transketolase and transaldolase) converts the pentose phosphates back into fructose-6-phosphate and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, which re-enter glycolysis or are rebuilt into glucose-6-phosphate to go around again.

Exercise 6

A 24-year-old with known G6PD deficiency is prescribed a sulfonamide antibiotic and two days later reports dark urine and fatigue. Explain the chain of events from the drug to the symptoms, naming the coenzyme and the tripeptide involved.

Answer
The sulfonamide generates reactive oxygen species (such as hydrogen peroxide) in red cells. Removing them requires reduced glutathione, and re-reducing glutathione requires NADPH from G6PD. With too little G6PD, NADPH runs out, glutathione stays oxidized, hemoglobin and the membrane are oxidized, and the cells lyse (hemolysis). Free hemoglobin and its breakdown products darken the urine, and the falling red-cell count causes fatigue and pallor.

Exercise 7

Classify each enzyme by class: (a) galactokinase, (b) 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase, (c) phosphomannose isomerase, (d) transketolase (a transfer of a two-carbon unit between sugars).

Answer
(a) Transferase (phosphate from ATP). (b) Oxidoreductase (reduces NADP+). (c) Isomerase (aldose to ketose). (d) Transferase (moves a two-carbon group from one sugar phosphate to another).

Exercise 8

A student says, “Galactosemia and lactose intolerance are the same thing: the body can’t handle milk.” Correct the statement using the enzyme and the location of the problem in each case.

Answer
Lactose intolerance is a shortage of lactase, a hydrolase in the intestinal brush border; lactose is not split, so it is fermented by colon bacteria and causes gas and diarrhea, but no toxic metabolite builds up and small amounts are tolerated. Galactosemia is a missing GALT inside liver (and other) cells; lactose is digested normally, but the galactose it releases cannot be converted to glucose, so galactose-1-phosphate and galactitol accumulate and damage the liver, brain, and lens. One is a digestion problem; the other is a metabolism problem, and far more serious.
