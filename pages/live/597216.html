Learning Objectives

  • Describe the urea cycle reactions.

Deamination of amino acids results in the production of ammonia (as well as the ammonium ion, NH4+). Ammonia is an extremely toxic base and its accumulation in the body would quickly be fatal. Animals that live in aquatic environments tend to release ammonia into the water. Terrestrial organisms have evolved other mechanisms to excrete nitrogenous wastes. These animals must detoxify ammonia by converting it into a relatively nontoxic form such as urea or uric acid. Mammals, including humans, produce urea through the urea cycle, whereas reptiles and many terrestrial invertebrates produce uric acid. Animals that excrete urea as the primary nitrogenous waste material are called ureotelic animals.

The Urea Cycle

The urea cycle is the primary mechanism by which mammals convert ammonia to urea. Urea is made in the liver and excreted in urine. The overall chemical reaction by which ammonia is converted to urea is 2 NH3 (ammonia) + CO2 + 3 ATP + H2O → H2N-CO-NH2 (urea) + 2 ADP + 4 Pi + AMP.

The urea cycle utilizes five intermediate steps, catalyzed by five different enzymes, to convert ammonia to urea, as shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\). The amino acid L-ornithine gets converted into different intermediates before being regenerated at the end of the urea cycle. Hence, the urea cycle is also referred to as the ornithine cycle. The enzyme ornithine transcarbamylase catalyzes a key step in the urea cycle and its deficiency can lead to accumulation of toxic levels of ammonia in the body. The first two reactions occur in the mitochondria and the last three reactions occur in the cytosol. Urea concentration in the blood, called blood urea nitrogen or BUN, is used as an indicator of kidney function.

Note Everyday Connection: Gout

Mammals use uric acid as an antioxidant in their cells. However, too much uric acid tends to form kidney stones and may also cause a painful condition called gout, where uric acid crystals accumulate in the joints, as illustrated in Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\). Food choices that reduce the amount of purines (one type of nitrogenous base) in the diet help reduce the risk of gout. For example, people with gout are usually advised to limit purine-rich foods such as red meat, organ meats, and some seafood, as well as alcohol and drinks sweetened with fructose. Tea, coffee, and chocolate contain purine-like compounds called methylxanthines, but the body does not turn these into uric acid, so they are not usually restricted.

Molecule in the Clinic: Sodium phenylbutyrate

Children born with a urea cycle disorder cannot turn ammonia into urea. One treatment, sodium phenylbutyrate, gives the body another way out for nitrogen: it is converted to phenylacetate, which joins with glutamine to form phenylacetylglutamine, and that is excreted in the urine.

Your turn: How many nitrogen atoms leave the body in each phenylacetylglutamine, and how does that compare with urea?

Answer
Two, because glutamine carries two nitrogens (the amino group and the side-chain amide). Urea also carries two.

Your turn: What type of bond joins phenylacetate to glutamine’s amino group?

Answer
An amide bond, like a peptide bond (Chapter 7).

The urea cycle as a four-question table

The cycle runs only in the liver. It takes in two nitrogen atoms, one from NH4+ and one from aspartate, plus one carbon from CO2 (as bicarbonate), and releases urea, (NH2)2C=O, a small, neutral, very water-soluble molecule that the kidneys excrete in urine. Three amino acids serve as intermediates: ornithine and citrulline, nonstandard amino acids not found in proteins, and arginine, a standard one; all three are built on the same five-carbon chain. Ornithine is regenerated each turn, like oxaloacetate in the citric acid cycle. The ammonium ion does not enter the cycle directly: it is first converted to carbamoyl phosphate by carbamoyl phosphate synthetase (a ligase, using 2 ATP).

The four steps up close

Only the end of each molecule that reacts is drawn; R stands for the rest of the amino acid. Highlights show what changes, and curved arrows show what goes in and comes out.

The two cycles share traffic: the fumarate released in step 3 enters the citric acid cycle, becomes oxaloacetate, and by transamination becomes aspartate, which brings the next nitrogen into step 2. The cost of one urea is the equivalent of 3 ATP: two at the carbamoyl phosphate step and one, split all the way to AMP, in step 2.

Example 1: Classify the reactions of the urea cycle

Problem. For the entry step and each of the four urea-cycle steps, name the reaction type and the enzyme class, and say which of the two nitrogen atoms of urea enters at that step, if any.

Solution. Entry: carbamoyl phosphate synthetase joins NH4+ and bicarbonate at the cost of 2 ATP: a ligase; the first nitrogen enters here. (1) Ornithine transcarbamoylase transfers the carbamoyl group onto ornithine to make citrulline: a transferase; no new nitrogen. (2) Argininosuccinate synthetase condenses citrulline with aspartate using ATP: a ligase; the second nitrogen (aspartate’s amino group) enters here. (3) Argininosuccinate lyase cleaves off fumarate without water or oxidation: a lyase. (4) Arginase hydrolyzes arginine into urea and ornithine: a hydrolase.

Check. Three ATP molecules are spent (2 at the entry step, 1 at step 2, the latter split to AMP), the “3 ATP” cost quoted in lecture. Two nitrogens in, one urea out, and ornithine returned, so the cycle balances.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

BUN and ammonia. Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) rises when the kidneys cannot clear urea; read with creatinine, it is the everyday screen for kidney function, and in dehydration it rises before creatinine does because the kidney reabsorbs urea along with water. In liver failure the problem is upstream: the urea cycle fails and NH4+ accumulates (hyperammonemia), causing hepatic encephalopathy, the confusion and flapping tremor of advanced cirrhosis; inherited urea cycle disorders (ornithine transcarbamoylase deficiency is the most common) produce the same chemistry in infants, with vomiting and lethargy soon after protein feeding begins. Both are managed partly by limiting dietary protein, and lactulose, a non-absorbed disaccharide, lowers ammonia by acidifying the colon so that NH3 is trapped as NH4+ and excreted. A patient with a high BUN and a normal ammonia has a kidney problem; a normal BUN with a high ammonia points to the liver.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Urea in the mouth. A urine-like or ammonia odor on the breath (uremic fetor) suggests kidney failure: urea accumulates in saliva, and bacterial urease hydrolyzes it to ammonia and CO2, the same hydrolysis arginase performs in the liver. The ammonia raises plaque pH, which is one reason patients on dialysis often have more calculus but, paradoxically, fewer caries. Liver disease matters for a different reason: the liver makes the clotting proteins and metabolizes amide local anesthetics, so patients with hepatic disease may bleed more after instrumentation and clear lidocaine or articaine more slowly; the dentist may limit the dose.
