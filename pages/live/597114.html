Learning Objectives

  • Compare the boiling points of esters with alcohols of similar molar mass.
  • Compare the solubilities of esters in water with the solubilities of comparable alkanes and alcohols in water.

Ester molecules are polar but have no hydrogen atom attached directly to an oxygen atom. They are therefore incapable of engaging in intermolecular hydrogen bonding with one another and thus have considerably lower boiling points than their isomeric carboxylic acid counterparts. Because ester molecules can engage in hydrogen bonding with water molecules, however, esters of low molar mass are somewhat soluble in water. Borderline solubility occurs in those molecules that have three to five carbon atoms. Table \(\PageIndex{1}\) lists the physical properties of some common esters.

Esters are common solvents. Ethyl acetate is used to extract organic solutes from aqueous solutions—for example, to remove caffeine from coffee. It also is used to remove nail polish and paint. Cellulose nitrate is dissolved in ethyl acetate and butyl acetate to form lacquers. The solvent evaporates as the lacquer “dries,” leaving a thin film on the surface. High boiling esters are used as softeners (plasticizers) for brittle plastics.

Summary

Esters have polar bonds but do not engage in hydrogen bonding and are therefore intermediate in boiling points between the nonpolar alkanes and the alcohols, which engage in hydrogen bonding. Ester molecules can engage in hydrogen bonding with water, so esters of low molar mass are therefore somewhat soluble in water.

Lecture Connection

Esters are the carbonyl family's answer to ethers: polar, able to accept a hydrogen bond from water, but with no O–H of their own. That one fact puts them well below the acids they came from in boiling point and explains why fruit smells like fruit.

Where esters sit in the ranking

Can esters hydrogen bond with other esters? No. Like ethers, aldehydes and ketones, an ester has no H bonded to an O, so esters among themselves have only dipole–dipole and dispersion forces. Esters therefore boil lower than alcohols and carboxylic acids, a little lower than aldehydes and ketones of similar size, and higher than alkanes. Compare ethyl acetate (88 g/mol, 77 °C) with its isomer butanoic acid (163 °C): same atoms, 86 degrees apart, because the acid forms hydrogen-bonded dimers and the ester cannot.

Boiling-point trend for similar molar masses: alkane < ester < aldehyde/ketone < alcohol < carboxylic acid (esters boil a little lower than aldehydes and ketones of similar size).

Solubility. The C=O oxygen of an ester can accept a hydrogen bond from water, so small esters (up to about four or five carbons) dissolve somewhat, much like aldehydes and ketones; larger ones are oily and insoluble. The section text's "borderline at three to five carbons" is the same rule.

Professor Tip

An ester is always compared with the acid it came from on exams, and the answer is always the same: the acid boils higher (O–H and dimers) and dissolves better (donor and acceptor). The ester wins only one contest, volatility, which is why you smell the ester and not the acid.

Why fruit smells like fruit

Many small esters have pleasant fruity smells, and the section's table is worth reading as a list of molecules you will make and identify by nose in lab: ethyl butanoate (pineapple), methyl butanoate (apple), pentyl acetate (pear), isopentyl acetate (banana), pentyl butanoate (apricot), octyl acetate (orange), benzyl acetate (jasmine). Their volatility follows from their weak intermolecular forces; a carboxylic acid of the same size would hydrogen bond to itself and stay in the liquid. Esters are also good solvents (the ethyl acetate in nail-polish remover) for the same reason: polar enough to dissolve polar residues, no O–H to get in the way.

In the Lab

In the carboxylic acids and esters experiment you make esters and identify them by smell: pentyl acetate (pear), octyl acetate (orange), isobutyl formate (raspberry). Each one is a Fischer esterification (Section 6.7), and the smell that reaches you is the ester, not the acid or the alcohol you started with.

Practice 6.6

Four compounds have nearly the same molar mass: pentane (72 g/mol), methyl acetate (74 g/mol), 1-butanol (74 g/mol) and propanoic acid (74 g/mol).

  1. Rank the four from lowest to highest boiling point. Give the reason for each position.
  2. Can two methyl acetate molecules hydrogen bond to each other? Can methyl acetate hydrogen bond to water? Explain.
  3. Which dissolves better in water, methyl acetate or pentane? Why?
Answer
  1. Pentane < methyl acetate < 1-butanol < propanoic acid. Pentane is nonpolar and has only dispersion forces. Methyl acetate is polar, so it adds dipole–dipole forces, but it has no O–H. 1-Butanol has an O–H and hydrogen bonds to itself. Propanoic acid forms hydrogen-bonded dimers, the strongest attraction of the four.
  2. Not to each other: an ester has no H bonded to O, so it has nothing to donate. To water, yes: the O–H of water donates a hydrogen bond to the C=O oxygen of the ester.
  3. Methyl acetate. It can accept hydrogen bonds from water and it has only three carbons, so it is somewhat soluble. Pentane cannot hydrogen bond with water at all and is insoluble.

Check. The measured boiling points agree with the ranking: pentane 36 °C, methyl acetate 57 °C (Table 6.6.1), 1-butanol 117 °C, propanoic acid 141 °C. Methyl acetate and propanoic acid are isomers (C3H6O2), so the 84-degree gap comes from hydrogen bonding alone, not from mass.
