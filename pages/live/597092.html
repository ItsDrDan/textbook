Learning Objectives

  • Explain why the boiling points of alcohols are higher than those of ethers and alkanes of similar molar masses.
  • Explain why alcohols and ethers of four or fewer carbon atoms are soluble in water while comparable alkanes are not soluble.

Alcohols can be considered derivatives of water (H2O; also written as HOH).

Like the H–O–H bond in water, the R–O–H bond is bent, and alcohol molecules are polar. This relationship is particularly apparent in small molecules and reflected in the physical and chemical properties of alcohols with low molar mass. Replacing a hydrogen atom from an alkane with an OH group allows the molecules to associate through hydrogen bonding (Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\)).

Recall that physical properties are determined to a large extent by the type of intermolecular forces. Table \(\PageIndex{1}\) lists the molar masses and the boiling points of some common compounds. The table shows that substances with similar molar masses can have quite different boiling points.

Alkanes are nonpolar and are thus associated only through relatively weak dispersion forces. Alkanes with one to four carbon atoms are gases at room temperature. In contrast, even methanol (with one carbon atom) is a liquid at room temperature. Hydrogen bonding greatly increases the boiling points of alcohols compared to hydrocarbons of comparable molar mass. The boiling point is a rough measure of the amount of energy necessary to separate a liquid molecule from its nearest neighbors. If the molecules interact through hydrogen bonding, a relatively large quantity of energy must be supplied to break those intermolecular attractions. Only then can the molecule escape from the liquid into the gaseous state.

Alcohols can also engage in hydrogen bonding with water molecules (Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\)). Thus, whereas the hydrocarbons are insoluble in water, alcohols with one to three carbon atoms are completely soluble. As the length of the chain increases, however, the solubility of alcohols in water decreases; the molecules become more like hydrocarbons and less like water. The alcohol 1-decanol (CH3CH2CH2CH2CH2CH2CH2CH2CH2CH2OH) is essentially insoluble in water. The borderline of solubility in a family of organic compounds falls at about four or five carbon atoms. In this course, use a simple cut-off: four carbons or fewer with one OH = soluble; more than four = treat as insoluble.

“Soluble” in this rule means that a useful amount dissolves, not that the two liquids mix in every proportion. Alcohols with one to three carbons are miscible with water. 1-Butanol is not: only about 7–8 g dissolves in 100 mL of water, and the rest forms a separate layer. That is still enough to count as soluble on exams.

Summary

Alcohols have higher boiling points than do ethers and alkanes of similar molar masses because the OH group allows alcohol molecules to engage in hydrogen bonding. Alcohols of four or fewer carbon atoms are soluble in water because the alcohol molecules engage in hydrogen bonding with water molecules; comparable alkane molecules cannot engage in hydrogen bonding.

Lecture Connection

The lecture approaches every physical-property question the same way: start with the geometry around the oxygen, decide which intermolecular forces (IMFs) are possible, then rank. The two rules of thumb to memorize are "stronger forces = higher melting and boiling points" and "if a molecule's intermolecular forces are similar to water's, it dissolves in water."

Start with the geometry

The oxygen of an alcohol (or an ether) has four electron groups: two bonds and two lone pairs. Its electron-group geometry is tetrahedral, its molecular geometry is bent, and the C–O–H (or C–O–C) angle is about 109.5°. Two polar bonds in a bent shape do not cancel, so every alcohol, phenol, ether and thiol is a polar molecule. What differs between the families is whether the molecule can hydrogen bond.

Which forces are possible?

  • Alcohols and phenols have an H bonded to O, so they hydrogen bond with each other: the H on one oxygen is attracted to a lone pair on another oxygen. Hydrogen bonding is the strongest intermolecular force in this chapter and requires an H bonded to O, N or F.
  • Ethers and thiols are polar, so they have dipole–dipole forces, but they cannot hydrogen bond with each other: an ether has no H on its O, and the S–H of a thiol is not polar enough. (S–H is barely polar, so thiols are weak even at dipole–dipole; they are known for strong odors, not strong attractions.) Ethers can hydrogen bond to an R–OH or to water, because the ether oxygen's lone pairs accept the H.

Boiling and melting points

Boiling point increases with the size of the alkyl chain (more dispersion forces). For molecules of similar molecular weight, first look at which intermolecular forces are possible, then look at the shape (straight chains pack and attract better than branched ones). Straight-chain alcohols with fewer than about 12 carbons are liquids at room temperature. The cleanest comparison is a set of molecules of nearly the same mass with different functional groups; read the table with the question "can this molecule hydrogen bond with itself?"

Ranking by boiling point at similar mass: alkane < ether < alcohol. The ether sits only slightly above the alkane; the alcohol sits far above both. Ethanethiol, although heavier than ethanol, boils more than 40 °C lower because thiols cannot hydrogen bond (the S–H bond is barely polar). The lecture's own example: pentane (36 °C), 1-methoxypropane (39 °C) and 1-butanol (118 °C) are all about the same size and shape; the ether's dipole lifts it just above the alkane, and only the alcohol's hydrogen bonding makes a large difference.

Solubility in water

Alcohols, phenols and ethers can all hydrogen bond with water. Alcohols and phenols are both hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors; ethers are acceptors only, so an ether is less soluble than an alcohol with a similar alkyl group. How much dissolves depends on the length of the alkyl chain: about 4 carbons or fewer, soluble; longer chains, increasingly insoluble, because the molecule becomes more like a hydrocarbon and less like water. Each extra OH increases solubility (Section 4.6).

Prediction (lecture style). Which is most soluble in water: pentane, ethyl methyl ether, 1-propanol or 1-pentanol? Pentane has no polar group. 1-Pentanol has an OH but a five-carbon chain. The ether and 1-propanol are both small, but 1-propanol can both donate and accept hydrogen bonds with water, so 1-propanol is the most soluble (it is miscible, meaning it mixes with water in any proportion).

In the Lab

In the alcohols and phenols experiment you test solubility directly: alcohols with four or fewer carbons dissolve in water; longer ones form a separate layer. Phenol is only moderately soluble in water (its OH hydrogen bonds, but the ring is nonpolar), yet it dissolves completely in sodium hydroxide solution because it is converted to the ionic phenoxide salt; Sections 4.7 and 4.10 explain why.

Professor Tip

Two different questions, two different tests. Hydrogen bonding with itself controls boiling point. Hydrogen bonding with water controls solubility. That is why diethyl ether boils like pentane but dissolves in water about as well as 1-butanol (Section 4.8).
