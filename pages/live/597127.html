Generally, amides resist hydrolysis in plain water, even after prolonged heating. In the presence of added acid or base, however, hydrolysis proceeds at a moderate rate. In living cells, amide hydrolysis is catalyzed by enzymes. Amide hydrolysis is illustrated in the following example:

Hydrolysis of an amide in acid solution actually gives a carboxylic acid and the salt of ammonia or an amine (the ammonia or amine initially formed is neutralized by the acid). Basic hydrolysis gives a salt of the carboxylic acid and ammonia or an amine.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Write the equation for the hydrolysis of each compound.

  1. butyramide
  2. benzamide

Solution a

The hydrolysis of a simple amide produces an organic acid and ammonia. Butanamide (butyramide) thus yields butanoic acid and ammonia. The conditions decide which form you see: in acid (HCl), the products are butanoic acid + NH4+Cl−; in base (NaOH), they are sodium butanoate + NH3.

Solution b

The hydrolysis of an amide produces an organic acid and ammonia. Benzamide thus yields benzoic acid and ammonia.

Career Spotlight: Athletic Trainer

Athletic trainers prevent, assess and treat sports injuries and help athletes recover. They need to understand how common drugs work, many of which are esters, amines or amides like those in this chapter, and to check supplements so that an athlete does not take a banned substance.

Lecture Connection

In lecture, amide hydrolysis is "cut the C(=O)–N bond, then ask what each piece does under the conditions." The carbonyl side always becomes the acid part and the nitrogen side always becomes the amine part; acid or base decides which one ends up as a salt.

Predicting the products

Amides are the least reactive of the carboxylic acid derivatives. They are stable in water, but with heat and an acid or a base (or a specific enzyme in the body) water splits the C–N bond, the reverse of amidation; amides need more vigorous conditions than esters do. Professor Tip

It works just like esters: the C=O side came from the carboxylic acid and the N side came from the amine. The amide bond is also the peptide bond that links amino acids in every protein (Chapter 10). Amides are much harder to hydrolyze than esters: in the lab you need strong acid or base and prolonged heating, which is why proteins and nylon are so durable, and why your body relies on enzymes (proteases, amidases) to do the job at 37 °C. Practice: which undergoes acid hydrolysis to give acetic acid, CH3CO2CH3, CH3CONH2, or CH3CONHCH3? All three; the CH3CO– side is the acid part in each.

Professor Tip

On exams you will always be told acid or base, and your answer must show the salt that forms under those conditions. Use the IUPAC names (butanamide, butanoic acid).

Amide drugs: the link decides the metabolism

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Amide bonds survive the hour or two a drug spends in stomach acid, which is one reason amide drugs such as acetaminophen can be given by mouth. Amide drugs are then cleared mainly by enzymes in the liver, and not always by cutting the amide bond: acetaminophen is mostly cleared by attaching a glucuronide or sulfate group to its phenol OH, and lidocaine is broken down by liver enzymes. Ester drugs, by contrast, are hydrolyzed quickly by esterases in plasma. Liver disease slows the metabolism of amide drugs, so a patient's liver function matters when these drugs are dosed; that judgment belongs to the prescriber.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Local anesthetics come in two chemical families, and the name tells you which: amide-type drugs have an "i" before the "-caine" (lidocaine, articaine, mepivacaine, prilocaine, bupivacaine); ester-type drugs do not (benzocaine, procaine). Most of them contain a 3° amine (prilocaine and articaine have a 2° amine; benzocaine has only an aromatic 1° amine) joined through a short chain to an aromatic ring by either an amide or an ester link. The link decides the metabolism: esters are hydrolyzed quickly by esterases in plasma and release p-aminobenzoic acid (PABA), a known allergen, which is why ester anesthetics are now mostly limited to topical use; amides are broken down more slowly, mainly by enzymes in the liver, so liver disease matters in the health history.

In the Lab

Amide hydrolysis: heating an amide with NaOH releases ammonia (or an amine). You detect it by its odor and by moist red litmus turning blue, which is the base-hydrolysis row of the table above happening in your test tube: the nitrogen leaves as the free base, not as a salt.
