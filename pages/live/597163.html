Protein misfolding and associated diseases

Newly synthesized proteins fold in a specific way, i.e., into secondary, tertiary, and quaternary structures, to be able to perform their function, as illustrated in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\). Some proteins can fold in only one way, but others can fold in multiple ways. There are proteins in the cell, called chaperones, that help newly formed proteins to fold in the way needed for their function.

Sometimes normal proteins misfold and cause disease. Often a protein that is normally soluble refolds into sticky \(\beta\)-pleated sheets that clump into plaques or amyloid fibers, as illustrated in Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\), with the example of plaque formation in Alzheimer's affected brain.

Prions are misfolded forms of a normal protein found in nerve tissue (the prion protein). A misfolded prion can make normal copies of the same protein misfold too, so the damage spreads. Prions cause mad cow disease in cattle and Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (CJD) in humans.

Other diseases also involve misfolded proteins that clump together, but they are not prion diseases. Examples are Alzheimer's disease (β-amyloid plaques), Parkinson's disease, Huntington's disease, and familial amyloid cardiomyopathy or polyneuropathy.

Denaturation of proteins

Proteins usually keep their primary, secondary, tertiary, and quaternary structures under physiological conditions. However, some physical processes and chemical agents can break the interactions, i.e., hydrogen bonding, disulfide linkages, salt bridges, and hydrophobic interactions.

Denaturation

Loss of the secondary, tertiary, and quaternary structures of proteins by a physical process or a chemical agent while maintaining the primary structure almost intact is called denaturation of proteins.

Proteins unfold and become almost linear polypeptide chains upon denaturation. Denatured proteins cannot perform their functions. Denaturation can be caused by heat, acids or bases, organic compounds and solvents, heavy metal ions, and agitation, as explained below.

  • Heating disrupts hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, causing protein denaturation. Many proteins unfold above about 50 °C, and some human enzymes begin to unfold just above 41 °C, which is why a high fever or heat stroke is dangerous. Examples are cooking food and sterilizing surgical instruments in an autoclave. In laser surgery, a focused beam of light heats a small spot and denatures its proteins; this seals (cauterizes) the cut and helps prevent blood loss.
  • Acids and bases disrupt hydrogen bonding and salt bridges, e.g., by neutralizing some ions involved in the salt bridges.
  • Organic compounds like urea disrupt hydrogen bonding by replacing them with stronger hydrogen bonding with the compound, and organic solvents disrupt hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions. For example, 70% alcohol disinfects skin by denaturing and coagulating the proteins of microbes (it kills most microbes but does not sterilize).
  • Heavy-metal ions like \(\ce{Pb^{2+}}\) and \(\ce{Hg^{2+}}\) bind to –SH groups, breaking disulfide bonds, and to carboxylate groups, disrupting salt bridges (for example –S− Pb2+ −S–). Egg white or milk was once given after heavy-metal poisoning because its proteins bind the metal ions in the stomach. Today the advice for any suspected poisoning is to call Poison Control (1-800-222-1222) or 911; vomiting should not be induced.
  • Agitation physically disrupts hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions. Whipped cream and whipped egg white are prepared based on the agitation process.

Valley Connection: When Summer Heat Unfolds Proteins

Valley summers are long and hot, and many families work outside picking crops, packing fruit, or building roads. A CDC study found that from 1992 to 2006, U.S. crop workers died from heat at a rate nearly 20 times higher than all U.S. civilian workers (0.39 vs. 0.02 deaths per 100,000 workers).1

The chemistry. A protein’s folded shape is held together mostly by weak forces: hydrogen bonds, ionic attractions (salt bridges), and hydrophobic interactions. Heat gives molecules enough energy to break these forces, so the protein unfolds, or denatures, even though its peptide bonds stay intact. A denatured enzyme can no longer do its job. Heat stroke is defined by a core body temperature above 40 °C (104 °F) along with brain dysfunction, and one of the earliest effects inside cells is protein denaturation and clumping (aggregation).2 Cells fight back with heat shock proteins such as HSP70, chaperones that help refold damaged proteins. If heat overwhelms them, misfolded proteins pile up, cell membranes become leaky, and organs such as the liver, kidneys, and brain can fail.2

The law. California’s outdoor heat illness standard (Title 8, §3395) requires employers to provide free, fresh water—one quart per worker per hour for the whole shift—and shade whenever it is hotter than 80 °F. Workers may take a cool-down rest in the shade whenever they feel they need one. At 95 °F or above, employers must start high-heat procedures: keeping workers in contact with a supervisor, watching for signs of heat illness, holding pre-shift meetings, and reminding workers to drink water. Farmworkers also get a 10-minute cool-down rest every two hours. New workers in high heat must be watched closely for their first 14 days, because the body needs about 4 to 14 days to adjust to heat.3

In the field and the ER. EMTs and nurses must spot heat stroke fast: confusion, slurred speech, hot dry skin or heavy sweating, and seizures. Body temperature can reach 106 °F or higher within 10 to 15 minutes. Call 911 and cool the person quickly with a cold-water or ice bath, cold wet cloths, or ice packs on the head, neck, armpits, and groin.4 Every minute counts, because proteins keep denaturing as long as the body stays that hot.

Think about it: When you cook an egg, the clear egg white turns solid white and never goes back. How is this like what happens in cells during heat stroke, and how are living cells different from the egg?

Answer
In both cases heat breaks the hydrogen bonds and other weak interactions holding proteins in their folded shape, so the proteins denature and clump together (aggregate). The egg has no way to repair its proteins, so the change is permanent. Living cells make heat shock proteins (chaperones) that can help refold some damaged proteins, which is why fast cooling can save a heat stroke patient. If the heat lasts too long, the damage overwhelms the chaperones and cells die.

Lecture Connection

Denaturation and hydrolysis are the two ways to ruin a protein, and the exam always asks you to tell them apart: denaturation unfolds (secondary, tertiary, quaternary lost; peptide bonds intact); hydrolysis breaks the peptide bonds (primary structure lost). To predict what an agent does, ask which interactions it touches.

Match the agent to the interaction it breaks

Protein function depends on shape, and shape depends on a web of weak interactions. When the chain unfolds, buried hydrophobic groups come to the surface, and unfolded chains tangle and precipitate: a cooked egg white turns from a clear solution of albumin into a white solid. Denaturation can be reversible for small proteins if the agent is removed gently, but for most proteins (and for anything cooked) it is permanent. The table pairs each denaturing agent with the interactions it attacks; knowing which lets you predict what it will do.

Professor Tip

Match each denaturing agent to the interactions it disrupts: heat, alcohol and agitation break hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions; acids and bases break salt bridges and hydrogen bonds; heavy-metal ions break disulfide bonds and salt bridges; reducing agents break only disulfide bonds. No denaturing agent breaks a peptide bond.

Professor Tip

To predict what a denaturing agent does, ask one question: which interactions does it touch? Then list the levels those interactions hold. Reducing agents touch only disulfide bonds, so they unfold keratin (lots of Cys) but barely affect a protein with no cysteine. Nothing on the denaturation list breaks peptide bonds, so the amino acid sequence is always intact afterward.

Hydrolysis is different

Hydrolysis is the reverse of peptide-bond formation: water is added across the amide link, with strong acid and hours of heating in the lab or with protease enzymes in the body (pepsin in the stomach, trypsin and chymotrypsin from the pancreas). Hydrolysis destroys the primary structure and releases free amino acids, which is exactly what digestion is for: dietary protein is hydrolyzed to amino acids, absorbed, and reassembled into the body's own proteins. It is also why a protein drug taken by mouth stops being a drug.

Misfolding

A protein can also lose function by folding into the wrong stable shape. The section text describes amyloid plaques and prions; the chemistry to remember is that the misfolded form is rich in sticky β-sheet and clumps together. Prions go further: a prion converts normal copies of its protein into the same shape, and prion aggregates survive the heat of ordinary sterilization. That last point is why instruments used on a patient with Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease need special handling: a standard autoclave denatures ordinary proteins but does not reliably destroy prions.

Worked example

Example 1: Predict the effect of a denaturing agent

Problem. A 70% isopropyl alcohol swab is wiped across skin carrying bacteria. (a) Which interactions in the bacterial enzymes does the alcohol disrupt, and which levels of structure are lost? (b) Are the enzymes' amino acid sequences changed? (c) Would the swab have the same effect on a protein with no cysteine as a hair-relaxer reducing agent would?

Solution.

(a) Alcohol is a small polar molecule that hydrogen-bonds and also dissolves nonpolar groups. It disrupts the hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions that hold the fold, so secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure are lost. The enzymes unfold and stop working, and the cell dies.

(b) No. Alcohol does not hydrolyze amide bonds, so the primary structure is intact; the protein is denatured, not digested.

(c) Not at all. A reducing agent such as thioglycolate attacks only disulfide bonds. On a cysteine-free protein it would do almost nothing, whereas alcohol attacks hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions that every protein has.

Check. Match agent to interaction: heat, alcohol and agitation hit hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic packing; acids, bases and heavy metals hit salt bridges (and metals hit disulfides); reducing agents hit disulfides only. Nothing on that list touches peptide bonds.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Denaturation is the chemistry behind infection control. An autoclave (steam at 121 °C) and a 70% alcohol swab kill microbes by unfolding their enzymes, and heavy-metal antiseptics such as silver nitrate do the same by binding sulfur. The same vulnerability is why a high fever is dangerous: human enzymes begin to unfold above about 41 °C, and Chapter 11 shows how temperature and pH change enzyme activity. Insulin is a protein drug: it is injected, not swallowed, because stomach acid denatures it and pepsin then hydrolyzes its peptide bonds. Vials of protein drugs are rolled, never shaken, because agitation at the air–liquid surface denatures them.

Summary

  • Denaturation unfolds the secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure; the primary structure and its peptide bonds remain intact.
  • Heat, alcohol and agitation disrupt hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions; acids and bases disrupt salt bridges and hydrogen bonds; heavy-metal ions disrupt disulfide bonds and salt bridges; reducing agents break disulfide bonds.
  • A denatured protein loses its function; hydrolysis, which does break peptide bonds, is a different process.
  • Misfolded proteins rich in β-sheet (amyloid, prions) clump together and cause disease.
