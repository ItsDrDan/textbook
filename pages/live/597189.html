Learning Objectives

  • To describe the importance of ATP as a source of energy in living organisms.

Adenosine triphosphate (ATP), a nucleotide composed of adenine, ribose, and three phosphate groups, is perhaps the most important of the so-called energy-rich compounds in a cell. Its concentration in the cell varies from 0.5 to 2.5 mg/mL of cell fluid.

Energy-rich compounds are substances having particular structural features that lead to a release of energy after hydrolysis. As a result, these compounds are able to supply energy for biochemical processes that require energy. The structural feature important in ATP is the phosphoric acid anhydride, or pyrophosphate, linkage:

The pyrophosphate bond, symbolized by a squiggle (~), is hydrolyzed when ATP is converted to adenosine diphosphate (ADP). In this hydrolysis reaction, the products contain less energy than the reactants; there is a release of energy (> 7 kcal/mol). One reason for the amount of energy released is that hydrolysis relieves the electron-electron repulsions experienced by the negatively charged phosphate groups when they are bonded to each other (Figure 13.2.1).

Energy is released because the products (ADP and phosphate ion) have less energy than the reactants [ATP and water (H2O)].

The general equation for ATP hydrolysis is as follows:

\[ATP + H_2O → ADP + P_i + 7.3\; kcal/mol \nonumber \]

If the hydrolysis of ATP releases energy, its synthesis (from ADP) requires energy. In the cell, ATP is made by the processes that supply energy, such as the breakdown of food in animals (and the capture of sunlight in green plants). It is hydrolyzed by the processes that need energy: building carbohydrates, lipids and proteins, sending nerve impulses, and contracting muscles. In fact, ATP is the principal medium of energy exchange in biological systems. Many scientists call it the energy currency of cells.

\(P_i\) is the symbol for the inorganic phosphate anions \(H_2PO_4^−\) and \(HPO_4^{2−}\).

ATP is not the only high-energy compound needed for metabolism. Several others are listed in Table \(\PageIndex{1}\). Notice, however, that the energy released when ATP is hydrolyzed is approximately midway between those of the high-energy and the low-energy phosphate compounds. This means that the hydrolysis of ATP can provide energy for the phosphorylation of the compounds below it in the table. For example, the hydrolysis of ATP provides sufficient energy for the phosphorylation of glucose to form glucose 6-phosphate (step 1 of glycolysis). By the same token, the hydrolysis of compounds, such as creatine phosphate, that appear above ATP in the table can provide the energy needed to resynthesize ATP from ADP.

Summary

The hydrolysis of ATP releases energy that can be used for cellular processes that require energy.

Lecture Connection

The lecture wants three things from this section: rank AMP, ADP, and ATP by energy; know which bond is which; and be able to say, for any step, whether ATP → ADP is releasing energy or ADP → ATP is storing it. Coupled reactions are the reason that question comes up in almost every pathway.

Rank them: ATP > ADP > AMP

Adenosine can carry one, two, or three phosphates: adenosine monophosphate (AMP), diphosphate (ADP), and triphosphate (ATP). The bond between ribose and the first phosphate is a phosphoester bond; the bonds between phosphates are phosphoanhydride bonds (the “pyrophosphate” or squiggle bonds of the section above), and these are the high-energy ones. More phosphoanhydride bonds means more stored energy, so ATP > ADP > AMP. Other triphosphates (UTP in glycogen synthesis, GTP in the citric acid cycle and gluconeogenesis, CTP in lipid synthesis) store energy the same way.

  • Hydrolysis releases energy, and that release fuels walking, running, breathing, and every anabolic reaction: ATP + H2O → ADP + Pi + energy.
  • Phosphorylation of ADP absorbs and stores energy: ADP + Pi + energy → ATP + H2O. Catabolism pays for this step.

Professor Tip

Use 7.3 kcal/mol (30.5 kJ/mol) for ATP hydrolysis on exams; tables that print 7.4 or 7.5 are rounding the same value differently. When a problem says “energy is released,” write energy as a product and treat the change as negative; when it says “energy is required,” energy is a reactant and the change is positive.

Coupled reactions: why ATP shows up next to so many steps

Some reactions a cell needs are energetically unfavorable on their own. The fix is to couple the unfavorable reaction to a favorable one that releases more energy than the first one requires. The two run together as a pair, the energy released by one is absorbed by the other, and the overall change is favorable. Step 1 of glycolysis is the classic case: putting a phosphate on glucose needs energy, hydrolyzing ATP releases more than that, and the enzyme hexokinase runs the two as a single reaction. The table of phosphate compounds in the section above is the same idea read vertically: a compound above ATP in the table (1,3-bisphosphoglycerate, creatine phosphate) releases enough energy on hydrolysis to phosphorylate ADP, which is exactly what happens in the ATP-making steps of glycolysis (substrate-level phosphorylation, Section 13.5); a compound below ATP (glucose-6-phosphate, glycerol-3-phosphate) can be made by letting ATP do the phosphorylating.

Example 1: Coupling to ATP

Problem. Attaching a phosphate to glucose using free phosphate has an energy change (ΔG°′) of +3.3 kcal/mol; the plus sign means energy must be put in. Show that hexokinase can make the reaction go by using ATP instead, and state the overall ΔG°′.

Solution. Add the two reactions. Glucose + Pi → glucose-6-phosphate + H2O (+3.3 kcal/mol) and ATP + H2O → ADP + Pi (−7.3 kcal/mol) sum to glucose + ATP → glucose-6-phosphate + ADP, ΔG°′ = +3.3 + (−7.3) = −4.0 kcal/mol. The water and the free phosphate cancel. The negative sign means the coupled reaction is favorable.

Check: The coupled reaction is favorable but by less than 7.3 kcal/mol, because part of ATP’s energy was “spent” pushing the unfavorable half uphill. This is the pattern for every kinase step you will meet.

Example 2: Making ATP from creatine phosphate

Problem. In a sprinting muscle, creatine kinase moves a phosphate from creatine phosphate to ADP. Use the table above to find ΔG°′ for creatine phosphate + ADP → creatine + ATP.

Solution. Add two reactions. Creatine phosphate + H2O → creatine + Pi releases 10.3 kcal/mol (−10.3, from the table). Making ATP is the reverse of ATP hydrolysis, so ADP + Pi → ATP + H2O needs 7.3 kcal/mol (+7.3). Sum: creatine phosphate + ADP → creatine + ATP, ΔG°′ = −10.3 + 7.3 = −3.0 kcal/mol. The negative sign means the reaction is favorable.

Check. This works because creatine phosphate sits above ATP in the table. A compound below ATP cannot make ATP this way: glucose 6-phosphate gives −3.3 + 7.3 = +4.0 kcal/mol, which is unfavorable. (Use 7.3 kcal/mol for ATP, as in Example 1 and the Professor Tip.)

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Creatine phosphate and the cardiac enzymes. The table above lists creatine phosphate just above ATP. Muscle keeps a few seconds’ worth of it as a buffer: when contraction burns ATP faster than metabolism can remake it, the enzyme creatine kinase (CK, a transferase) moves the phosphate from creatine phosphate onto ADP, and ATP is restored in one step. CK lives inside muscle cells, so when cells die it leaks into the blood. The heart-specific form, CK-MB, rises within hours of a myocardial infarction and was for decades the standard marker of heart muscle damage; today troponin (a contractile protein, not an enzyme) is more specific and is drawn first, but CK is still ordered to follow skeletal-muscle breakdown (rhabdomyolysis) after crush injuries, prolonged immobility, extreme exertion, heat stroke (a risk for farmworkers and others who work outdoors in Valley summers), or statin side effects. Either way, the laboratory test is reporting a metabolic enzyme that should have stayed inside the cell.

Practice 13.2

Use 7.3 kcal/mol for the hydrolysis of ATP and the values in Table 13.2.1: hydrolysis of glycerol 3-phosphate releases 2.2 kcal/mol, and hydrolysis of 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate to 3-phosphoglycerate releases 11.8 kcal/mol.

  1. Glycerol + Pi → glycerol 3-phosphate + H2O requires 2.2 kcal/mol. Find the overall energy change when this reaction is coupled to the hydrolysis of ATP. Is the coupled reaction favorable?
  2. Find the energy change for 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate + ADP → 3-phosphoglycerate + ATP. Is it favorable?
  3. Rank AMP, ADP and ATP from least to most stored energy. Which type of bond holds that energy?
Answer
  1. Add the two reactions. Glycerol + Pi → glycerol 3-phosphate + H2O is +2.2 kcal/mol. ATP + H2O → ADP + Pi is −7.3 kcal/mol. Sum: glycerol + ATP → glycerol 3-phosphate + ADP, +2.2 + (−7.3) = −5.1 kcal/mol. The sign is negative, so the coupled reaction is favorable.
  2. Hydrolysis of 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate is −11.8 kcal/mol. Making ATP is the reverse of ATP hydrolysis, so ADP + Pi → ATP + H2O is +7.3 kcal/mol. Sum: −11.8 + 7.3 = −4.5 kcal/mol. Favorable.
  3. AMP < ADP < ATP. The energy is in the phosphoanhydride bonds between phosphates: AMP has none, ADP has one and ATP has two.

Check. The answers agree with the table. Glycerol 3-phosphate sits below ATP, so ATP can phosphorylate glycerol. 1,3-Bisphosphoglycerate sits above ATP, so it can phosphorylate ADP. In both sums the water and the free phosphate cancel, and each answer is smaller in size than the larger number that went in.
