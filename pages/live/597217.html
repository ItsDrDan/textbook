Learning Objectives

  • Tell essential from nonessential amino acids and explain why some amino acids are conditionally essential.
  • Describe how nonessential amino acids are made from metabolic intermediates, mainly by transamination.
  • Explain the link between phenylalanine, tyrosine and phenylketonuria (PKU).

In humans, ten of the standard amino acids (Glu, Gln, Pro, Asp, Asn, Ala, Gly, Ser, Tyr, Cys) can be made in the body; they are the nonessential amino acids. Most of them get their amino group from glutamate. Glutamate itself is made by adding ammonia to α-ketoglutarate (glutamate dehydrogenase running in reverse, with NADPH), and glutamate passes its amino group to other α-keto acids by transamination.

Glutamine is made by adding a second nitrogen, from ammonia, to glutamate; this is also the body’s main way to trap ammonia safely. Proline is made from glutamate in a few steps. Alanine and aspartate are made by transamination of pyruvate and oxaloacetate, and asparagine is made from aspartate.

Serine is made from the glycolysis intermediate 3-phosphoglycerate. Serine then gives glycine (using a coenzyme made from the vitamin folic acid) and the carbon skeleton of cysteine; the sulfur of cysteine comes from the essential amino acid methionine.

Tyrosine is another amino acid that depends on an essential amino acid as a precursor. In this case, the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase oxidizes phenylalanine to tyrosine, using O2 to add an –OH group to the aromatic ring. It is the enzyme missing in phenylketonuria (PKU).

In general, the synthesis of essential amino acids, usually in microorganisms, is much more complex than for the nonessential amino acids and is best left to a full-fledged biochemistry course.

Lecture Connection

The lecture’s version of this section is short. The body makes the nonessential amino acids from glycolysis and citric acid cycle intermediates, adding nitrogen by transamination. It cannot make the essential ones, because it cannot build their carbon skeletons. Those must come from food. Three nonessential amino acids are made in a single transamination step; tyrosine is made from an essential one; the rest take several reactions. That, plus the list of essential amino acids, is what you are responsible for.

Professor Tip

This course counts ten nonessential and ten essential amino acids (arginine counted as essential; see below). The pathway details here (glutamine synthetase, S-adenosylmethionine, cystathionine, tetrahydrofolate) are background.

Essential versus nonessential

The body builds the carbon skeletons of the nonessential amino acids from glycolysis and citric acid cycle intermediates. It then adds nitrogen by transamination (Section 15.8). These routes are shorter than the routes to the essential amino acids. Nine are essential for all adults; the mnemonic PVT TIM HaLL lists them: Phenylalanine, Valine, Threonine, Tryptophan, Isoleucine, Methionine, Histidine, Leucine, Lysine. The lower-case “a” is arginine. The urea cycle makes it (Section 15.9), but not fast enough in infants or during severe illness. So many GOB textbooks and the lecture slides count it as a tenth essential amino acid, which leaves ten nonessential. Tyrosine and cysteine are also conditionally essential, for a different reason. Tyrosine is made from phenylalanine, and cysteine’s sulfur comes from methionine. The body can make them only when it has enough of those two essential amino acids. Three nonessential amino acids (alanine, aspartate, and glutamate) are made in one step: transamination of the matching α-keto acid (pyruvate, oxaloacetate, or α-ketoglutarate). These are the ALT and AST reactions of Section 15.8 running in reverse. The other nonessential amino acids take several more reactions.

Practice: Oxaloacetate accepts an amino group from glutamate. Name the amino acid formed, the reaction type, the enzyme class, and the coenzyme, and state whether the product is essential or nonessential.

Answer
Aspartate. Transamination (amino-group transfer), by an aminotransferase (AST), a transferase, with pyridoxal phosphate (vitamin B6). Nonessential: its carbon skeleton is a citric acid cycle intermediate the body already has.

Health Career Connection

PKU and sugar-free products. People with phenylketonuria (PKU) lack phenylalanine hydroxylase, so they cannot convert phenylalanine to tyrosine. Phenylalanine and its by-products build up and damage the developing brain, which is why every newborn is screened and why the diet restricts phenylalanine while supplying tyrosine, which has become essential for them. The sweetener aspartame is the methyl ester of a dipeptide made of aspartate and phenylalanine. Digestion releases the phenylalanine. That is why sugar-free gums, diet sodas, and some mouth products carry the warning “Phenylketonurics: contains phenylalanine.” For a dental hygienist recommending sugar-free gum to a patient with PKU, xylitol-sweetened products are the alternative (Section 13.5). For a nurse, the lesson is the same one this section teaches: an amino acid is “nonessential” only as long as the enzyme that makes it works.

Summary

  • The body can make the 10 nonessential amino acids from metabolic intermediates, mostly by transamination; the 10 essential amino acids (arginine counted as essential in this course) must come from the diet.
  • Tyrosine is made by oxidizing phenylalanine (phenylalanine hydroxylase); people with phenylketonuria (PKU) lack this enzyme, so tyrosine becomes essential for them.

Practice 15.11

Use the course list of ten essential amino acids (PVT TIM HaLL).

  1. Pyruvate accepts an amino group from glutamate. Name the amino acid formed, the other product, and the type of reaction.
  2. Classify each amino acid as essential or nonessential: leucine, serine, lysine, glutamate, arginine.
  3. Tyrosine is on the nonessential list. Explain why a person with phenylketonuria (PKU) must get tyrosine from the diet.
Answer
  1. Alanine. Glutamate, having given up its amino group, becomes α-ketoglutarate. The reaction is a transamination (the ALT reaction running in reverse).
  2. Essential: leucine, lysine and arginine (this course counts arginine). Nonessential: serine and glutamate.
  3. The body makes tyrosine from the essential amino acid phenylalanine in one step, using the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase. People with PKU lack that enzyme, so they cannot make tyrosine, and it becomes essential for them.

Check. In (a) the amino group only moves: an amino acid and an α-keto acid go in, and a new amino acid and a new α-keto acid come out. In (b), leucine, lysine and arginine are the two Ls and the lower-case a of PVT TIM HaLL; serine and glutamate are not in the mnemonic.
