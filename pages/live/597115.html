Some esters can be prepared by esterification, a reaction in which a carboxylic acid and an alcohol, heated in the presence of a mineral acid catalyst, form an ester and water:

The reaction is reversible. As a specific example of an esterification reaction, butyl acetate can be made from acetic acid and 1-butanol.

A Closer Look: Condensation Polymers

A commercially important esterification reaction is condensation polymerization, in which a reaction occurs between a dicarboxylic acid and a dihydric alcohol (diol), with the elimination of water. Such a reaction yields an ester that contains a free (unreacted) carboxyl group at one end and a free alcohol group at the other end. Further condensation reactions then occur, producing polyester polymers. (Read this as a preview: you will practice spotting the repeating ester link in a polyester alongside the polyamides of Section 7.10.)

The most important polyester, polyethylene terephthalate (PET), is made from terephthalic acid and ethylene glycol monomers:

Polyester molecules make excellent fibers and are used in many fabrics. A knitted polyester tube, which is biologically inert, can be used in surgery to repair or replace diseased sections of blood vessels. PET is used to make bottles for soda pop and other beverages. It is also made into thin, strong films (Mylar) used in food packaging, emergency blankets and balloons. Synthetic arteries can be made from PET, polytetrafluoroethylene, and other polymers.

Esters are made by the reaction of a carboxylic acid with an alcohol, a process that is called esterification.

Lecture Connection

Esterification is the first condensation reaction you write in full in this course (acetal formation in Section 5.3 was a preview): two molecules join and a small molecule (here water) is split out. The same pattern builds amides, glycosidic bonds and peptide bonds later, so learn the bookkeeping now: the water comes from the acid's –OH and the alcohol's H, and the acid catalyst sits over the arrow.

Fischer esterification: a condensation at equilibrium

Heating a carboxylic acid with an alcohol and a trace of strong acid (usually H2SO4) joins them into an ester and releases a molecule of water; this acid-catalyzed reaction is called Fischer esterification: RCOOH + R′OH (H+, heat) \(\rightleftharpoons\) RCOOR′ + H2O. The –OR′ group replaces the –OH of the acid. A condensation reaction is one in which two molecules combine with the loss of a small molecule (water, HCl, methanol and so on); a dehydration is the subset of condensations in which the small molecule lost is water. The H+ is a catalyst: it speeds the reaction up but is not used up, so it appears above the arrow rather than as a reactant. The water comes from the –OH of the acid plus the H of the alcohol's –OH; the alcohol's oxygen ends up in the ester.

The scheme shows the forward direction, but esterification is reversible, as described above, and the mixture at equilibrium typically contains large amounts of unreacted acid and alcohol. To push the equilibrium toward the ester, chemists use Le Chatelier's principle in two ways: add a large excess of one reactant (usually the cheaper alcohol), or remove the water as it forms. Either change shifts the position of equilibrium to the right. In the carboxylic acids and esters experiment you will use excess alcohol and concentrated sulfuric acid, which also absorbs water. The reverse direction, adding water with an acid catalyst, is exactly the acid-catalyzed hydrolysis of Section 6.8.

Professor Tip

To draw an ester from its acid and alcohol, write the acid, erase its –OH, write the alcohol, erase the H of its –OH, and join the two open ends: acid C(=O)–O–R′. The two pieces you erased are the water. Count carbons on each side of the C=O when you are done and you can name the ester at once (Section 6.5).

Cyclic esters and thioesters

A molecule that contains both a –COOH and an –OH (a hydroxy acid) can esterify with itself, in the same way a hydroxy aldehyde closes to a cyclic hemiacetal (Chapter 5). Internal esterification happens when a five- or six-membered ring can form, which means the OH must be on the γ (C4) or δ (C5) carbon. The cyclic ester is called a lactone; 4-hydroxybutanoic acid, for example, closes to a five-membered lactone and water. A thiol can take the place of the alcohol in a condensation with a carboxylic acid; the product, R–CO–S–R′, is a thioester, and acetyl-CoA, the central molecule of metabolism (Chapter 13), is one.

Making aspirin: an esterification you will run

In the Lab

Aspirin and other analgesics: you make aspirin by esterifying the phenol –OH of salicylic acid with acetic anhydride (a more reactive relative of acetic acid; the by-product is acetic acid instead of water). Unreacted salicylic acid still has a phenol, so a purple color with FeCl3 (Chapter 4) means the product is impure. You will also separate aspirin, acetaminophen, ibuprofen, naproxen and caffeine in pain relievers by thin-layer chromatography (TLC).
