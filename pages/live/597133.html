Problem 7.4

Ethylamine + HCl gives

  1. ethylammonium chloride
  2. ethanamide
  3. chloroethane + NH3
  4. no reaction
Answer
A. The amine is a base, so its nitrogen lone pair picks up H+ from HCl, forming the salt CH3CH2NH3+ Cl−, ethylammonium chloride.

Problem 7.6

What is the name of CH3CH2CONHCH3?

  1. N-methylpropanamide
  2. N-ethylethanamide
  3. methylpropylamine
  4. propyl methanamide
Answer
A. The C=O side is a three-carbon chain (propanamide), and the CH3 is on the nitrogen, shown by N-methyl. It has a C=O, so it is an amide, not an amine.

Problem 7.7

Pyridine, an aromatic ring with one N, is an example of a(n)

  1. amide
  2. heterocyclic amine
  3. quaternary ammonium salt
  4. nitrile
Answer
B. A heterocycle has a ring atom other than carbon; pyridine’s ring nitrogen has a lone pair that makes it a basic amine. It has no C=O, so it is not an amide.

True or False? (If false, correct it.)

Problem 7.8

Amides are basic and react with HCl to form ammonium salts, just as amines do.

Answer
False. Amides are essentially neutral; the C=O pulls on the nitrogen’s lone pair.

Problem 7.9

Base hydrolysis of an amide gives a carboxylate salt and an amine (or ammonia).

Answer
True. OH− breaks the amide C–N bond; the acid part ends up as its carboxylate salt (because the solution is basic) and the nitrogen part is released as the amine or ammonia.

(1) quaternary ammonium ion; (2) primary amine; (3) tertiary amine; (4) amide

Problem 7.50

A sample of 2-butanamine is shaken with 1 M HCl, and then an excess of NaOH is added. After both steps, the nitrogen compound present is

  1. 2-butanammonium chloride
  2. butanamide
  3. 2-butanamine (the free amine)
  4. sodium butanoate
Answer
C. HCl converts the amine to its ammonium chloride salt; NaOH then removes the H+ from the ammonium ion and regenerates the free amine (plus NaCl and water). No C=O is ever formed, so no amide or carboxylate appears.

Problem 7.51

Compare and contrast: the isomers CH3CH2CH2NH2 (1-propanamine) and (CH3)3N (trimethylamine) both have molar mass 59 g/mol. (a) Which has the higher boiling point, and why? (b) Would you expect a large difference in their water solubility? (c) Which one can be converted into an amide?

Answer
(a) 1-Propanamine (48 °C vs about 3 °C). It has N–H bonds and hydrogen bonds with itself; trimethylamine has no N–H, so only dipole–dipole and dispersion forces hold its molecules together. (b) No. Both are small and both accept hydrogen bonds from water through the lone pair, so both are very water soluble; the missing N–H lowers the boiling point far more than it lowers solubility. (c) Only 1-propanamine. Amide formation requires an N–H to lose as water; trimethylamine has none.

Problem 7.52

Predict: N-methylbutanamide is heated separately with (a) aqueous HCl and (b) aqueous NaOH. Give the products of each and explain why the amine product looks different in the two cases, even though the same C–N bond is broken.

Answer
Cut at the C(=O)–N bond: the carbonyl side is butanoic acid, the nitrogen side is methylamine. (a) In acid: butanoic acid + methylammonium chloride, CH3NH3+Cl−. (b) In base: sodium butanoate, CH3CH2CH2COO−Na+, + methylamine, CH3NH2. The amine is a base, so in excess HCl it is protonated to the ammonium salt; in NaOH there is no acid to protonate it, so it stays as the free amine (and the carboxylic acid is the one that is neutralized instead).

Problem 7.53

Analyze the data. Four compounds of similar molar mass are listed with their boiling points.

Boiling points of four compounds of similar molar mass (for the questions below)
CompoundFormulaMolar mass (g/mol)Boiling point (°C)
WCH3CH2CH2CH2NH27378
XCH3CH2N(CH3)27337
YCH3CONHCH373206
ZCH3CH2CH2CH2CH37236

(a) Classify W, X, and Y (amine or amide; 1°, 2°, 3°). (b) Explain why X boils almost exactly where the hydrocarbon Z does, while W boils 40 °C higher. (c) Y has only one N–H, the same as a 2° amine, yet it boils more than 100 °C above W. What is different about the N–H in an amide?

Answer
(a) W: 1° amine; X: 3° amine; Y: 2° amide. (b) X has no N–H, so it cannot hydrogen bond with itself; its weak dipole–dipole attraction adds almost nothing to the dispersion forces that pentane also has. W has N–H hydrogen bonds, which raise the boiling point. (c) In the amide the N–H sits next to a C=O. The carbonyl pulls electron density away from the nitrogen, making the N–H much more polar (a better hydrogen bond donor), and the C=O oxygen is a strong hydrogen bond acceptor. The result is a network of strong hydrogen bonds, which is why amides have the highest boiling and melting points in this course.

More Practice: Clinical and Integrative

Problem 7.55

Nursing. A vial is labeled "dopamine hydrochloride, 40 mg/mL, for IV infusion after dilution." Dopamine's structure is a catechol ring (benzene with two adjacent OH groups) attached to –CH2CH2NH2. (a) Identify every functional group and classify the amine. (b) Write the structure of the ammonium ion in the vial. (c) Why is the hydrochloride salt used instead of the free amine?

Answer
(a) Two phenol groups (OH on an aromatic ring) and one 1° amine (one carbon on N). (b) The amine nitrogen is protonated: catechol–CH2CH2NH3+ with Cl− as the counter-ion. (c) The ionic ammonium chloride dissolves readily in water (and in D5W or saline for infusion), is a stable, odorless solid that can be weighed accurately, and does not evaporate or oxidize as readily as the free amine.

Problem 7.56

Dental hygiene. A patient with a periapical abscess (infection at the root tip; the surrounding tissue is inflamed and its pH is lower than normal) reports that the injection "did not work." The anesthetic in the cartridge is a 3° amine supplied as its hydrochloride salt. (a) Write the equilibrium between the ammonium form and the free amine. (b) In which direction does the lower pH of infected tissue shift that equilibrium? (c) Explain, in terms of polarity and membranes, why the shift reduces the anesthetic effect.

Answer
(a) R3NH+ \(\rightleftharpoons\) R3N + H+. (b) Extra H+ (lower pH) pushes the equilibrium to the left, toward the charged ammonium ion (Le Chatelier's principle). (c) Only the neutral, less polar free amine can cross the nonpolar nerve-cell membrane to reach its site of action inside. With more of the drug trapped as the polar ammonium ion outside the nerve, less reaches the target, so the onset is slower and the block is weaker.

Problem 7.57

Nursing. Acetaminophen has the structure HO–C6H4–NH–C(=O)CH3. (a) Name the two functional groups. (b) Classify the amide as 1°, 2°, or 3°. (c) Give the products if the amide bond were hydrolyzed (treat the reaction as simple hydrolysis with water). (d) Would you expect acetaminophen to react with stomach acid to form an ammonium salt? Explain.

Answer
(a) A phenol (OH on the ring) and an amide (N bonded to C=O). (b) 2° amide: the nitrogen carries one carbon group (the ring) besides the carbonyl carbon. (c) 4-Aminophenol (HO–C6H4–NH2) and acetic acid (present as acetate at body pH). (d) No. The amide nitrogen is not basic because the C=O draws its lone pair away, so amides do not form salts with acids. Acetaminophen passes through the stomach unchanged.

Problem 7.58

Integrative (Chapters 6 and 7). Aspirin is an ester of salicylic acid; acetaminophen is an amide of 4-aminophenol. Both are made from the same acetylating reagent. (a) For each drug, name the two molecules that would be obtained by complete hydrolysis in aqueous NaOH, in the form they exist under basic conditions. (b) Which of the two drugs hydrolyzes more easily in the bottle (that is, which is less stable toward moisture), and why?

Answer
(a) Aspirin (ester): sodium salicylate + sodium acetate. Acetaminophen (amide): 4-aminophenol (its phenol OH is also deprotonated by excess NaOH) + sodium acetate. In both cases the acetyl group leaves as the acetate salt. (b) Aspirin. Esters hydrolyze much more readily than amides, which is why old aspirin tablets smell of vinegar (acetic acid) while acetaminophen tablets do not change.

Problem 7.59

Integrative (Chapters 7 and 10). The dipeptide glycylalanine has the structure H2N–CH2–C(=O)–NH–CH(CH3)–COOH. (a) Circle (describe) the amide bond and classify it as 1°, 2°, or 3°. (b) Give the products of complete hydrolysis in aqueous HCl, in the form they exist in acid. (c) Nylon-6,6 contains the same kind of bond. State one way the nylon chain differs from a peptide chain.

Answer
(a) The C(=O)–NH between the two amino acid units; it is a 2° amide (one carbon group on N besides the carbonyl). (b) Glycine and alanine, each with its amine protonated: +H3N–CH2–COOH and +H3N–CH(CH3)–COOH (as chloride salts). (c) Any one: in a peptide each monomer (an amino acid) carries both the acid and the amine, so the amide repeats every three chain atoms and every unit can carry a different side chain; nylon-6,6 is built from alternating diacid and diamine units, so its amide links are separated by runs of CH2 groups (six from the diamine, four from the diacid) and all repeat units are identical. Peptides also have a defined sequence and are hydrolyzed by enzymes.

Problem 7.60

Nursing. A patient's blood urea nitrogen (BUN) is elevated. Urea has the structure H2N–C(=O)–NH2. (a) What functional group class is urea, and how many amide nitrogens does it have? (b) Write the products of complete hydrolysis of urea with water (the reaction catalyzed by the bacterial enzyme urease). (c) Which organ makes urea, and what simpler nitrogen compound does it package? (d) An elevated BUN with normal liver function most likely points to a problem in which organ?

Answer
(a) An amide; specifically a diamide, with two NH2 groups on one carbonyl. (b) H2NCONH2 + H2O → CO2 + 2 NH3 (the carbonyl carbon ends up as carbonic acid/CO2 and each nitrogen as ammonia; at body pH the ammonia is mostly NH4+). (c) The liver, which converts toxic ammonia from amino acid breakdown into urea (Chapter 15). (d) The kidneys, which normally filter urea out of the blood into the urine; when they fail, urea accumulates.

Problem 7.61

Which compound is NOT an amide?

  1. CH3CH2CH2CONH2
  2. CH3CH2NHCOCH3
  3. CH3CH2NHCH2COCH3
  4. CH3CON(CH3)CH2CH3
Answer
C. An amide needs the C=O bonded directly to the N. In C the nitrogen is bonded to a CH2, and the C=O one carbon away is a ketone, so C is an amine plus a ketone. B is an amide written from the nitrogen end (N–CO), the same as CH3CONHCH2CH3, N-ethylethanamide.

Problem 7.62

Which compound is a skeletal (branched-chain) isomer of 1-pentanamine?

  1. 2-pentanamine
  2. 3-methyl-1-butanamine
  3. 1-hexanamine
  4. N-methyl-1-butanamine
Answer
B. Same formula (C5H13N) but a branched carbon skeleton. A is a positional isomer (same chain, NH2 moved to C2). C has six carbons, so it is not an isomer at all. D is also C5H13N but the extra carbon sits on nitrogen, making it a 2° amine; it is a constitutional isomer, but not a skeletal isomer of the carbon chain.

Draw (as a condensed structure) N,2-dimethylpropanamide and classify the amide.

Answer
(CH3)2CHCONHCH3. The parent is propanamide (three carbons, C1 = the C=O); one methyl is on C2 and one on nitrogen. One carbon group on N besides the carbonyl makes it a 2° amide.

Problem 7.67

Name (CH3)2CHCH2CON(CH3)2.

Answer
N,N,3-Trimethylbutanamide. The acyl chain is four carbons (butanamide) with a methyl on C3; the two methyls on nitrogen are N,N. All three methyls are collected into one "trimethyl" with the locants N,N,3. It is a 3° amide.

Problem 7.68

List the intermolecular forces (a) between two molecules of N,N-dimethylethanamide, CH3CON(CH3)2, and (b) between N,N-dimethylethanamide and water. Then state whether it should be water soluble.

Answer
(a) Dispersion forces and dipole–dipole forces (the C=O and C–N bonds are polar), but no hydrogen bonding: a 3° amide has no N–H. (b) Dispersion, dipole–dipole, and hydrogen bonding: water's O–H can donate a hydrogen bond to the carbonyl oxygen (and to the N), even though the amide cannot donate one back. Because it can accept hydrogen bonds and is small, it is water soluble, though less so than a 1° or 2° amide of the same size.

Problem 7.69

Rank from lowest to highest boiling point and explain: 1-butanamine (CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2, 73 g/mol), 1-butanol (74 g/mol), diethyl ether (CH3CH2OCH2CH3, 74 g/mol).

Answer
Diethyl ether < 1-butanamine < 1-butanol. All three are about the same size, so compare hydrogen bonding. The ether has no O–H or N–H and cannot hydrogen bond with itself (dipole–dipole only). The 1° amine hydrogen bonds through N–H, but N–H hydrogen bonds are weaker than O–H because N is less electronegative than O, so the alcohol is highest (about 35, 78, and 118 °C).

Problem 7.70

Ethyl acetate (an ester, CH3COOCH2CH3, 88 g/mol) boils at 77 °C. Predict whether propanamide (CH3CH2CONH2, 73 g/mol) boils higher or lower, and explain.

Answer
Higher (it is a solid that boils above 200 °C) even though it is the smaller molecule. The 1° amide has two N–H bonds next to a C=O and hydrogen bonds strongly with itself; the ester has a polar C=O but no O–H or N–H, so it cannot hydrogen bond with itself.

Problem 7.71

Predict the product when butanoic acid is heated with trimethylamine, (CH3)3N, and name it.

Answer
No amide forms: a 3° amine has no N–H to lose as water. The only reaction is neutralization, giving the ammonium salt trimethylammonium butanoate, CH3CH2CH2COO− (CH3)3NH+.

Problem 7.72

Which of the following gives butanoic acid as a product of acid hydrolysis (HCl, H2O, heat)?

  1. CH3CH2CH2CONH2
  2. CH3CH2CH2COOCH3
  3. CH3CH2CONHCH2CH2CH3
  4. Both A and B
Answer
D. Cut each molecule at the bond between the C=O carbon and the N or O. In A (an amide) and B (an ester) the carbonyl side has four carbons, so both release butanoic acid (A also gives NH4+Cl−; B gives methanol). In C the carbonyl side has only three carbons: it gives propanoic acid plus propylammonium chloride. The four-carbon piece in C is on the nitrogen side, so it becomes an amine, not an acid.

Problem 7.73

Fill in the blanks. A neurotransmitter is stored in ______ at the end of the ______ neuron, is released into the ______ when a nerve impulse arrives, and is received by ______ on the ______ neuron.

Answer
vesicles; presynaptic; synapse; receptors (receptor proteins); postsynaptic. After the signal, the neurotransmitter is removed by reuptake into the presynaptic neuron or by an enzyme in the synapse.

Problem 7.74

Albuterol, the drug in a rescue inhaler, contains a nitrogen bonded to one H, a CH2 of the side chain, and a tert-butyl group, C(CH3)3. (a) Classify the amine. (b) The inhaler solution contains albuterol sulfate rather than albuterol. Which of the two is more water soluble, and why? (c) Write the general equation for forming the salt, using R2NH for the drug and H2SO4 as the acid (one H+ per amine).

Answer
(a) Two carbons on N: a 2° amine. (b) Albuterol sulfate. The salt is ionic (an ammonium ion with a sulfate counter-ion), so it dissolves in water far better than the neutral amine, which also lets it be made into a stable, accurately measured solution. (c) 2 R2NH + H2SO4 → (R2NH2+)2 SO42−; the nitrogen gains an H and a positive charge, and no water forms.

Problem 7.75

True or false? (If false, correct it.) When ethylamine reacts with hydrochloric acid, the products are ethylammonium chloride and water.

Answer
False. The only product is ethylammonium chloride, CH3CH2NH3+Cl−. The H+ from HCl bonds to the nitrogen's lone pair; there is no OH− to make water, unlike the neutralization of NaOH by HCl.

Problem 7.76

For the nitrogen atom in methylamine, CH3NH2: (a) How many electron groups surround N? (b) What are the electron-group geometry and the molecular geometry? (c) What is the approximate H–N–C bond angle? (d) Is the molecule polar?

Answer
(a) Four: three bonds (two N–H and one N–C) and one lone pair. (b) Tetrahedral electron-group geometry; trigonal pyramidal molecular geometry (the lone pair occupies one corner). (c) About 107°, a little less than the ideal 109.5° because the lone pair pushes the bonds together. (d) Yes. Problem 7.77

A student writes: “Heating trimethylamine, (CH3)3N, with acetic acid gives an amide and water.” Find the error and correct it.

Cumulative Problem 7.1 (Chapters 1, 4 and 7)

Propylamine (CH3CH2CH2NH2), 1-propanol and butane have masses of 59, 60 and 58 g/mol. Rank them by boiling point.

Cumulative Problem 7.2 (Chapters 6 and 7)

Methylamine is bubbled into hydrochloric acid. (a) Write the reaction and name the product. (b) Compare it with the reaction of acetic acid with sodium hydroxide: in each case, what is the acid, what is the base, and what kind of product forms? (c) Why are so many drugs sold as hydrochloride salts?

Answer
(a) CH3NH2 + HCl → CH3NH3+ Cl−, methylammonium chloride. The amine’s lone pair accepts the proton. (b) Here the amine is the base and HCl the acid; in Chapter 6 acetic acid was the acid and NaOH the base. Cumulative Problem 7.3 (Chapters 6 and 7)

Acetic acid and methylamine are heated together. (a) Draw and name the product. (b) Compare this reaction with esterification. (c) Write the products of hydrolyzing your product in aqueous acid.

Answer
(a) CH3CONHCH3, N-methylacetamide (N-methylethanamide). (b) Both are condensations that lose water: with an alcohol the acid gives an ester (C–O–C=O link); with an amine it gives an amide (C–N–C=O link). (c) Acid hydrolysis breaks the amide bond: acetic acid + methylammonium ion (the amine is protonated by the acid). In base you would get acetate ion + free methylamine. The amide bond is the peptide bond of proteins (Chapter 10), and this hydrolysis is what your digestive enzymes do to dietary protein.

Cumulative Problem 7.4 (Chapters 2, 4 and 7)

tert-Butyl alcohol, (CH3)3COH, is a tertiary alcohol. Is tert-butylamine, (CH3)3CNH2, a tertiary amine? Explain the difference in how alcohols and amines are classified, and give an example of a true tertiary amine.

Answer
No, it is a primary amine. Alcohols are classified by the number of carbons attached to the carbon that holds the OH (three here, so 3°). Amines are classified by the number of carbons attached to the nitrogen; tert-butylamine has only one carbon on N, so it is 1°. A true tertiary amine has three carbons on the nitrogen, for example trimethylamine, (CH3)3N, the compound responsible for the smell of fish, or triethylamine.

Cumulative Problem 7.5 (Chapters 3, 6 and 7)

para-Aminobenzoic acid (PABA) has a benzene ring with an –NH2 group and a –COOH group on opposite carbons. It was the original sunscreen and is a building block of folic acid in bacteria. (a) Identify every functional group. (b) Which group is acidic and which is basic? (c) In what form is each group at pH 7.4?

Answer
(a) An aromatic ring, an aromatic amine (aniline-type, –NH2 on the ring) and a carboxylic acid. (b) The –COOH is the acid; the –NH2 is the base. (c) At pH 7.4 the carboxylic acid is ionized to –COO−. The amine stays mostly neutral (–NH2): an amine attached directly to a benzene ring is a much weaker base than an alkylamine because its lone pair is shared with the ring, so it is only protonated in strongly acidic solution. Sulfa antibiotics work because they look like PABA and fool the bacterial enzyme that uses it (Chapter 11).

Cumulative Problem 7.6 (Chapters 4, 6 and 7)

In the dental office. Lidocaine has a benzene ring, an amide group and a tertiary amine; it is supplied as lidocaine hydrochloride. Acetaminophen has an amide and a phenol. (a) Why is lidocaine supplied as the hydrochloride salt? (b) Lidocaine must be in its neutral amine form to cross the nerve membrane. Why does it work poorly in an infected, inflamed tooth, where the tissue pH is low? (c) Which of the two drugs can be broken down by hydrolysis, and what bond is broken?

Answer
(a) The salt is water soluble, so it can be dissolved in the injection solution and stored stably. (b) In tissue at normal pH some of the drug converts back to the neutral amine, which is nonpolar enough to pass through the nerve membrane. In acidic (infected) tissue the equilibrium is pushed toward the protonated ammonium form, which is charged and cannot cross the membrane, so the anesthetic is less effective. (c) Both contain an amide bond, which can in principle be hydrolyzed to a carboxylic acid and an amine. (In the body, lidocaine is broken down in the liver; acetaminophen is mostly cleared by attaching glucuronic acid or sulfate to its phenol, Section 8.5.) Acetaminophen’s phenol group is also what the liver oxidizes, and the product of that oxidation is what damages the liver in an overdose.
