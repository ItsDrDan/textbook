Learning Objectives

  • Explain why a cell needs both fast (seconds) and slow (hours) ways to control its enzymes.
  • Describe covalent modification by phosphorylation: what a protein kinase and a phosphatase each do, which amino acids are modified, and what kind of bond is formed.
  • Trace the hormone-to-enzyme path by which glucagon and epinephrine turn glycogen breakdown on and glycogen synthesis off, and how insulin reverses it.
  • Distinguish enzyme induction from repression and explain, at the concept level, why inducing a liver enzyme changes how fast a drug is cleared from the blood.
  • Compare allosteric control, feedback inhibition, zymogen activation, covalent modification, and genetic control by speed, reversibility, and example.

A patient with diabetes injects insulin before a meal; a patient who drinks alcohol every day clears some sedatives faster than expected; the pancreas ships its protein-digesting enzymes out in a safe, inactive form. Each of these comes down to a question this section answers: how does a cell change how much an enzyme does, and how fast can it make the change? Section 11.6 covered the quickest switches, allosteric regulators and feedback inhibition, which work in seconds because a small molecule simply binds and leaves. Here you meet two more: covalent modification, a chemical change to the enzyme itself, and genetic control, a change in how much enzyme the cell makes.

Fast switches and slow switches

Think of the two controls on a gas stove. The knob changes the flame in a second; that is allosteric control. Replacing the burner with a bigger one takes a trip to the store; that is genetic control. A cell needs both. Between meals your blood glucose must be held near 4–6 mmol/L (about 70–110 mg/dL) minute by minute, so the enzymes that release glucose from liver glycogen are switched by hormones within seconds to minutes. But if you start a new medication or begin drinking alcohol daily, the body answers over days by making more or less of particular enzymes. The table at the end of this section puts all five regulation strategies side by side; keep it in mind as you read.

Covalent modification: phosphorylation and dephosphorylation

In covalent modification, an enzyme is switched between an active and an inactive shape by forming or breaking a covalent bond to a small group. By far the most common group is a phosphate. Three amino acids have a side-chain –OH that can carry it: serine, threonine, and tyrosine.

  • A protein kinase transfers the terminal phosphate of ATP onto the –OH, leaving ADP. Kinases are transferases. The new bond is a phosphate ester, the same kind of link you met in Chapter 6 when an alcohol condenses with an acid; here the acid is phosphoric acid. At cell pH the attached phosphate carries two negative charges.
  • A protein phosphatase removes the phosphate by hydrolysis, releasing inorganic phosphate (HPO42−, written Pi). Phosphatases are hydrolases. The enzyme returns to its original form.

Why does one phosphate matter so much? Its 2− charge attracts nearby –NH3+ groups (lysine, arginine) and repels –COO− groups, so the chain folds slightly differently, and the active site opens or closes. For some enzymes the phosphorylated form is the active one; for others it is the inactive one. There is no rule; you learn each case. The switch is fast (seconds to minutes), it is reversible, and because the kinase and the phosphatase are themselves controlled by hormones, it lets a signal from outside the cell change what happens inside.

Professor Tip

Students mix up the two enzymes constantly. Fix it with the names: a kinase puts a phosphate on (from ATP) and is a transferase; a phosphatase is named like any hydrolase (lipase, protease) and takes the phosphate off with water. Then remember that "on" does not mean "active": glycogen phosphorylase is active with its phosphate, glycogen synthase is active without it.

The glycogen switch

The classic example, which you will meet again in Chapter 13, is the control of glycogen in liver and muscle. Two enzymes do opposite jobs: glycogen phosphorylase breaks glycogen down to glucose-1-phosphate, and glycogen synthase builds glycogen from glucose. Running both at once would waste ATP, so the cell phosphorylates both with the same signal and gets opposite results.

  1. Blood glucose falls (between meals), or you are frightened or exercising. The pancreas releases glucagon or the adrenal glands release epinephrine.
  2. The hormone binds a receptor on the outside of the cell (glucagon acts on liver; epinephrine on liver and muscle). It never enters. The receptor passes the signal inside by raising the level of a small messenger molecule, cyclic AMP (cAMP), a nucleotide you will meet again in Chapter 12.
  3. cAMP switches on a protein kinase (protein kinase A in the figure), which phosphorylates glycogen phosphorylase (switching it ON) and phosphorylates glycogen synthase (switching it OFF). Glycogen is broken down and, in the liver, glucose is released into the blood.
  4. After a meal, insulin from the pancreas activates the protein phosphatases. Both phosphates come off: phosphorylase stops, synthase starts, and the incoming glucose is stored as glycogen.

One hormone molecule activates many kinase molecules, each of which phosphorylates many enzyme molecules, so the signal is amplified thousands of times in a few seconds. That is why a tiny dose of epinephrine has such a large and rapid effect.

Other covalent changes

Covalent modification means any change in enzyme activity brought about by adding or removing a chemical group through a covalent bond. Phosphorylation is the main reversible kind. Zymogen activation is a covalent change too, since peptide bonds are hydrolyzed, but it runs one way only: once the blocking piece is cut off, it cannot be reattached. Some enzymes are synthesized in this inactive form (a zymogen or proenzyme), transported to the part of the body where the active form is needed, and then "turned on." Cells use it for enzymes that must never act inside the cell that makes them: the digestive proteases (pepsinogen, trypsinogen, chymotrypsinogen) and the clotting factors (prothrombin → thrombin; thrombin then trims fibrinogen to fibrin). Insulin is made the same way: the single-chain precursor proinsulin becomes active insulin when peptidases remove the 31-amino-acid C-peptide (plus two linking pairs of amino acids, 35 in all), leaving two chains held together by disulfide bonds (Chapter 10).

Genetic control: induction and repression

Every enzyme is a protein, and a protein is made only when its gene is copied into messenger RNA (transcription) and the mRNA is read by ribosomes (translation); Chapter 12 gives the details. The cell can therefore control an enzyme at the source: make more of it (induction) or make less (repression). Because mRNA and protein must be built, and the old enzyme molecules must be worn out and degraded, the change takes hours to days. It is the slow, long-term switch.

  • Lactase. The enzyme that hydrolyzes lactose is made at high levels in every infant's intestine. In most of the world's population its gene is turned down after weaning, and by adulthood too little lactase is left to handle a glass of milk: undigested lactose draws water into the gut and is fermented by bacteria, giving the bloating, cramping, and diarrhea of lactose intolerance. This decline after childhood is the most common pattern worldwide, including in many Latino, Asian, African and Indigenous families; people of northern European and some African and Middle Eastern ancestries more often carry a variant that keeps the gene on (lactase persistence).
  • Liver drug-metabolizing enzymes. The liver clears most drugs, and alcohol, by oxidizing them with a family of oxidoreductases. Several of these enzymes are inducible: a substance taken day after day signals the liver to make more of the enzyme that handles it. This is enzyme induction: a high level of a reactant turns on transcription of the enzyme that uses it. The patient now clears not only that substance but every other drug the same enzyme handles faster, so those drugs fall to lower blood levels than intended. The reverse also happens: a substance that inhibits the enzyme (the fast, reversible kind of control from Section 11.4) raises the levels of drugs that share it. The Clinical Connection boxes give two examples.
  • Bacteria do it too. A bacterium growing on glucose does not bother making the enzymes for digesting lactose; when lactose appears, the genes for those enzymes are switched on within minutes, and when it is gone they are switched off again. This is the simplest case of enzyme induction, and the first genetic switch ever worked out.

Worked examples

Example 1: Predict the effect of a kinase

Problem. The table lists how phosphorylation affects four enzymes. A hormone activates a kinase that phosphorylates all four. Predict what happens to glycogen storage, fat storage, and the release of fatty acids from fat cells.

Enzymes switched on or off by phosphorylation
EnzymeJobActive form
Glycogen phosphorylaseBreaks down glycogenPhosphorylated
Glycogen synthaseBuilds glycogenDephosphorylated
Acetyl-CoA carboxylaseFirst step of fatty-acid synthesisDephosphorylated
Hormone-sensitive lipaseHydrolyzes stored fat to fatty acidsPhosphorylated

Solution. Read each row: if the active form is "phosphorylated," the kinase turns the enzyme on; if "dephosphorylated," it turns it off. Glycogen phosphorylase on and glycogen synthase off: glycogen is broken down, not stored. Acetyl-CoA carboxylase off: no new fat is made. Hormone-sensitive lipase on: stored fat is hydrolyzed and fatty acids pour into the blood. All four changes release stored fuel.

Check. That is exactly the pattern you would want from epinephrine during a sprint or from glucagon during a fast; these are the hormones this kinase answers to. A phosphatase (insulin's signal) would reverse every row.

Example 2: Classify the regulation

Problem. Name the regulation strategy in each scenario and say whether it is reversible. (a) A patient who begins drinking alcohol daily is found, three weeks later, to clear a sedative drug twice as fast as before. (b) In a cut, prothrombin in the blood is converted to thrombin by removal of part of its chain. (c) Five minutes after an insulin injection, the phosphate groups on glycogen synthase in the patient's liver are gone and the enzyme is active. (d) In a muscle cell during exercise, AMP binds phosphofructokinase at a site away from the active site and the enzyme speeds up.

Solution. (a) Genetic control (enzyme induction): the time scale of weeks and the change in the amount of enzyme give it away. Reversible once the inducer is stopped, over days. (b) Zymogen activation: hydrolysis of a peptide bond; irreversible. (c) Covalent modification (dephosphorylation by a phosphatase); reversible by a kinase. (d) Allosteric control by a positive regulator; reversible as soon as AMP leaves.

Check. Only one of the four involves a change in the number of enzyme molecules; the other three change the activity of enzymes that already exist.

Example 3: An enzyme inducer and a second drug

Problem. A patient on a steady dose of a drug that is cleared by a liver oxidoreductase starts taking a second substance every day that induces that same enzyme. Over the next two weeks the blood level of the first drug drops by half. Explain what happened and why it took two weeks.

Solution. The second substance induces the gene, so the liver makes more of the enzyme. Rate is proportional to enzyme concentration (Section 11.3), so with about twice as much enzyme the first drug is now oxidized and cleared roughly twice as fast at any given blood level, and its blood level falls until the lower level balances the same daily dose. It took two weeks because induction is genetic control: new mRNA and new enzyme protein had to be made and accumulate.

Check. If the second substance were an inhibitor of the enzyme instead, the first drug's level would have risen, and within hours rather than weeks. Direction (down) and time scale (weeks) both point to induction.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Drug interactions through liver enzymes. Many drugs share the same liver oxidoreductases, so a medication, herb or food that induces one of those enzymes lowers the blood level of every other drug it clears, and one that inhibits the enzyme raises those levels. This is why a medication history asks about everything a patient takes, and why a drug with a narrow margin between too little and too much, such as the anticoagulant warfarin, is monitored with a blood test (the INR, a clotting-time test) whenever another medication is started or stopped. Know one example of each kind. The antibiotic rifampin is a strong inducer of CYP3A, an enzyme family that clears many drugs,1 and the herbal supplement St. John’s wort can weaken birth control pills, warfarin and some statins such as simvastatin.2 Going the other way, grapefruit juice inhibits CYP3A4 in the small intestine, so more of a drug such as simvastatin gets into the blood.3 Insulin signaling. Insulin works by switching on phosphatases and kinases that store fuel; in type 1 diabetes the hormone is missing, and in type 2 the cells answer it poorly (insulin resistance), so glycogen synthase stays off and glucose stays in the blood (hyperglycemia). Insulin is a peptide and would be hydrolyzed by digestive proteases, which is why it is injected, never swallowed.

Sources: 1. U.S. Food and Drug Administration, Drug Development and Drug Interactions: Table of Substrates, Inhibitors and Inducers, accessed 2026. 2. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health, St. John’s Wort, accessed 2026. 3. U.S. Food and Drug Administration, Grapefruit Juice and Some Drugs Don’t Mix, accessed 2026.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Why the alcohol history matters. A patient who drinks heavily every day has induced several liver oxidoreductases, including those that metabolize acetaminophen and many sedatives, and possibly some that clear amide local anesthetics such as lidocaine and mepivacaine (Chapter 7). Sedatives (and possibly the anesthetic) may be cleared from the blood faster than usual, and the induced enzyme converts acetaminophen to a toxic product faster, which is why the alcohol history is part of every medical history. A patient who is acutely intoxicated is a different case: alcohol in the blood competes for the same enzymes and slows drug clearance. Epinephrine in the cartridge. The epinephrine added to local anesthetic constricts blood vessels at the injection site, but any that reaches the circulation does what it does in the liver: raises cAMP and switches glycogen breakdown on. That is one reason the dose of epinephrine-containing anesthetic is limited in patients with poorly controlled diabetes or heart disease.

Summary

  • Cells regulate enzymes on two time scales: fast changes in the activity of enzymes that already exist (allosteric control, feedback inhibition, covalent modification, zymogen activation) and slow changes in the amount of enzyme (genetic control).
  • Covalent modification usually means phosphorylation. A protein kinase (a transferase) moves a phosphate from ATP onto a serine, threonine, or tyrosine –OH, forming a phosphate ester; a protein phosphatase (a hydrolase) removes it with water.
  • Glucagon and epinephrine raise cAMP, activate a protein kinase, and phosphorylate glycogen phosphorylase (ON) and glycogen synthase (OFF); insulin activates phosphatases and reverses both. The signal is amplified at every step.
  • Zymogen activation (trypsinogen → trypsin, proinsulin → insulin) is a covalent change that cannot be reversed.
  • Genetic control works by induction or repression of a gene and takes hours to days. Lactase decline after weaning, liver enzyme induction by drugs and alcohol, and lactose enzymes induced in bacteria are the standard examples.
  • An enzyme inducer lowers the blood level of other drugs cleared by that enzyme; an inhibitor raises it.

Exercises

Exercise 1

Write a word equation for what a protein kinase does to an enzyme with a serine –OH, and classify the kinase. Then do the same for the phosphatase.

Answer
Enzyme–OH + ATP → enzyme–O–PO32− + ADP; the kinase is a transferase (it moves a phosphate group). Enzyme–O–PO32− + H2O → enzyme–OH + HPO42−; the phosphatase is a hydrolase.

Exercise 2

Which three amino acids can be phosphorylated, and what functional group do they share that makes this possible? What type of bond is formed?

Answer
Serine, threonine, and tyrosine. Each has a side-chain hydroxyl (–OH; alcohol in serine and threonine, phenol in tyrosine). The phosphate attaches as a phosphate ester, the condensation product of the –OH with phosphoric acid.

Exercise 3

A new drug blocks the enzyme that makes cAMP in liver cells. Predict its effect on glycogen phosphorylase and on glycogen synthase when glucagon is released, and explain.

Answer
Glucagon's signal stops at the second step: without cAMP, protein kinase A is not activated, so neither enzyme is phosphorylated. Glycogen phosphorylase stays off and glycogen synthase stays on; the liver keeps storing glycogen instead of releasing glucose, and blood glucose could fall.

Exercise 4

Explain why zymogen activation is listed as "irreversible" while phosphorylation is "reversible," even though both involve covalent bonds.

Answer
Phosphorylation adds a group that a second enzyme (a phosphatase) can remove by hydrolysis, so the enzyme cycles between two forms. Zymogen activation hydrolyzes a peptide bond and discards a segment of the chain; no cellular enzyme reattaches it, so the active enzyme stays active until it is degraded.

Exercise 5

A patient's blood level of a drug rises sharply, within a day, after she starts a second medication. Is the second medication more likely an inducer or an inhibitor of the liver enzyme that clears the first drug? Give two reasons.

Answer
An inhibitor. (1) Direction: inhibiting the clearing enzyme makes the drug accumulate, so its level rises; induction would lower it. (2) Speed: inhibition is a direct binding event that acts within hours, whereas induction requires new enzyme to be made and takes days to weeks.

Exercise 6

Lactose intolerance in adults and the lactose-digesting enzymes of bacteria both involve an enzyme that hydrolyzes lactose, but the regulation runs in opposite directions. Explain.

Answer
In bacteria, lactose induces the enzyme: it is made only when lactose is present. In most adult humans the lactase gene is repressed after weaning regardless of diet, so the enzyme is scarce even when lactose arrives. Both are genetic control, but one is turned on by the substrate and the other is turned down by a developmental program.

Exercise 7

Classify each as allosteric control, feedback inhibition, covalent modification, zymogen activation, or genetic control: (a) chymotrypsinogen is cut by trypsin; (b) a bacterium stops making tryptophan when tryptophan accumulates, because tryptophan binds the first enzyme of the pathway away from its active site; (c) after two weeks of daily drinking, a patient makes more of the liver enzyme that oxidizes alcohol; (d) epinephrine causes hormone-sensitive lipase in fat cells to gain a phosphate and become active.

Answer
(a) Zymogen activation. (b) Feedback inhibition (allosteric, by the end product). (c) Genetic control (induction). (d) Covalent modification (phosphorylation by a kinase).

Exercise 8

Using the idea that rate is proportional to enzyme concentration, explain in one or two sentences why doubling the amount of a drug-clearing liver enzyme can halve a drug's steady blood level.

Answer
With twice the enzyme, the drug is cleared twice as fast at any given concentration. A fixed daily dose can only balance that faster clearance at a lower concentration, so the steady level settles at about half.
