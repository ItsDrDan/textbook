Multiple Choice

Problem 11.1

An enzyme speeds up a reaction by

  1. raising the temperature of the cell
  2. lowering the activation energy
  3. changing the products formed
  4. being consumed in the reaction
Answer
B. An enzyme provides a different path with a lower energy barrier, so more collisions succeed at body temperature. It does not change the products (C) and is not used up (D).

Problem 11.2

Which enzyme class catalyzes: sucrose + H2O → glucose + fructose?

  1. isomerase
  2. hydrolase
  3. ligase
  4. oxidoreductase
Answer
B. Water is used to split a bond (the glycosidic bond of sucrose), which is hydrolysis, so the enzyme (sucrase) is a hydrolase.

Problem 11.3

The induced-fit model states that

  1. the active site is rigid
  2. the active site adjusts its shape when the substrate binds
  3. any substrate fits any enzyme
  4. enzymes change shape only when denatured
Answer
B. In induced fit the active site is flexible and changes shape to wrap around the substrate as it binds. A rigid active site (A) is the lock-and-key model.

Problem 11.4

Above the optimum temperature, enzyme activity drops because the enzyme

  1. is diluted
  2. is denatured
  3. runs out of cofactors
  4. is converted to a zymogen
Answer
B. Heat breaks the R-group interactions that hold the enzyme’s 3-D shape, so the active site loses its shape and can no longer bind the substrate.

Problem 11.5

An inhibitor that resembles the substrate and binds the active site is a(n)

  1. competitive inhibitor
  2. noncompetitive inhibitor
  3. allosteric activator
  4. coenzyme
Answer
A. A competitive inhibitor looks like the substrate and competes for the active site; adding more substrate can outcompete it. A noncompetitive inhibitor binds elsewhere and changes the enzyme’s shape.

Problem 11.6

Which vitamin is fat-soluble?

  1. vitamin C
  2. riboflavin (B2)
  3. vitamin K
  4. niacin (B3)
Answer
C. The fat-soluble vitamins are A, D, E, and K; they are stored in fatty tissue. Vitamin C and the B vitamins (riboflavin, niacin) are water-soluble.

Problem 11.7

Coenzyme NAD+ is made from which vitamin?

  1. thiamine (B1)
  2. niacin (B3)
  3. biotin
  4. vitamin A
Answer
B. NAD+ (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) contains nicotinamide, which comes from niacin. Thiamine (A) forms thiamine pyrophosphate, a different coenzyme.

True or False? (If false, correct it.)

Problem 11.8

Adding more substrate overcomes noncompetitive inhibition.

Answer
False. More substrate overcomes competitive inhibition only.

Problem 11.9

When the final product of a pathway slows an enzyme near the start of that same pathway, the regulation is called feedback control.

Answer
True. The end product acts as an allosteric inhibitor of an early enzyme, so the pathway slows down when enough product has built up and speeds up again when it is used.

Match each enzyme to its class: (1) hexokinase (2) lactate dehydrogenase (3) triose phosphate isomerase (4) lipase. Choices: hydrolase, transferase, oxidoreductase, isomerase.

Answer
(1) transferase; (2) oxidoreductase; (3) isomerase; (4) hydrolase

Short Answer

Problem 11.11

Sketch and explain the shape of a graph of rate vs. substrate concentration at constant enzyme concentration.

Answer

Problem 11.12

Pepsin works best near pH 2. Predict and explain its activity at pH 8.

Answer
Very low. At pH 8 the charges on R groups in and around the active site change, disrupting substrate binding and the enzyme’s shape.

Problem 11.13

What is a zymogen? Give one example.

Answer
An inactive form of an enzyme that is activated later (often by cutting off part of the chain). Examples: pepsinogen, trypsinogen, chymotrypsinogen. (Insulin, a hormone, is activated the same way from proinsulin.)

Problem 11.38

What is the difference between a cofactor and a coenzyme?

More Practice: Analyze and Compare

Problem 11.52

The graph shows the rate of one enzyme-catalyzed reaction as substrate is added, measured with no inhibitor (curve A), with inhibitor X (curve B), and with inhibitor Y (curve C).

(a) Which inhibitor is competitive and which is noncompetitive? (b) Curve B is still climbing at the right edge of the graph. If you kept adding substrate, would it eventually reach curve A? Would curve C? Explain. (c) If inhibitor Y bound covalently instead of reversibly, which curve would look the same and why?

Answer
(a) X is competitive and Y is noncompetitive. (b) Curve B rises more slowly because X competes with substrate for the active site, but at very high substrate the substrate wins almost every time, so curve B eventually reaches the same maximum rate as A. Y binds somewhere else and disables a fraction of the enzyme molecules no matter how much substrate is present, so the maximum rate itself is lowered and C never catches up. (c) Curve C. An irreversible inhibitor also removes a fraction of the enzyme permanently; on this kind of plot it looks like noncompetitive inhibition, with a lowered maximum rate that substrate cannot restore. The difference is that washing the inhibitor away would restore Y's enzyme but not a covalently modified one.

Problem 11.53

A student measured the activity of an unknown digestive enzyme at 37 °C in a series of buffers and recorded the data in the table. The relative rate is the product formed per minute compared with the fastest trial.

Relative reaction rate of an enzyme at different pH values
pH1.02.03.04.56.07.59.0
Relative rate (%)551007015300

(a) What is the optimum pH? (b) Which of the enzymes in this chapter is this most likely to be, and in what organ does it work? (c) Would you expect the activity at pH 7.5 to return if the sample were brought back to pH 2.0? Explain.

Answer
(a) About pH 2, where the rate is highest. (b) Pepsin, the protease of the stomach, whose optimum is near 2. Trypsin (optimum near 8) would show the opposite pattern. (c) Probably not. Small moves away from the optimum change the charges on active-site R groups reversibly, but a change from pH 2 to 7.5 is large enough to denature pepsin, and denaturation of a protein by pH is usually permanent. (In the body this is how pepsin is inactivated once it reaches the small intestine.)

Problem 11.54

Compare and contrast a competitive inhibitor and a negative allosteric regulator. Give one similarity and two differences, and name a drug or metabolite that is an example of each.

Answer
Similarity: both bind the enzyme reversibly and lower its activity; both let go, so the inhibition ends when their concentration falls. Differences: (1) A competitive inhibitor binds the active site and resembles the substrate; an allosteric regulator binds a separate regulatory site and need not look like the substrate. (2) Competitive inhibition is overcome by more substrate; allosteric inhibition is not, because the regulator is not competing for the same site. Examples: a statin (competitive at HMG-CoA reductase) and ATP as a negative allosteric regulator of phosphofructokinase, or the end product of any feedback-controlled pathway.

Problem 11.55

Two enzyme preparations are stored for a week: one in a refrigerator at 4 °C and one in a hot car that reached 60 °C. Both are then assayed at 37 °C. Predict the activity of each relative to a fresh sample and explain the difference in terms of protein structure.

Answer
The refrigerated sample should show nearly full activity: cold slows reactions but does not break the hydrogen bonds and other weak interactions that hold the tertiary structure, so the active site is intact once rewarmed. The sample from the hot car will have little or no activity: 60 °C is well above the optimum of a human enzyme, the weak interactions were disrupted, the chain unfolded (denatured), and the active site no longer exists. Heat denaturation is not reversed by cooling.

More Practice: Clinical and Integrative

Problem 11.56

Which statement about enzymes and energy is correct?

  1. An enzyme lowers ΔG for the reaction so that more product forms at equilibrium.
  2. An enzyme lowers the activation energy and leaves ΔG and the equilibrium position unchanged.
  3. An enzyme raises the energy of the products so the reaction stops sooner.
  4. An enzyme supplies the activation energy from ATP.
Answer
B. A catalyst changes only the path between reactants and products (the height of the hill), not the energies of the reactants or products themselves, so ΔG and the equilibrium constant are the same with or without the enzyme; equilibrium is simply reached sooner. Choice D confuses catalysis with the ATP-driven reactions of ligases and kinases, where ATP is a reactant, not a source of activation energy.

Problem 11.57

In the citric acid cycle, malate + NAD+ → oxaloacetate + NADH + H+. The enzyme is a(n)

  1. hydrolase
  2. lyase
  3. oxidoreductase
  4. ligase
Answer
C. NAD+ is reduced to NADH while malate's secondary alcohol is oxidized to a ketone; any reaction with NAD+/NADH or FAD/FADH2 in the equation is catalyzed by an oxidoreductase (here malate dehydrogenase). No water splits a bond (not a hydrolase) and no ATP joins two molecules (not a ligase).

Problem 11.58

Pyruvate carboxylase catalyzes pyruvate + CO2 + ATP → oxaloacetate + ADP + Pi, and pyruvate decarboxylase catalyzes pyruvate → acetaldehyde + CO2. These enzymes belong, respectively, to the classes

  1. ligase and lyase
  2. lyase and ligase
  3. transferase and hydrolase
  4. ligase and oxidoreductase
Answer
A. The carboxylase uses ATP to join two molecules (pyruvate and CO2) into one, the definition of a ligase; it also needs biotin as its coenzyme. The decarboxylase splits one molecule into two with no water, no ATP, and no redox, the definition of a lyase. Notice that the reactions are almost mirror images, and the names ("carboxylase" vs. "decarboxylase") tell you which way each goes.

Problem 11.59

A child who has been eating chips of old lead-based paint has elevated blood lead and several enzymes with cysteine in their active sites have stopped working. The lead ions are acting as

  1. competitive inhibitors, since Pb2+ resembles the substrate
  2. coenzymes that the enzymes cannot use
  3. irreversible inhibitors that bond to –SH groups and change the enzyme's shape
  4. positive allosteric regulators
Answer
C. Heavy-metal ions such as Pb2+ and Hg2+ have a strong affinity for sulfur; they bond to cysteine –SH groups in or near the active site, the shape changes, and the substrate can no longer enter. The bond is not undone by adding substrate or by dilution, so the inhibition is irreversible, and because the metal does not compete with the substrate it also counts as noncompetitive; the cell must make new enzyme. Lead ions do not resemble any substrate (A), they are not organic molecules (B), and they lower activity rather than raise it (D).

Problem 11.60

A farm worker arrives in the emergency department after a pesticide spill, drooling, sweating, with twitching muscles and difficulty breathing. The pesticide is an organophosphate. (a) Which enzyme has been inhibited and what type of inhibition is it? (b) Why does the level of acetylcholine at the synapse rise? (c) Why will the patient not recover simply by waiting for the pesticide to wash out of the blood?

Answer
(a) Acetylcholinesterase, the hydrolase that breaks down the neurotransmitter acetylcholine (an ester, Chapter 6; see also Section 7.11). Organophosphates phosphorylate the serine –OH in its active site, a covalent and therefore irreversible inhibition. (b) Acetylcholine is released normally but is no longer hydrolyzed, so it accumulates and keeps stimulating glands (saliva, sweat, tears) and muscles (twitching, then exhaustion and paralysis of the breathing muscles). (c) The enzyme is covalently modified; removing free pesticide from the blood does not restore it. Recovery requires the body to make new enzyme (or, in the first hours, a drug that can pull the phosphate off before the bond "ages"), which is why organophosphate poisoning is an emergency.

Problem 11.61

A dental hygienist notes that a patient's gingiva bleed easily on probing, the patient reports fatigue, and the diet history shows almost no fruit or vegetables for several months. (a) Which vitamin deficiency fits, and what is the disease called? (b) Which protein is affected, and what does the vitamin do in making it? (c) Why does the deficiency develop within months rather than years?

Answer
(a) Vitamin C (ascorbic acid); the disease is scurvy. (b) Collagen, the main protein of gingiva, periodontal ligament, and blood-vessel walls. Vitamin C keeps the iron cofactor of prolyl hydroxylase in its reduced (Fe2+) state so the enzyme can add –OH groups to proline residues; without those hydroxyl groups the collagen triple helix is unstable and the tissue is weak, so capillaries leak and gums bleed. (c) Vitamin C is water-soluble and is not stored in fat; the body holds only a small reserve and excretes the excess, so a diet without it empties the supply in a few months. A fat-soluble vitamin such as A or D, stored in the liver and adipose tissue, would take much longer to run out.

Problem 11.62

Sucrose is more cariogenic (cavity-causing) than glucose even though both are fermented to acid by plaque bacteria. Using the enzyme glucosyltransferase of Streptococcus mutans, which builds sticky glucan polymers from sucrose, explain why. Classify the enzyme and name the bond type it forms.

Answer
Glucosyltransferase moves the glucose unit of sucrose onto a growing glucan chain and releases fructose; the glucans are sticky polysaccharides that glue bacteria to the enamel surface and thicken the plaque biofilm, so acid is produced right against the tooth and saliva cannot buffer it away. Glucose alone can be fermented but cannot be used to build these glucans. The enzyme is a transferase (it moves a glucosyl group from one molecule to another), and the bonds it forms are glycosidic linkages (acetals, Chapter 8). The energy stored in sucrose's glycosidic bond is what pays for the transfer; the bacterium needs no ATP.

Problem 11.63

A hospitalized patient with a long history of heavy drinking is to receive an IV infusion of D5W (5% dextrose in water). The nurse checks that thiamine has been given first. Explain the chemistry: which coenzyme is made from thiamine, which reaction needs it, and why giving glucose before thiamine can make the patient worse.

Answer
Thiamine (vitamin B1) is the precursor of thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP), the coenzyme of pyruvate dehydrogenase, which converts pyruvate from glycolysis into acetyl-CoA for the citric acid cycle. Alcohol use disorder depletes thiamine (poor diet, poor absorption). A glucose load drives glycolysis and produces pyruvate that cannot be processed without TPP, so the small remaining thiamine is consumed and the brain, which depends on glucose, is starved of energy; this can precipitate or worsen Wernicke encephalopathy. Giving thiamine first restores the coenzyme before the glucose arrives.

Problem 11.64

Integrative. Aspirin (Chapter 6) is an ester that transfers its acetyl group to a serine –OH in the active site of cyclooxygenase. (a) What functional group is formed on the enzyme, and what small molecule is released? (b) Why is this inhibition irreversible while the binding of a statin to HMG-CoA reductase is not? (c) Aspirin's effect on platelets lasts about a week even though the drug is gone from the blood in hours. Explain using the idea of genetic control.

Answer
(a) The serine –OH becomes an ester (an acetyl ester, O–C(=O)CH3), and salicylic acid, the other half of aspirin, is released. (b) Aspirin forms a covalent bond to the enzyme; a statin binds the active site by shape and weak interactions only, so it comes and goes, and more substrate can displace it. (c) A platelet has no nucleus and cannot transcribe genes, so it cannot make new cyclooxygenase to replace the acetylated enzyme. The effect lasts until the body makes new platelets, about 7–10 days. Nucleated cells recover within a day because they can induce fresh enzyme.

Problem 11.65

Integrative. Insulin is a peptide hormone, yet its main effect on liver enzymes is to remove phosphate groups. Connect the chapters: (a) why can insulin not be taken as a pill (Chapter 10)? (b) What class of enzyme does insulin ultimately activate to dephosphorylate glycogen synthase, and what bond does that enzyme break? (c) What happens to blood glucose in a patient who misses an insulin dose, and which glycogen enzyme is left in the "on" state?

Answer
(a) Insulin is a protein; the proteases of the stomach and intestine (pepsin, trypsin, chymotrypsin) would hydrolyze its peptide bonds, and the fragments are inactive, so it must be injected. (b) A protein phosphatase, a hydrolase. It hydrolyzes the phosphate ester bond between a serine –OH of the enzyme and the phosphate, releasing HPO42−. (c) Without insulin the phosphatases stay quiet while glucagon's kinase pathway continues, so glycogen phosphorylase stays phosphorylated and "on," the liver keeps releasing glucose, and glucose from food is not stored: blood glucose rises (hyperglycemia), and in type 1 diabetes this can progress to diabetic ketoacidosis.

Problem 11.66

Lactase hydrolyzes lactose in the small intestine at an optimum pH of about 6. All except which of the following would decrease the rate of the lactase-catalyzed reaction?

  1. Lowering the lactose concentration
  2. Adding more lactase
  3. Changing the pH to 2
  4. Running the reaction at 70 °C
Answer
B. More enzyme means more active sites and a faster reaction (with substrate in excess, rate is proportional to enzyme concentration). Less substrate (A) means fewer collisions; pH 2 (C) is four units from the optimum and changes the charges in the active site; 70 °C (D) denatures the enzyme.

Problem 11.67

In the lock-and-key model of enzyme action, the active site

  1. is a rigid cavity that the substrate must match exactly
  2. changes shape to wrap around the substrate
  3. is located on the regulator rather than on the enzyme
  4. binds any molecule of about the right size
Answer
A. Lock-and-key pictures a rigid active site and a substrate shaped to fit it exactly. Choice B is the induced-fit model, in which the site is flexible and adjusts to the substrate. Neither model allows just any molecule to bind (D), and the active site is always on the enzyme (C).

Problem 11.68

Match each enzyme name to the reaction it catalyzes: (1) deaminase, (2) kinase, (3) lipase, (4) dehydrogenase. Reactions: (A) transfers a phosphate group from ATP to a substrate; (B) removes two H atoms to form a C=O or C=C; (C) removes NH3 from a substrate; (D) hydrolyzes the ester bonds of a fat.

Answer
(1) C, a lyase (a small molecule is removed, no water needed); (2) A, a transferase; (3) D, a hydrolase; (4) B, an oxidoreductase, which needs NAD+ or FAD to accept the hydrogens.

Problem 11.69

Classify the enzyme from the description, then name the class: (a) converts a cis double bond in a fatty acid to a trans double bond; (b) adds a molecule of water across the C=C of fumarate; (c) joins two amino acids using the energy of ATP; (d) splits a disaccharide into two monosaccharides with water.

Answer
(a) Isomerase: one molecule, same formula, rearranged. (b) Lyase (a hydratase): water is added to one molecule and nothing is split in two, so this is a hydration, not a hydrolysis. (c) Ligase (a synthetase): two molecules joined at the cost of ATP. (d) Hydrolase: water breaks the glycosidic bond and one molecule becomes two.

Problem 11.70

Explain in two or three sentences what specificity means for an enzyme, and name the three categories of specificity with an example of each.

Answer
Specificity means an enzyme catalyzes only one reaction or one narrow set of reactions, because only a substrate with the right shape and pattern of charges fits its active site. Absolute specificity: one substrate only (urease acts only on urea). Group specificity: one type of substrate (hexokinase phosphorylates several hexoses). Linkage specificity: one type of bond (lipases hydrolyze ester bonds wherever they occur in a lipid). Enzymes are also specific for stereochemistry, acting on D-sugars and L-amino acids but not their mirror images.

Problem 11.71

Sketch (or describe) the graph of reaction rate versus enzyme concentration when substrate is present in large excess. How does it differ from the graph of rate versus substrate concentration?

Answer
Rate versus enzyme concentration (with excess substrate) is a straight line through the origin: twice the enzyme, twice the rate, because every added enzyme molecule finds plenty of substrate. Rate versus substrate concentration (fixed enzyme) rises steeply, then bends over and levels off at a maximum when all the active sites are occupied (saturation). Clinical enzyme assays rely on the first graph; the second shows why adding more substrate eventually stops helping.

Problem 11.72

Match each description to the type of regulation: (1) the end product of a four-step pathway binds the first enzyme at a site away from its active site and slows it; (2) a phosphate is added to an enzyme by a kinase and the enzyme becomes active; (3) pepsinogen is converted to pepsin when part of its chain is removed in the stomach; (4) a regulator binds away from the active site and makes the active site bind substrate better. Types: positive allosteric control, feedback control, zymogen activation (covalent modification by hydrolysis), covalent modification by phosphorylation.

Answer
(1) feedback control (a negative allosteric effect by the end product); (2) covalent modification by phosphorylation; (3) zymogen activation, a covalent modification in which peptide bonds are broken; (4) positive allosteric control. In (1) and (4) the regulator binds reversibly and no covalent bond changes; in (2) and (3) a covalent bond is formed or broken.

Problem 11.73

True or false? If false, correct it: "A noncompetitive inhibitor has a structure similar to the substrate and binds in the active site."

Answer
False; that describes a competitive inhibitor. A noncompetitive inhibitor binds to the enzyme somewhere other than the active site, distorts the enzyme's shape so the substrate no longer fits, and need not resemble the substrate. Its effect cannot be reversed by adding more substrate, although it is reversible when the inhibitor leaves.

Problem 11.74

Identify each as a water-soluble vitamin or a fat-soluble vitamin, and give one job for each: (a) riboflavin (B2); (b) vitamin D; (c) ascorbic acid; (d) vitamin K; (e) pantothenic acid (B5).

Answer
(a) Water-soluble; precursor of FAD, which carries hydrogens in oxidation reactions. (b) Fat-soluble; regulates calcium and phosphate absorption for bone and tooth mineralization. (c) Water-soluble; needed to make collagen (prevents scurvy). (d) Fat-soluble; needed to make the zymogens for blood clotting. (e) Water-soluble; part of coenzyme A, which carries acyl groups.

Problem 11.75

Which of the following is a coenzyme rather than a cofactor, according to the definitions used in class?

  1. Zn2+
  2. NAD+
  3. Mg2+
  4. Fe2+
Answer
B. NAD+ is an organic molecule made from the vitamin niacin, so it is a coenzyme. The three metal ions are inorganic cofactors: Zn2+ in carboxypeptidase and carbonic anhydrase, Mg2+ with ATP in kinases, and Fe2+ in heme.

Problem 11.76

A bacterium makes the amino acid histidine in a ten-step pathway. When histidine is plentiful, the first enzyme of the pathway slows down; when histidine is used up, the enzyme speeds up again. (a) Name this type of regulation. (b) Is the histidine binding at the active site? (c) Why is it more efficient to slow the first enzyme than the last one?

Answer
(a) Feedback control (feedback inhibition): the end product inhibits an earlier enzyme in the sequence. (b) No; it binds an allosteric (regulatory) site, since histidine does not resemble the first enzyme's substrate. (c) Stopping the first step means no intermediates are made and then wasted; if only the last enzyme were blocked, the cell would keep spending energy building nine intermediates it could not use.

Board-Style Case Set: Bleeding gums and a blood thinner

Health-care licensing exams such as the National Board Dental Hygiene Examination use case sets: a short patient scenario followed by several questions. Read the case, then answer each question.

The case

A 70-year-old man with a limited diet has swollen gums that bleed easily, and his health history lists warfarin for atrial fibrillation. The hygienist notes a possible vitamin C deficiency and plans for extra bleeding.

Case Set Question 11.1

Vitamin C deficiency (scurvy) causes fragile, bleeding gums because vitamin C is needed

Problem 11.77

A student writes: “Adding a large excess of substrate restores the normal maximum rate when a noncompetitive inhibitor is present.” Find the error and correct it.

Answer
That is true only for a competitive inhibitor, which competes with the substrate for the active site. A noncompetitive inhibitor binds elsewhere and changes the enzyme’s shape, so it lowers the maximum rate no matter how much substrate is added. (An irreversible inhibitor cannot be overcome by substrate either.) Review: Section 11.4.

Cumulative Problem 11.1 (Chapters 4, 6, 8, 9 and 11)

Match each enzyme with its class and with the earlier-chapter reaction it catalyzes: lactase, lipase, alcohol dehydrogenase.

  1. lactase: hydrolase, glycosidic (acetal) bond; lipase: hydrolase, ester bond; alcohol dehydrogenase: oxidoreductase, alcohol → aldehyde
  2. lactase: transferase; lipase: ligase; alcohol dehydrogenase: hydrolase
  3. lactase: lyase; lipase: isomerase; alcohol dehydrogenase: hydrolase
  4. all three are hydrolases
Answer
A. Lactase hydrolyzes the acetal (glycosidic) bond of lactose (Chapter 8); lipase hydrolyzes the ester bonds of triacylglycerols (Chapters 6 and 9); both add water across a bond, so they are hydrolases. Alcohol dehydrogenase removes two hydrogens from ethanol to make acetaldehyde (Chapter 4), an oxidation, so it is an oxidoreductase, and it hands the hydrogens to the coenzyme NAD+. The suffix “-ase” plus the substrate or reaction type usually tells you the class.

Cumulative Problem 11.2 (Chapters 1, 6, 7, 10 and 11)

An enzyme’s active site binds its substrate by the same forces that hold the enzyme’s own shape. For an enzyme whose active site contains an aspartate, a serine and a leucine, describe what kind of group on a substrate each residue could hold and name the force. Then explain why the enzyme is specific for that substrate.

Answer
Aspartate (–COO−) can hold a positively charged group such as an ammonium, –NH3+, by a salt bridge (ionic attraction). Serine (–OH) can hydrogen bond to an alcohol, carbonyl or amine on the substrate. Leucine provides a nonpolar pocket for a hydrocarbon group through hydrophobic (dispersion) interaction. The active site is a pocket whose shape and arrangement of these groups matches one substrate (lock-and-key, or induced fit after a small adjustment); a molecule with the wrong shape or its polar and nonpolar groups in the wrong places cannot line up all the interactions at once, so it does not bind well. Specificity is Chapter 1 intermolecular forces arranged in three dimensions.

Cumulative Problem 11.3 (Chapters 4 and 11)

Methanol poisoning is treated with fomepizole (the first choice) or, as an older alternative, ethanol; both act on alcohol dehydrogenase. (a) Why is ethanol a competitive inhibitor of methanol oxidation? (b) Describe the effect of a competitive inhibitor on an enzyme and how raising the substrate concentration changes it. (c) How does a noncompetitive inhibitor differ?

Answer
(a) Ethanol and methanol are both small 1° alcohols that fit the same active site; when ethanol occupies it, methanol cannot bind, so methanol is not oxidized to formaldehyde and formic acid (Chapter 4) and is excreted unchanged. (b) A competitive inhibitor resembles the substrate and competes for the active site; raising the substrate concentration lets the substrate win more often, so the inhibition can be overcome. (c) A noncompetitive inhibitor binds somewhere else on the enzyme and changes the active site’s shape; it does not look like the substrate and cannot be overcome by more substrate. Cyanide, which binds the iron of cytochrome oxidase, acts this way; heavy-metal ions that bind –SH groups (Chapter 10) also act away from the active site; in this course they count as both noncompetitive and irreversible inhibitors.

Cumulative Problem 11.4 (Chapters 6, 7, 10 and 11)

Pepsin works best at pH 2 and trypsin at pH 8, yet both hydrolyze peptide bonds. (a) Why does each enzyme have an optimum pH? (b) What happens to pepsin when it reaches the small intestine? (c) Why does a high fever (above about 41 °C) become dangerous?

Answer
(a) The acidic and basic side chains in the active site (aspartate, glutamate, histidine, lysine, Chapters 6 and 7) must be in a particular protonated or deprotonated state to bind the substrate and to catalyze the reaction; change the pH and some of those charges flip, hydrogen bonds and salt bridges that hold the active site in shape are lost, and activity falls. Pepsin’s site is built to work with its groups protonated; trypsin’s with them in their pH 8 forms. (b) The pancreas secretes bicarbonate that raises the pH to about 8, and pepsin is denatured and permanently inactivated; its job is finished. (c) Enzymes are proteins; above the body’s normal range the weak interactions that hold their tertiary structure begin to break, enzymes denature, and metabolism fails. Activity rises with temperature only up to the optimum and then collapses.

Cumulative Problem 11.5 (Chapters 1, 9, 10 and 11)

(a) Which vitamin is the source of NAD+, and what general role does NAD+ play? (b) Vitamin C is needed to hydroxylate proline in collagen; what are the symptoms when it is missing, and why? (c) Explain, by structure, why vitamins A, D, E and K can be stored in the body and taken in excess, while vitamin C and the B vitamins cannot.

Answer
(a) Niacin (vitamin B3). NAD+ is the coenzyme that accepts two hydrogens (as a hydride and a proton) in oxidation reactions, becoming NADH; alcohol dehydrogenase, lactate dehydrogenase and most of the oxidations of metabolism use it. (b) Scurvy: bleeding gums, loose teeth, poor wound healing and easy bruising. The enzyme that adds –OH groups to proline needs vitamin C; without the hydroxyl groups the collagen triple helix cannot form its hydrogen bonds (Chapter 10) and the collagen is weak, so gums, blood-vessel walls and ligaments fail. (c) Vitamins A, D, E and K are nonpolar, hydrocarbon-rich molecules that dissolve in fat (Chapters 1 and 9); they are stored in fat and the liver, so they accumulate and can reach toxic levels. Vitamin C and the B vitamins carry many –OH, –COOH or ionic groups, dissolve in water, and the excess is simply excreted in urine, which is why they must be eaten regularly.

Cumulative Problem 11.6 (Chapters 3, 6, 8, 10 and 11)

In the clinic. (a) Aspirin permanently inactivates cyclooxygenase by transferring its acetyl group to a serine –OH in the active site. What kind of bond forms, and why is the inhibition irreversible? (b) Penicillin inactivates the bacterial enzyme that cross-links cell walls by bonding covalently to a serine in its active site; why does it not harm human cells? (c) Fluoride in toothpaste inhibits enolase, an enzyme of glycolysis in S. mutans. How does that protect enamel?

Answer
(a) The acetyl group of aspirin (an ester, Chapter 6) is transferred to the serine, forming a new ester bond on the enzyme. A covalent bond does not come off by itself, so the enzyme is dead until the cell makes a new one; this is why one aspirin keeps a platelet from clotting for the platelet’s whole lifetime. (b) Human cells have no cell wall and no transpeptidase, so there is no target; the drug is selectively toxic to bacteria. (c) Enolase is a step in the pathway that turns sugar into lactic acid (Chapter 8, and Chapter 13 in detail). Inhibiting it slows acid production in plaque, so the enamel is exposed to less acid; fluoride also strengthens the enamel mineral itself.

Problem 11.54

Similarity: both bind the enzyme reversibly and lower its activity; both let go, so the inhibition ends when their concentration falls. Differences: (1) A competitive inhibitor binds the active site and resembles the substrate; an allosteric regulator binds a separate regulatory site and need not look like the substrate. (2) Competitive inhibition is overcome by more substrate; allosteric inhibition is not, because the regulator is not competing for the same site. Examples: a statin (competitive at HMG-CoA reductase) and ATP as a negative allosteric regulator of phosphofructokinase, or the end product of any feedback-controlled pathway.

Problem 11.55

The sample from the hot car will have little or no activity: 60 °C is well above the optimum of a human enzyme, the weak interactions were disrupted, the chain unfolded (denatured), and the active site no longer exists. Heat denaturation is not reversed by cooling.

Problem 11.58

Problem 11.59

C. Heavy-metal ions such as Pb2+ and Hg2+ have a strong affinity for sulfur; they bond to cysteine –SH groups in or near the active site, the shape changes, and the substrate can no longer enter. The bond is not undone by adding substrate or by dilution, so the inhibition is irreversible, and because the metal does not compete with the substrate it also counts as noncompetitive; the cell must make new enzyme. Lead ions do not resemble any substrate (A), they are not organic molecules (B), and they lower activity rather than raise it (D).

Problem 11.62

Glucosyltransferase moves the glucose unit of sucrose onto a growing glucan chain and releases fructose; the glucans are sticky polysaccharides that glue bacteria to the enamel surface and thicken the plaque biofilm, so acid is produced right against the tooth and saliva cannot buffer it away. Glucose alone can be fermented but cannot be used to build these glucans. The energy stored in sucrose's glycosidic bond is what pays for the transfer; the bacterium needs no ATP.

Problem 11.63

Thiamine (vitamin B1) is the precursor of thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP), the coenzyme of pyruvate dehydrogenase, which converts pyruvate from glycolysis into acetyl-CoA for the citric acid cycle. Alcohol use disorder depletes thiamine (poor diet, poor absorption). A glucose load drives glycolysis and produces pyruvate that cannot be processed without TPP, so the small remaining thiamine is consumed and the brain, which depends on glucose, is starved of energy; this can precipitate or worsen Wernicke encephalopathy. Giving thiamine first restores the coenzyme before the glucose arrives.

Problem 11.70

Specificity means an enzyme catalyzes only one reaction or one narrow set of reactions, because only a substrate with the right shape and pattern of charges fits its active site. Absolute specificity: one substrate only (urease acts only on urea). Group specificity: one type of substrate (hexokinase phosphorylates several hexoses). Linkage specificity: one type of bond (lipases hydrolyze ester bonds wherever they occur in a lipid). Enzymes are also specific for stereochemistry, acting on D-sugars and L-amino acids but not their mirror images.

Problem 11.71

Rate versus enzyme concentration (with excess substrate) is a straight line through the origin: twice the enzyme, twice the rate, because every added enzyme molecule finds plenty of substrate. Rate versus substrate concentration (fixed enzyme) rises steeply, then bends over and levels off at a maximum when all the active sites are occupied (saturation). Clinical enzyme assays rely on the first graph; the second shows why adding more substrate eventually stops helping.

Problem 11.72

In (1) and (4) the regulator binds reversibly and no covalent bond changes; in (2) and (3) a covalent bond is formed or broken.
