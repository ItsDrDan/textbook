Esters are neutral compounds, unlike the acids from which they are formed. In typical reactions, the alkoxy (OR′) group of an ester is replaced by another group. One such reaction is hydrolysis, literally “splitting with water.” The hydrolysis of esters is sped up by either an acid or a base, but the two do different jobs: the acid is a catalyst, and the base is a reactant that is used up.

Acidic hydrolysis is simply the reverse of esterification. The ester is heated with a large excess of water containing a strong-acid catalyst. Like esterification, the reaction is reversible and does not go to completion.

As a specific example, butyl acetate and water react to form acetic acid and 1-butanol. The reaction is reversible and does not go to completion.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Write an equation for the acidic hydrolysis of methyl butanoate and name the products.

Answer

CH3CH2CH2COOCH3 + H2O \(\rightleftharpoons\) CH3CH2CH2COOH + CH3OH, with H+ over the arrows. The products are butanoic acid and methanol.

When a base (such as sodium hydroxide [NaOH] or potassium hydroxide [KOH]) is used to hydrolyze an ester, the products are a carboxylate salt and an alcohol. Because soaps are prepared by the alkaline hydrolysis of fats and oils, alkaline hydrolysis of esters is called saponification (Latin sapon, meaning “soap,” and facere, meaning “to make”). In a saponification reaction, the base is a reactant, not simply a catalyst. The reaction goes to completion:

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Write an equation for the hydrolysis of methyl benzoate in a potassium hydroxide solution.

Solution

Find the C=O and cut the single bond between that carbon and the oxygen next to it, the same bond that breaks in acidic hydrolysis. Because the solution is basic, the acid that would form is neutralized at once by the KOH. The acid portion of the ester ends up as the salt of the acid (in this case, the potassium salt). The alcohol portion of the ester ends up as the free alcohol.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Write the equation for the hydrolysis of ethyl propanoate in a sodium hydroxide solution.

Answer

CH3CH2COOCH2CH3 + NaOH → CH3CH2COO−Na+ + CH3CH2OH. The products are sodium propanoate (a salt, because the base is used up) and ethanol.

Summary

Hydrolysis is a most important reaction of esters. Acidic hydrolysis of an ester gives a carboxylic acid and an alcohol. Basic hydrolysis of an ester gives a carboxylate salt and an alcohol.

Molecule in the Clinic: Aspirin

An old bottle of aspirin often smells like vinegar. Moisture slowly hydrolyzes the ester in aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid), and one of the products is acetic acid, the acid in vinegar.

Your turn: Identify the functional groups in aspirin and write the products of its hydrolysis.

Answer
Aspirin has a carboxylic acid and an ester on a benzene ring. Hydrolysis of the ester gives salicylic acid (2-hydroxybenzoic acid) and acetic acid.

Lecture Connection

Two ways to take an ester apart, and the lecture wants you to keep them separate: acid-catalyzed hydrolysis gives back the acid and the alcohol (reversible; H+ is a catalyst), while base hydrolysis, saponification, gives the carboxylate salt and the alcohol (goes to completion; NaOH is consumed). Both cut the same bond, the single bond between the acyl carbon and the oxygen.

Same bond, two outcomes

Hydrolysis is a chemical reaction in which water is used to break a covalent bond. Esters hydrolyze only very slowly in plain water, even boiling water; the reaction becomes much faster when the ester is heated in aqueous acid or base, or when an enzyme does the job. Both routes break the same bond, both give the alcohol unchanged, and in both the C=O side becomes the acid part. The differences matter, so compare them side by side.

Think of saponification as hydrolysis followed by an acid–base reaction: the carboxylic acid that forms is immediately neutralized by the base (Section 6.4). The word saponification comes from the Latin for soap, as the section text explains. Fats and oils are triesters of glycerol with three long-chain fatty acids; boiling them with NaOH gives glycerol plus the sodium salts of the fatty acids. If the acyl R group is a long carbon chain, the carboxylate anion is a soap molecule: an ionic head that dissolves in water and a long nonpolar tail that dissolves grease, so that in water the soap molecules cluster into micelles (pronounced my-SELLS) with the grease trapped inside. In the body, enzymes called esterases and lipases do the same job at pH 7.4 without strong acid or base.

Professor Tip

Treating H+ like NaOH is the most common error here. In acid-catalyzed hydrolysis the H+ is a catalyst and the product is the free acid; in saponification the hydroxide is a reactant that is used up, and the product is the carboxylate salt. Read the reagent over the arrow before you write the product.

Ester drugs come apart in the body

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Aspirin. Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid) is a two-functional-group molecule: a carboxylic acid on the ring and an ester made from the phenol –OH of salicylic acid and acetic acid. Old aspirin tablets that smell like vinegar have been hydrolyzed by moisture, releasing acetic acid; they should be discarded. In the stomach and blood the same ester is hydrolyzed quickly by esterases to salicylate, the carboxylate ion that actually circulates and is measured in a serum salicylate level (what happens to that acid at blood pH is Section 6.10). Hand hygiene. Soap is a carboxylate salt made by saponification. Its long nonpolar tail dissolves grease and its ionic head dissolves in water, which lets rinse water carry oils and microbes away.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Topical anesthetics. Benzocaine, the active ingredient in topical numbing gels used before injections and in oral pain gels, is an ester (the ethyl ester of PABA, 4-aminobenzoic acid). Ester-type anesthetics are hydrolyzed quickly by esterase enzymes in the blood, which is why they are short-acting, and one of the hydrolysis products, PABA, is behind most true allergies to this class. That is why patients are asked about anesthetic allergies, and why amide-type anesthetics (Chapter 7), which hydrolyze far more slowly, are used for injections.
