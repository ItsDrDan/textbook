We can write the structure of butane (C4H10) by stringing four carbon atoms in a row,

–C–C–C–C–

and then adding enough hydrogen atoms to give each carbon atom four bonds:

The compound butane has this structure, but there is another way to put 4 carbon atoms and 10 hydrogen atoms together. Place 3 of the carbon atoms in a row and then branch the fourth one off the middle carbon atom:

Now we add enough hydrogen atoms to give each carbon four bonds.

The result is a different compound with different properties, isobutane (IUPAC name 2-methylpropane). Its molecular formula is also C4H10, so it is an isomer of butane: same molecular formula, different structure (Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\)).

Notice that C4H10 is depicted with a bent chain in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\). The four-carbon chain may be bent in various ways because the groups can rotate freely about the C–C bonds. However, this rotation does not change the identity of the compound. It is important to realize that bending a chain does not change the identity of the compound; all of the following represent the same compound, butane:

The structure of isobutane shows a continuous chain of three carbon atoms only, with the fourth attached as a branch off the middle carbon atom of the continuous chain, which is different from the structures of butane (compare the two structures in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\)).

Unlike C4H10, the compounds methane (CH4), ethane (C2H6), and propane (C3H8) do not exist in isomeric forms because there is only one way to arrange the atoms in each formula so that each carbon atom has four bonds.

Next beyond C4H10 in the homologous series is C5H12, which has three isomers, shown in the figure below. (The table in Section 2.2 has a column identifying the number of possible isomers for the first 10 straight-chain alkanes.) The compound on the left of the figure is pentane because it has all five carbon atoms in a continuous chain. The compound in the middle is isopentane (IUPAC name 2-methylbutane); like isobutane, it has one CH3 branch off the second carbon atom of the continuous chain. The compound at the far right, discovered after the other two, was named neopentane (from the Greek neos, meaning “new”); its IUPAC name is 2,2-dimethylpropane. Although all three have the same molecular formula, they have different properties, including boiling points: pentane, 36.1°C; isopentane, 27.7°C; and neopentane, 9.5°C.

A continuous (unbranched) chain of carbon atoms is often called a straight chain even though the tetrahedral arrangement about each carbon gives it a zigzag shape. Straight-chain alkanes are sometimes called normal alkanes, and their names are given the prefix n-. For example, butane is called n-butane. You will hear n-butane and n-pentane in class to stress “normal, unbranched”; either form is accepted, but the n- is not part of the IUPAC name.

Lecture Connection

Lecture makes two distinctions here that students mix up on exams: a conformation (the same molecule twisted a different way) is not an isomer, and constitutional isomers come in two kinds you must name, skeletal and positional.

Conformations: what rotation does and does not change

  • Single bonds rotate freely, so one molecule can twist into many conformations. Different conformations of the same molecule are not isomers.

What rotation does and does not change. Molecules are 3-D and constantly in motion. Picture ethane as two CH3 groups joined by one C–C bond: spin one CH3 like a propeller and you get a new conformation, but no bond breaks and the atoms stay connected in the same order. The same is true of butane twisting about its C2–C3 bond: a "stretched out" zig-zag and a "folded" shape are the same compound, just drawn from another view. Double and triple bonds, by contrast, cannot rotate (Chapter 3). To turn one isomer into another you must break and remake a bond; to turn one conformation into another you only rotate.

Two kinds of constitutional isomer

Constitutional (structural) isomers share a molecular formula but connect their atoms in a different order. For alkanes and haloalkanes there are two kinds you must recognize:

  • Skeletal isomers differ in the carbon skeleton itself: the chain is longer or shorter, with branches in different places. Pentane, 2-methylbutane, and 2,2-dimethylpropane are the three skeletal isomers of C5H12 (part (a) of the figure below).
  • Positional isomers keep the same skeleton but put a substituent on a different carbon. 1-Chloropropane and 2-chloropropane are positional isomers of C3H7Cl (part (b) of the figure below); so are 1,1-dichloroethane and 1,2-dichloroethane.

Professor Tip

Same molecule or constitutional isomer? Find the terminal CH3 groups and count the carbons in between. If both drawings have the same longest chain with the same branches in the same places (that is, the same IUPAC name), they are the same molecule drawn from another view. To draw all the isomers of a formula, work from the longest chain down: draw the straight chain, then shorten it by one carbon and move the leftover methyl along the chain (never onto an end carbon), then shorten it again and place two methyls, and so on. Name each one as you go; a repeat name means a repeat structure.

Isomers are different compounds with different boiling points, and their names differ either in the parent or the substituent list (skeletal) or only in a locant, the number that tells where a group is attached (positional). Two drawings that give the same IUPAC name are the same compound, however differently they are twisted on the page; that is a conformation, not an isomer. In Chapter 4 you will add a third kind, functional group isomers, such as an alcohol and an ether with the same formula.

Professor Tip

Recognize the common names isobutane, isopentane, and neopentane when you see them on a label, but on exams write the IUPAC names, 2-methylpropane, 2-methylbutane, and 2,2-dimethylpropane, which tell you the structure directly (Section 2.5).

Example 1: Drawing every isomer of a formula

Problem. Draw and name all the constitutional isomers of C6H14.

Solution. Start with the longest possible chain and shorten it step by step.

  1. Six-carbon chain: hexane. (1 isomer)
  2. Five-carbon chain + one methyl. The methyl cannot go on C1 or C5 (that would just be hexane again), and C4 is the same as C2 numbered from the other end. So: 2-methylpentane and 3-methylpentane. (2 isomers)
  3. Four-carbon chain + two methyls. Both on C2 (2,2-dimethylbutane) or one on C2 and one on C3 (2,3-dimethylbutane). "2,4-dimethylbutane" is impossible because C4 is an end carbon. (2 isomers)
  4. Three-carbon chain + three methyls: impossible; the middle carbon can hold only two branches, and any more would lengthen the chain.

Total: five isomers, shown below.

Check. Each drawing has six carbons and, by CnH2n+2, fourteen hydrogens. The five names are all different, so no structure has been drawn twice. "2-ethylbutane" and "3-methylpentane" would describe the same molecule, which is why you name each one before counting it. ✓

Summary

  • Isomers have the same molecular formula but different structures; butane and 2-methylpropane are both C4H10.
  • Skeletal isomers differ in the carbon chain (straight versus branched); positional isomers have the same chain with a group in a different place.
  • Rotation about single bonds gives conformations of the same molecule, not new isomers.
  • Recognize common names (isobutane, isopentane, neopentane), but write IUPAC names on exams.
