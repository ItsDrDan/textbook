Learning Objectives

  • Sort a lipid into the hydrolyzable or non-hydrolyzable family and name its class (wax, triacylglycerol, glycerophospholipid, sphingolipid, steroid, fat-soluble vitamin, eicosanoid).
  • Relate fatty acid structure (chain length, saturation, cis double bonds) to melting point, and read the shorthand notation (for example 18:2, omega-6).
  • Locate α, β, and omega (ω) positions on a fatty acid and name the essential fatty acids.
  • Draw a triacylglycerol and predict the products of hydrogenation, hydrolysis, and saponification; explain oxidation (rancidity) and trans fats.
  • Distinguish glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids, steroids, fat-soluble vitamins, and eicosanoids, and give one function of each.
  • Describe the fluid-mosaic lipid bilayer and classify how a molecule crosses it (diffusion, facilitated transport, active transport).

Lipids are the one family of biomolecules defined by a physical property rather than a functional group: a lipid is a biological compound that does not dissolve in water but does dissolve in nonpolar solvents. That single fact explains most of this chapter. It is why fat floats on soup, why cell membranes form, why some vitamins can build up to toxic levels, and why a lipid panel needs a fasting blood draw. The functional groups you already know (esters, amides, alcohols, alkenes) do all of the chemistry. This guide is the short version; the teaching, figures, worked examples, and clinical boxes behind each Key Idea sit on the section pages, in the Lecture Connection block after each section's text.

Chapter Summary

The big ideas of the chapter, one paragraph each. Each paragraph ends with the section where it is taught in full.

1. Fatty acids

Long unbranched carboxylic acids, usually an even number of carbons (12–20); the tail is nonpolar, only the COOH is polar. Saturated (no C=C) chains pack tightly and melt high (solids); each cis C=C kinks the chain, cuts dispersion-force contact, and lowers the melting point (oils); a trans C=C does not kink, so it melts like a saturated chain. Shorthand carbons:double bonds (stearic 18:0, oleic 18:1, linoleic 18:2); α = C2, β = C3, ω = the CH3 carbon; omega number = total carbons − position of the last C=C (oleic: 18 − 9 = omega-9). Linoleic (omega-6) and α-linolenic (omega-3) are essential. Details, table, and examples: Section 9.1.

2. Triacylglycerols (fats and oils)

Triesters of glycerol (HOCH2–CHOH–CH2OH, know it from memory) and three fatty acids, made by condensation with loss of 3 H2O. Fat = solid (mostly saturated chains); oil = liquid (mostly cis-unsaturated). Four reactions: hydrogenation (H2, Ni) turns oils into fats and can create trans fats; hydrolysis (acid or lipase) gives glycerol + 3 fatty acids; saponification (NaOH or KOH, heat) gives glycerol + 3 fatty acid salts (soap, which cleans by forming micelles); oxidation at the C=C bonds causes rancidity, slowed by antioxidants such as vitamin E. Details, figures, and examples: Section 9.2.

3. Membrane lipids, steroids, and eicosanoids

Classify by backbone. Glycerophospholipids: glycerol + 2 fatty acids + a phosphodiester to an ionized amino alcohol (lecithin with choline, cephalin with ethanolamine); charged head, two nonpolar tails. Sphingolipids: sphingosine backbone, one fatty acid as an amide; sphingomyelin (phosphate–choline head) builds the myelin sheath, a cerebroside carries a sugar. Steroids: four fused rings, no fatty acids. Eicosanoids: 20-carbon local signals from arachidonic acid. Details: Sections 9.3, 9.4, 9.6, 9.1.

4. Hydrolyzable vs. non-hydrolyzable lipids

Hydrolyzable lipids contain an ester (or amide) that water can split: waxes, triacylglycerols, glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids, all built from fatty acids. Non-hydrolyzable lipids have no such link: steroids, eicosanoids, fat-soluble vitamins. The textbook's "saponifiable/nonsaponifiable" means the same thing. Classification map: Section 9.1.

5. Waxes

One ester of a long-chain fatty acid and a long-chain alcohol: two nonpolar tails, water-repellent, firm (beeswax, carnauba, lanolin). Hydrolysis gives one acid and one alcohol, which is how you tell a wax from a fat. Details and example: Section 9.1.

6. Oxidation of fats (rancidity)

O2 attacks the CH2 between double bonds and cuts the chain into volatile aldehydes and acids (oxidative rancidity); moisture and lipases release free fatty acids (hydrolytic rancidity). The more polyunsaturated, the faster it spoils; antioxidant phenols (vitamin E, BHT) and hydrogenation slow it. Details: Section 9.2.

7. More about steroids

Three six-membered rings and one five-membered ring, 17 carbons; steroids differ only in the groups on the frame. Cholesterol (one OH on C3) is a membrane fluidity buffer and the precursor of bile salts (amphipathic emulsifiers of dietary fat; excess crystallizes as gallstones), the sex hormones (estrogens, progestins, androgens), the adrenal corticosteroids (cortisol, aldosterone; prednisone is synthetic), and vitamin D. Details and hormone table: Section 9.6.

8. Cell membranes and transport

Phospholipids in water form a bilayer, heads out and tails in; the fluid-mosaic model adds drifting cholesterol and embedded proteins. Three ways across: diffusion (small nonpolar molecules, steroids; no protein, no ATP, down the gradient), facilitated transport (glucose, Cl−, HCO3− through a channel or carrier; no ATP), active transport (Na+, K+, Ca2+ against the gradient by a pump; ATP required). Details, table, and figure: Section 9.5.

9. Fat-soluble vitamins

A, D, E, and K are nonpolar: absorbed with fat, carried in lipoproteins, stored in liver and fat, so deficiency is slow and excess is toxic (hypervitaminosis). A (retinol) for vision, D (cholecalciferol, a modified steroid made from cholesterol by UV light) for Ca2+ absorption and bone, E (α-tocopherol) as a membrane antioxidant, K (phylloquinone) for clotting factors. Table: Section 9.6.

10. Eicosanoids

Twenty-carbon local hormones made on demand from arachidonic acid (20:4): prostaglandins (COX; five-membered carbon ring; pain, fever, inflammation, stomach protection, labor), thromboxanes (COX in platelets; six-membered ring with O; clotting), leukotrienes (lipoxygenase; no ring, conjugated C=C; asthma). NSAIDs block COX; corticosteroids block the release of arachidonic acid, so they lower all three. Details and figures: Section 9.1.

11. Recognizing a lipid from its structure

Find the backbone and the links: one ester joining two chains = wax; glycerol with three esters = triacylglycerol; glycerol with two esters and a phosphodiester = glycerophospholipid; sphingosine with an amide = sphingolipid; four fused rings = steroid; a 20-carbon acid with a ring (or conjugated C=C) = eicosanoid. Then decide cis or trans at each C=C and label α, β, and ω. Full table: Section 9.6.

Worked Examples

These five are the exam-style tasks. More worked examples are on the section pages (9.1, 9.2, 9.3).

Example 1: Saponification

Problem. What forms when glyceryl tripalmitate (tripalmitin) is heated with excess NaOH?

Solution

All three ester bonds are hydrolyzed in base: glycerol + 3 sodium palmitate (CH3(CH2)14COO−Na+), which is soap.

Check. Three esters need three OH−; the products are a salt (carboxylate), not a free acid, because the solution is basic.

Example 2: Identify a fatty acid from its notation and classify it

Problem. A fish-oil supplement lists a fatty acid as 20:5 with double bonds at C5, C8, C11, C14, and C17. Name it, give its omega class, say whether it is saturated or unsaturated, and predict whether it is a solid or a liquid at room temperature.

Solution

Twenty carbons and five double bonds is EPA (eicosapentaenoic acid). The last double bond begins at C17, so the omega number is 20 − 17 = 3: omega-3. Five C=C makes it polyunsaturated. Five cis kinks in a 20-carbon chain prevent any close packing, so it is a liquid (oil) at room temperature; the table in Section 9.1 lists a melting point near −54 °C.

Check. The double bonds are spaced three carbons apart (5, 8, 11, 14, 17), the usual pattern for polyunsaturated fatty acids; counting from C20 as ω1, the third carbon is C18, the first carbon of the C17=C18 double bond, confirming omega-3. Fish that live in cold water need membrane lipids that stay fluid, which fits a very low melting point.

Example 3: Rank by melting point

Problem. Rank these fatty acids from lowest to highest melting point: palmitic acid (16:0), α-linolenic acid (18:3), palmitoleic acid (16:1), lauric acid (12:0).

Solution

Sort by kinks first, then by length. Most kinks: α-linolenic (three cis C=C) is lowest. Next: palmitoleic (one cis C=C). The two saturated acids come last, and the shorter chain melts lower: lauric (12 carbons) below palmitic (16 carbons).

α-linolenic (18:3) < palmitoleic (16:1) < lauric (12:0) < palmitic (16:0)

Check. Actual values: about −11, 0, 44, and 63 °C. Notice that α-linolenic acid has the longest chain yet the lowest melting point: three cis double bonds outweigh six extra carbons.

Example 4: Classify the transport

Problem. For each case, name the transport type and justify it in one sentence. (a) After a meal, glucose moves from the blood into a muscle cell through a GLUT4 carrier that insulin has brought to the surface. (b) A steroid hormone such as cortisol enters a liver cell. (c) A kidney tubule cell keeps its internal Na+ low even though Na+ is high in the fluid around it.

Solution

(a) Facilitated transport (facilitated diffusion). Glucose is polar and needs a carrier, but it is moving from high (blood) to low (cell), so no ATP is used; insulin only supplies the door. (b) Diffusion (simple, passive). Cortisol is a nonpolar steroid and dissolves through the bilayer on its own, down its gradient. (c) Active transport. Keeping Na+ low inside against a steady inward leak means pumping it out against its gradient, which only the ATP-driven Na+/K+-ATPase can do.

Check. Ask two questions in order: does it need a protein (polar or charged = yes), and does it go against the gradient (yes = ATP)? Case (a) answers yes/no, (b) no/no, (c) yes/yes, which are the three rows of the table in Section 9.5.

Example 5: Lipase on a mixed triacylglycerol

Problem. Inside a fat cell, lipases completely hydrolyze a triacylglycerol that carries stearic acid on C1 and C3 of glycerol and oleic acid on C2. Write the products as condensed structures and state whether this reaction is a condensation or a hydrolysis.

Solution. Hydrolysis: water splits each of the three ester bonds, so 3 H2O are consumed. The alcohol part of every ester is glycerol, so one glycerol forms, HOCH2CH(OH)CH2OH. The acid parts come off as free fatty acids (lipase works at body pH, not in strong base, so no salts form): 2 stearic acid, CH3(CH2)16COOH, and 1 oleic acid, CH3(CH2)7CH=CH(CH2)7COOH. The cis double bond of oleic acid is untouched.

Check. 1 fat + 3 H2O → 1 glycerol + 3 fatty acids. Had the question said NaOH and heat instead of lipase, the same cut would have been made but the acids would have been written as sodium salts (saponification: hydrolysis followed by neutralization). Digestion in the small intestine is different: pancreatic lipase removes only the two outer fatty acids (from C1 and C3) and leaves a monoacylglycerol (Chapter 15).

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Assuming more carbons always beats double bonds. Compare chain lengths and the number of cis double bonds.
  • Counting omega positions from the COOH end. Omega is counted from the CH3 end.
  • Forgetting that saponification gives fatty acid salts, not fatty acids.
  • Thinking cholesterol is only harmful. It is required for membranes and to make hormones, bile salts, and vitamin D.
  • Treating a trans fatty acid like a cis one. A trans C=C does not bend the chain, so a trans fat packs and melts like a saturated fat; "unsaturated" does not automatically mean "low melting."
  • Confusing a wax with a fat. Both are esters, but a wax has one ester (fatty acid + long-chain alcohol) and a fat has three (fatty acids + glycerol). Hydrolyzing a wax gives one acid and one alcohol; hydrolyzing a fat gives three acids and glycerol.
  • Calling facilitated transport "active" because it uses a protein. Energy, not the protein, is the test: if the solute moves down its gradient, no ATP is spent, and it is facilitated transport (facilitated diffusion).
  • Mixing up the sphingolipid bond. The fatty acid in sphingomyelin is attached by an amide bond, not an ester, because sphingosine has an amine; it is still hydrolyzable.
  • Drawing glycerol with the wrong number of carbons or OH groups. It is three carbons, three OH groups, and every triacylglycerol, fat, oil, and glycerophospholipid is built on it.
  • Writing free fatty acids as the products of saponification, or fatty acid salts as the products of lipase or acid hydrolysis. Base gives salts; acid or enzyme gives the free acids.
  • Calling a prostaglandin a steroid because both have rings. A steroid has four fused rings (three six-membered, one five-membered) and no long fatty-acid chain; a prostaglandin has one five-membered ring on a 20-carbon chain that ends in COOH.

Clinical Update: Rosa’s Story, Part 9 of 15

The lipid panel

Rosa’s nurse practitioner goes over her lipid panel: LDL 142 mg/dL, HDL 38 mg/dL and triglycerides 230 mg/dL. High triglycerides and low HDL are common in type 2 diabetes. She keeps Rosa on atorvastatin, which lowers the liver’s production of cholesterol, and suggests eating fish such as salmon or sardines twice a week for their omega-3 fatty acids.

Rosa asks why cholesterol is called “bad” when the nurse also said her body needs it.

Case Question 9.1

What is a triglyceride, chemically?

Answer
A triacylglycerol: glycerol joined by three ester bonds to three fatty acids.

Case Question 9.2

What does “omega-3” tell you about a fatty acid’s structure?

Answer
Counting from the methyl (omega) end of the chain, the first C=C double bond starts at carbon 3. Omega-3 fatty acids are polyunsaturated, with cis double bonds.

Case Question 9.3

Cholesterol is a steroid. Name two things the body uses it for, and explain the difference between LDL and HDL.

Answer
Cholesterol is part of every cell membrane and is the starting material for bile salts, steroid hormones and vitamin D. LDL carries cholesterol out to the tissues, and high LDL is linked to plaque in arteries; HDL carries cholesterol back to the liver, so higher HDL is protective.

Where this chemistry is taught: Section 9.1, Section 9.2, Section 9.6. Rosa’s story continues at the start of Chapter 10.

Practice

Exercise 1

Which has the higher melting point: a triacylglycerol made from three stearic acids or one made from three linoleic acids?

Answer
Tristearin (all saturated, straight chains pack tightly). Trilinolein is an oil.

Exercise 2

What is the product of completely hydrogenating triolein?

Answer
Tristearin (the oleic chains, 18:1, become stearic chains, 18:0)

Exercise 3

Which can diffuse directly through a lipid bilayer: CO2, Na+, glucose?

Answer
Only CO2. Na+ and glucose are polar or charged and need protein transporters.

Exercise 4

Which lipid class do prostaglandins belong to, and what drug class blocks their synthesis?

Answer
Eicosanoids; NSAIDs (aspirin, ibuprofen, naproxen) block the COX enzyme.

Exercise 5

Classify as hydrolyzable or non-hydrolyzable: beeswax, cholesterol, sphingomyelin, prostaglandin E2.

Answer
Hydrolyzable: beeswax, sphingomyelin. Non-hydrolyzable: cholesterol, prostaglandin E2.

Exercise 6

A fatty acid is listed as 22:1 with its double bond at C13. Give its omega class and predict whether it melts above or below oleic acid (18:1, 13 °C).

Answer
22 − 13 = 9, so omega-9. One cis kink in both, but the 22-carbon chain has more dispersion-force contact, so it melts higher than oleic acid (erucic acid, about 33 °C).

Exercise 7

Which fat-soluble vitamin is (a) a modified steroid made in the skin, (b) a membrane antioxidant, (c) required for clotting factors, (d) the source of the light-absorbing retinal in the eye?

Answer
(a) vitamin D; (b) vitamin E; (c) vitamin K; (d) vitamin A.

Exercise 8

A patient takes a corticosteroid inhaler and a daily low-dose aspirin. Which eicosanoid family does each drug lower, and by acting on what step?

Answer
Aspirin blocks COX, lowering prostaglandins and thromboxanes (the antiplatelet effect comes from thromboxane). The corticosteroid acts earlier, suppressing release of arachidonic acid, so it lowers prostaglandins, thromboxanes, and leukotrienes; the leukotriene effect is what helps asthma.
