Alkenes are valued mainly for addition reactions, in which one of the bonds in the double bond is broken. Each of the carbon atoms in the bond can then attach another atom or group while remaining joined to each other by a single bond. Perhaps the simplest addition reaction is hydrogenation—a reaction with hydrogen (H2) in the presence of a metal catalyst such as nickel (Ni), platinum (Pt), or palladium (Pd).

The product is an alkane having the same carbon skeleton as the alkene.

Alkenes also readily undergo halogenation—the addition of halogens. Indeed, the reaction with bromine (Br2) can be used to test for alkenes. Bromine solutions are brownish red. When we add a Br2 solution to an alkene, the color of the solution disappears because the alkene reacts with the bromine:

Another important addition reaction is that between an alkene and water to form an alcohol. This reaction, called hydration, requires a catalyst—usually a strong acid, such as sulfuric acid (H2SO4):

The hydration reaction is discussed later, where we deal with this reaction in the synthesis of alcohols.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Write the equation for the reaction between CH3CH=CHCH3 and each substance.

  1. H2 (Ni catalyst)
  2. Br2
  3. H2O (H2SO4 catalyst)

Solution

In each reaction the C=C opens to a C–C single bond, and one piece of the reagent bonds to each of the two former double-bond carbons. 2-Butene is symmetrical, so in (c) it does not matter which carbon gets the OH. Check each product: no C=C is left, and it holds every atom of both reactants.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Write the equation for each reaction.

  1. CH3CH2CH=CH2 with H2 (Ni catalyst)
  2. CH3CH=CH2 with Cl2
  3. CH3CH2CH=CHCH2CH3 with H2O (H2SO4 catalyst)
Answer
(a) CH3CH2CH=CH2 + H2 → CH3CH2CH2CH3 (butane). (b) CH3CH=CH2 + Cl2 → CH3CHClCH2Cl (1,2-dichloropropane). (c) CH3CH2CH=CHCH2CH3 + H2O → CH3CH2CH(OH)CH2CH2CH3 (3-hexanol). The alkene is symmetric, so the OH can go on either double-bond carbon and gives the same product.

Valley Connection: The Alkene That Ripens Fruit

The simplest alkene, ethylene (C2H4, H2C=CH2), is made by plants themselves. It acts as a natural growth regulator (a plant hormone), and it works at amazingly low levels, in the parts-per-million and even parts-per-billion range. In “climacteric” fruits such as pears, avocados, bananas, and tomatoes, ethylene sets off ripening.1

In the packing house. Valley fruit handlers sometimes add ethylene and sometimes fight to keep it out. Kiwifruit, which can be grown in most parts of the Sacramento and San Joaquin Valleys, is extremely sensitive: a season-long level above 5 parts per billion shortens its storage life, so storage rooms use only battery-powered forklifts and keep kiwifruit away from other fruit that gives off ethylene.2 When packers want kiwifruit ready to eat, a ripening room can hold freshly harvested fruit in about 100 ppm ethylene for 12 hours. UC advisors at the Kearney Agricultural Center in Parlier recommend placing that room far from long-term storage so the gas does not drift over.3 Another tool is 1-methylcyclopropene (1-MCP), a small cyclic alkene that blocks the fruit’s ethylene receptors; in a UC Davis study it slowed softening and extended kiwifruit shelf life.4

Not every crop cares as much. In UC cold-storage trials, constant ethylene did not change decay in stone fruits or table grapes, and most peach, plum, and nectarine quality traits were unaffected (apricots did soften). The researchers saw no general commercial benefit from scrubbing ethylene out of those storage rooms.5

The chemistry link. Ethylene’s reactive spot is its C=C double bond. The same electron-rich double bond that adds H2, Br2, or water in this section can also be attacked by oxidizers. That is how ethylene-absorbing sachets of potassium permanganate and ozone generators, both tested in Valley trials, are meant to destroy ethylene.3

Think about it: A potassium permanganate sachet removes ethylene from the air around fruit. Would it also remove ethane (C2H6)? Explain using the structures.

Answer
No. Permanganate reacts with ethylene by attacking its C=C double bond. Ethane is a saturated alkane with only C–C and C–H single bonds, so it has no double bond to react. Like other alkanes, it is quite unreactive under these mild conditions.

Key Takeaway

  • Alkenes undergo addition reactions, adding such substances as hydrogen, bromine, and water across the carbon-to-carbon double bond.

Lecture Connection

The three additions above (H2, Br2, H2O) have a fourth partner, hydrohalogenation (H–Cl, H–Br, H–I). This block covers all four, adds Markovnikov's rule for deciding where the pieces go, and pairs every addition with its reverse, an elimination. The picture to keep in your head: alkenes are the reactants, and we "open the double doors."

Addition: open the double doors

Professor Tip

Alkenes are the reactants, and we "open the double doors." Picture the double bond as a pair of doors: addition swings them open, one piece of the reagent goes on each carbon, and the C=C becomes a C–C single bond. Two reactants become one product.

Addition reactions of alkenes
ReactionReagentAddsProduct type
HydrogenationH2 with a Pt, Pd, or Ni catalyst (the metal surface binds both the alkene and H2, which speeds up the reaction)H and HAlkane
HalogenationCl2 or Br2; room temperature, no catalystX and XDihaloalkane
HydrohalogenationHCl, HBr, HI; no catalystH and XHaloalkane
HydrationH2O with a strong acid (H+) catalystH and OHAlcohol

In the first two reactions the two added pieces are identical (H and H, or X and X), so it does not matter which carbon gets which. In hydrohalogenation and hydration the two pieces are different, so with an unsymmetrical alkene you have to decide where each one goes.

Because Br2 is red-orange and the dibromoalkane it forms is colorless, the disappearance of bromine color is a qualitative test for an alkene (In the Lab, Section 3.7).

Markovnikov's rule (for HX and H2O added to an unsymmetrical alkene): the H of H–X bonds to the less substituted double-bond carbon, the one that already has more H's. The X or OH goes to the more substituted carbon, the one with more carbon groups. In theory an unsymmetrical alkene could give two products; in practice one direction wins, so the reaction is called regioselective.

Professor Tip

"Hydrogen wants to make the most friends." The new H joins the carbon that already has the most hydrogens.

A symmetrical alkene, such as 2-butene or cyclohexene, has the same groups on both double-bond carbons, so both ways of adding H–X give the same product and no rule is needed. If the two double-bond carbons carry the same number of hydrogens but different groups (2-pentene), Markovnikov's rule cannot choose and both products form in roughly equal amounts. An alkyne adds twice: 2-butyne + 2 H2 (Pt) gives butane.

Alkenes and alkynes also undergo combustion, just like alkanes, and the products are the same: C2H4 + 3 O2 → 2 CO2 + 2 H2O. Combustion is the one reaction that does not care whether a hydrocarbon is saturated, unsaturated, or aromatic.

Professor Tip

You must predict the major product of H–X and H–OH addition to an unsymmetrical alkene, and recognize that no rule is needed when the two added pieces are identical (H2, X2) or when the alkene is symmetrical. For hydrogenation, Ni, Pt, or Pd is an acceptable catalyst.

Elimination: the reverse of addition

Elimination reactions are the inverse of addition: one reactant loses two pieces from neighboring carbons, a double bond forms, and a small molecule is the by-product. Each addition has a matching elimination, named with de-: dehydrogenation (−H2), dehalogenation (−X2), dehydrohalogenation (−HX), and dehydration (−H2O). Dehydration of an alcohol needs a strong acid catalyst and heat, and you will run it in Chapter 4.

When the starting material is unsymmetrical, two different alkenes can form. Zaitsev's rule (also spelled Saytzeff) says the alkene formed in the greatest amount is the one made by removing the hydrogen from the neighboring carbon that has the fewest hydrogens. The major product therefore has the most alkyl groups bonded to the C=C (the more substituted alkene). Notice that this is the opposite of Markovnikov's rule: in addition, H goes to the carbon with the most H's; in elimination, H is taken from the carbon with the fewest.

Professor Tip

Think predator versus prey. In an elimination, the "lonely" hydrogen on the carbon with the fewest hydrogens is the easiest prey; remove it together with the functional group (OH, X, or a second H) from the next-door carbon, and the double bond forms between those two carbons. The more substituted alkene wins.

Example 1: When does Markovnikov's rule decide the product?

Problem. Give the major organic product of HBr addition to (a) 2-butene, (b) 1-butene, and (c) 2-pentene.

Solution.

  • (a) 2-Butene, CH3CH=CHCH3, is symmetrical: both double-bond carbons carry one H and one CH3. Whichever carbon gets the Br, the product is the same: 2-bromobutane. No rule needed.
  • (b) 1-Butene, CH2=CHCH2CH3, is unsymmetrical: C1 has two H's, C2 has one. Markovnikov: H to C1, Br to C2. Major product 2-bromobutane (1-bromobutane is minor).
  • (c) 2-Pentene, CH3CH=CHCH2CH3, is unsymmetrical, but each double-bond carbon has exactly one H. Markovnikov's rule cannot choose, so you get a mixture of 2-bromopentane and 3-bromopentane in roughly equal amounts.

Check. Count the hydrogens on the two C=C carbons. A tie means either both products are identical (symmetrical alkene) or you get a mixture (unsymmetrical alkene with a tie). Only when one carbon clearly has more H's does Markovnikov's rule pick a single major product.

Example 2: Dehydration and Zaitsev's rule

Problem. 3-Methyl-2-butanol, CH3CH(OH)CH(CH3)CH3, is heated with a strong acid. Name the elimination reaction, draw both possible alkenes, and predict the major product.

Solution. Heating an alcohol with acid removes H and OH from neighboring carbons: dehydration, with water as the by-product. The OH is on C2, so the H can come from C1 or from C3.

  • Remove H from C1 (a CH3, three hydrogens): CH2=CHCH(CH3)CH3, 3-methyl-1-butene. Its double bond carries one carbon group.
  • Remove H from C3 (a CH, only one hydrogen): CH3CH=C(CH3)CH3, 2-methyl-2-butene. Its double bond carries three carbon groups.

Zaitsev: take the lonely hydrogen from the carbon with the fewest hydrogens (C3). The major product is 2-methyl-2-butene, the more substituted alkene; 3-methyl-1-butene is the minor product.

Check. Both products are C5H10, the alcohol C5H12O minus H2O. Count the carbon groups on each C=C: three beats one. And notice the mirror image of Markovnikov's rule: hydrating 2-methyl-2-butene would put the OH on the carbon that carries the two methyls, giving 2-methyl-2-butanol, a different alcohol from the one we started with. Addition and elimination are reverse types of reaction, but they do not always retrace the same path. ✓

Problem. (a) Draw the line structure of 1-butene. (b) Classify carbon 3 as 1°, 2°, 3°, or 4°. (c) Write the name and structure of the product of its hydration (H2O, H+). (d) Name the functional group of the product.

(b) C3 is bonded to C2 and C4: two carbon neighbors, so it is secondary (2°). (c) Open the double doors. C1 (=CH2) has two H's and C2 (=CH–) has one, so the new H goes to C1 and the OH to C2: CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3, 2-butanol. (d) An alcohol (a 2° alcohol, since the carbon carrying the OH has two carbon neighbors).

Check. Product formula C4H10O = alkene C4H8 + H2O. Dehydrating 2-butanol gives mostly 2-butene (Zaitsev, as in Example 2), not 1-butene, so addition and elimination are reverse reaction types but do not always retrace the same path. ✓
