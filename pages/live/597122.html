Learning Objectives

  • Explain why the boiling points of primary and secondary amines are higher than those of alkanes or ethers of similar molar mass but are lower than those of alcohols.
  • Compare the boiling points of tertiary amines with alcohols, alkanes, and ethers of similar molar mass.
  • Compare the solubilities in water of amines of five or fewer carbon atoms with the solubilities of comparable alkanes and alcohols in water.

Primary and secondary amines have hydrogen atoms bonded to a nitrogen atom and are therefore capable of hydrogen bonding (part (a) of Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\)), although not as strongly as alcohol molecules (which have hydrogen atoms bonded to an oxygen atom, which is more electronegative than nitrogen). These amines boil at higher temperatures than alkanes but at lower temperatures than alcohols of comparable molar mass. For example, compare the boiling point of methylamine (CH3NH2; −6°C) with those of ethane (CH3CH3; −89°C) and methanol (CH3OH; 65°C). Tertiary amines have no hydrogen atom bonded to the nitrogen atom and so cannot participate in intermolecular hydrogen bonding. They have boiling points comparable to those of ethers (Table \(\PageIndex{1}\)).

All three classes of amines can engage in hydrogen bonding with water (Figure \(\PageIndex{1b}\)). Amines of low molar mass are quite soluble in water; the borderline of solubility in water is at five or six carbon atoms.

To Your Health: Amines in Death and Life

Amines have “interesting” odors. The simple ones smell very much like ammonia. Higher aliphatic amines smell like decaying fish. Or perhaps we should put it the other way around: Decaying fish give off odorous amines. The stench of rotting fish is due in part to two diamines: putrescine and cadaverine. They arise from the decarboxylation of ornithine and lysine, respectively, amino acids that are found in animal cells.

\[\underset{\text{putrescine}}{\ce{H2N(CH2)4NH2}} \qquad \underset{\text{cadaverine}}{\ce{H2N(CH2)5NH2}} \nonumber \]

Aromatic amines generally are quite toxic. They are readily absorbed through the skin, and workers must exercise caution when handling these compounds. Several aromatic amines, including β-naphthylamine, are potent carcinogens.

Key Takeaways

  • Primary and secondary amines have higher boiling points than those of alkanes or ethers of similar molar mass because they can engage in intermolecular hydrogen bonding. Their boiling points are lower than those of alcohols because alcohol molecules have hydrogen atoms bonded to an oxygen atom, which is more electronegative.
  • The boiling points of tertiary amines, which cannot engage in hydrogen bonding because they have no hydrogen atom on the nitrogen atom, are comparable to those of ethers of similar molar mass and a little higher than those of alkanes.
  • Because all three classes of amines can engage in hydrogen bonding with water, amines of low molar mass are quite soluble in water.

Lecture Connection

In lecture we explain every physical property of an amine with the same three questions we asked for alcohols and ethers: what is the shape, is it polar, and can it hydrogen bond with itself? Answer those and the boiling-point and solubility rankings follow.

Shape, polarity, and intermolecular forces

  • Shape and polarity. The nitrogen has four electron groups (three bonds and the lone pair): tetrahedral electron-group geometry, trigonal pyramidal molecular geometry, bond angles about 107° (a little under the ideal 109.5°). The N–H bond is polar; the C–N bond is polar too, but only slightly. So every amine is a polar molecule.
  • Intermolecular forces (IMFs) between amine molecules: dispersion (every molecule has these), dipole–dipole (the molecule is polar), and hydrogen bonding, but only for 1° and 2° amines, which have an N–H. N–H hydrogen bonds are weaker than O–H hydrogen bonds because N is less electronegative than O.
  • Ranking boiling and melting points. 1° and 2° amines boil lower than alcohols of similar size (weaker hydrogen bonds), higher than ethers of similar size (ethers cannot hydrogen bond with themselves), and higher than 3° amines (no N–H, so no hydrogen bonding between 3° amine molecules; they rely on dipole–dipole and dispersion forces). Methylamine, dimethylamine, trimethylamine, and ethylamine are gases at room temperature; most other amines are liquids.
  • Solubility. 1° and 2° amines both donate and accept hydrogen bonds with water. 3° amines cannot donate, but the lone pair on N still accepts a hydrogen bond from water, so small 3° amines dissolve too, just not as well. Amines with up to about six carbons have appreciable water solubility; beyond that the nonpolar chain takes over. Solutions are basic (Section 7.3).
  • Many amines have foul odors: rotting fish, urine, bad breath.

Professor Tip

Practice the way the exam asks it. "Which has the highest boiling point: CH3CH2CH3, CH3CH2NH2, (CH3)2NH, or CH3CH2OH?" Same size, so compare IMFs: the alcohol wins (O–H hydrogen bonds), the two amines are next (N–H), and the alkane is last (dispersion only). "Which has the lowest: CH3CH2CH2NH2, CH3CH2NH2, (CH3)2NH, or (CH3)3N?" The 3° amine: no N–H, so no hydrogen bonding with itself. A common mistake is to assume a 3° amine is also insoluble; it still accepts hydrogen bonds from water, so the missing N–H lowers the boiling point, not the solubility.

Professor Tip

For compounds of similar size, rank boiling points as alcohol > 1°/2° amine > 3° amine ≈ ether > alkane.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

The putrescine and cadaverine of the section text are not only a fish-market problem. Bacteria in periodontal pockets break down proteins into the same diamines, which contribute to the characteristic odor of periodontal disease (halitosis with a periodontal source). Because they are amines, the odor compounds are volatile as the free bases and nonvolatile as ammonium salts, the same chemistry you will use in Section 7.3 to explain why acid tames a fishy smell.
