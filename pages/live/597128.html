Heterocyclic Amines

Looking back at the various cyclic hydrocarbons discussed previously, we see that all the atoms in the rings of these compounds are carbon atoms. In other cyclic compounds, called heterocyclic compounds (Greek heteros, meaning “other”), nitrogen, oxygen, sulfur, or some other atom is incorporated in the ring. Many heterocyclic compounds are important in medicine and biochemistry. Some compose part of the structure of the nucleic acids, which in turn compose the genetic material of cells and direct protein synthesis.

The nitrogen-containing rings you will meet most often in this course are listed below. Learn to recognize each one by counting the atoms in the ring and the nitrogen atoms it contains.

  • Pyridine: a six-membered aromatic ring with one nitrogen. It is found in nicotine and in niacin (vitamin B3), part of the coenzyme NAD+.
  • Pyrimidine: a six-membered aromatic ring with two nitrogens. It is the ring in the DNA and RNA bases cytosine, thymine and uracil (Chapter 12).
  • Purine: a pyrimidine ring fused to an imidazole ring. It is the ring in the bases adenine and guanine, and in caffeine.
  • Pyrrole: a five-membered aromatic ring with one N–H. Four pyrrole rings make up the heme of hemoglobin.
  • Imidazole: a five-membered aromatic ring with two nitrogens. It is found in the amino acid histidine and in histamine (Section 7.11).
  • Indole: a benzene ring fused to a pyrrole ring. It is found in the amino acid tryptophan and in serotonin (Section 7.11).

Many heterocyclic amines are made by plants. These alkaloids, such as caffeine, nicotine and cocaine, are described in Sections 7.3 and 7.9. The ring structures are drawn in the Lecture Connection below.

Summary

Heterocyclic amines are cyclic compounds with one or more nitrogen atoms in the ring. Pyridine, pyrimidine, purine, pyrrole, imidazole and indole rings appear in nucleic acid bases, amino acids, vitamins, neurotransmitters and many drugs.

Lecture Connection

In lecture a heterocyclic amine is defined in three parts: a cyclic organic compound, with a five- or six-atom ring, that contains one or more nitrogen atoms in the ring. The goal is recognition: you should be able to spot these rings inside the nucleic acid bases, amino acids, vitamins, and drugs you meet later in the course.

The six rings to recognize

Saturated examples are pyrrolidine (five-membered) and piperidine (six-membered). The aromatic ones below are the rings you will keep meeting in nucleic acids, amino acids, and drugs.

Professor Tip

Two habits make these rings easy. First, count atoms in the ring (five or six) and count nitrogens; that alone separates pyridine from pyrimidine and pyrrole from imidazole. Second, decide whether the ring nitrogen still owns its lone pair. A six-membered ring N (pyridine, pyrimidine) does, so it is weakly basic and can be protonated; an N–H in a five-membered aromatic ring (pyrrole, indole) has given its lone pair to the ring and is not basic. Nicotine shows both kinds at once.

Check yourself: name the ring. (a) A six-membered aromatic ring with two nitrogens, found in cytosine. (b) A five-membered aromatic ring with two nitrogens, found in histidine. (c) A six-membered ring fused to a five-membered ring, with four nitrogens, found in caffeine. (d) A benzene ring fused to a five-membered ring with one N–H, found in serotonin. (e) Which of these rings has a nitrogen that is not basic: pyridine or pyrrole?

Answer
(a) Pyrimidine. (b) Imidazole. (c) Purine. (d) Indole. (e) Pyrrole: its N–H nitrogen has given its lone pair to the aromatic ring; the pyridine nitrogen keeps its lone pair and is weakly basic.

Caffeine, nicotine, and cocaine (Section 7.3) are alkaloids, heterocyclic amines made by plants; Section 7.9 covers how their basicity is used to extract them and why they are sold as hydrochloride salts. Acetylcholine and dopamine, two of the messengers mentioned in Section 7.3, are neurotransmitters, the subject of Section 7.11.
