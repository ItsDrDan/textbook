Learning Objectives

  • Identify and name aldehydes and ketones.
  • Compare their boiling points and solubility with alkanes and alcohols.
  • Predict results of Tollens' and Benedict's tests, and products of oxidation and reduction.
  • Recognize hemiacetals and acetals and draw them from an aldehyde or ketone plus an alcohol.
  • Find chiral carbons, tell enantiomers from diastereomers, and assign D or L from a Fischer projection.

Chapter Summary

The big ideas of the chapter, one paragraph each. Each paragraph ends with the section where it is taught in full.

1. The carbonyl group, C=O

A carbonyl group is a carbon double-bonded to oxygen. An aldehyde has the C=O at the end of a chain (written –CHO, never –COH; ending -al); a ketone has it inside the chain (–CO–; ending -one). Read condensed formulas by counting bonds: carbon needs 4, oxygen needs 2. Naming: the CHO carbon is always C1 and never gets a number; a ketone is numbered to give the C=O the lowest number (propanone and butanone need none). Benzaldehyde and cyclohexanone are the ring cases. Details, common names and examples: Section 5.1.

2. Physical properties

The carbonyl carbon is trigonal planar (120°) and the C=O is polar, so aldehydes and ketones have dipole–dipole forces but cannot hydrogen bond with each other (no O–H). Boiling points: alkane < ether < aldehyde or ketone < alcohol at similar mass; treat aldehydes and ketones as one category. They accept hydrogen bonds from water, so 4 or fewer carbons = soluble, 5 sparingly, 6 or more insoluble. Details, data table and a ranking example: Section 5.2.

3. Reactions

Oxidation: aldehyde → carboxylic acid; ketones do not oxidize (write "no reaction"). Tollens' reagent (Ag+) gives a silver mirror with aldehydes; Benedict's reagent (blue Cu2+ → red Cu2O) responds to aldehydes and α-hydroxy ketones, which is why it detects glucose. Reduction [H] (H2/Ni, Pd or Pt, or NaBH4): aldehyde → 1° alcohol, ketone → 2° alcohol. Adding an alcohol: aldehyde or ketone + 1 alcohol → hemiacetal (one carbon with 1 OH and 1 OR); hemiacetal + a second alcohol → acetal (2 OR) + H2O. Look for the "goggle-eyed" carbon with two single-bonded oxygens. Details, the Tollens'/Benedict's table, reaction figures and examples: Sections 5.3 and 5.4.

4. Chirality

Stereoisomers have the same connectivity but a different arrangement in space. A chiral carbon (stereocenter) is bonded to four different groups; mark it with a star. A carbon in a double or triple bond is never one, and neither is a CH2, a CH3 or a carbon with two identical groups. A chiral molecule has a non-superimposable mirror image (hands); an achiral one is superimposable (socks). Enantiomers are mirror-image stereoisomers; diastereomers are stereoisomers that are not mirror images (two or more chiral carbons, some but not all flipped). n chiral carbons give at most 2n stereoisomers. Fischer projection: most oxidized carbon on top, horizontal bonds toward you, vertical bonds away; at the chiral carbon farthest from the C=O, OH on the right = D, OH on the left = L. Details, the counting table, figures and examples: Section 5.5.

5. Naming molecules with more than one functional group

The highest-priority group gets the suffix and the lowest number; every other group becomes a prefix. Order you need: carboxylic acid > ester > amide > aldehyde > ketone > alcohol > amine > alkene/alkyne > alkane; ethers (alkoxy-) and halogens are always prefixes. Aldehyde beats ketone (ketone becomes oxo-); carbonyl beats alcohol (alcohol becomes hydroxy-): 4-hydroxy-2-butanone, 3-oxobutanal. Details and the full priority table: Section 5.1.

6. Hydrolysis of acetals

Acetal + H2O (H+) → aldehyde or ketone + 2 alcohols; a hemiacetal splits into the carbonyl compound + 1 alcohol with no water needed. Find the carbon with two single-bonded oxygens, send each OR off as R–OH, and turn that carbon back into C=O (an H left on it means an aldehyde, two carbons mean a ketone). Glycosidic bonds in sugars are acetals, and digestion is this hydrolysis. Details and a worked example: Section 5.3.

7. Cyclic hemiacetals: a preview of sugars

A molecule with both a C=O and an OH on C4 or C5 curls up so the OH adds to its own C=O, giving a five- or six-membered ring that contains the oxygen: a cyclic hemiacetal, with the old carbonyl carbon holding one OH and the ring O. Glucose does exactly this, and a cyclic hemiacetal that reacts with another sugar's OH becomes a cyclic acetal, the glycosidic bond. Details and figures: Section 5.3.

Health Career Connection

  • Nursing: acetone, a ketone body, gives the fruity "acetone breath" of diabetic ketoacidosis (Section 5.2); Benedict's-type copper tablets detect reducing sugars in urine, and acetaldehyde from ethanol oxidation causes the flushing reaction (Section 5.4).
  • Dental hygiene: glutaraldehyde and formalin sterilize and fix by cross-linking proteins, vanillin and benzaldehyde flavor rinses, and camphorquinone (two ketone groups) starts the cure in composite resins (Section 5.2).
  • Chiral drugs: levothyroxine, levodopa and esomeprazole are single enantiomers, and only one hand of menthol cools (Section 5.5).

Worked Examples

Example 1: Naming

Problem. Name (a) CH3CH2CH(CH3)CHO and (b) CH3CH2COCH2CH2CH3.

Solution.

(a) The CHO carbon is C1 and the methyl is on C2: 2-methylbutanal.

(b) Six carbons with the C=O at C3 (numbered from the closer end): 3-hexanone.

Check. An aldehyde never needs a locant; a ketone always does unless only one position is possible (propanone, butanone).

Example 2: Tollens' test and reduction

Problem. Propanal and propanone (acetone) are isomers. (a) Which gives a positive Tollens' test? (b) What does each give when reduced?

Solution.

(a) Propanal (an aldehyde) is oxidized to propanoic acid, CH3CH2COOH, and deposits a silver mirror. Propanone: no reaction. Benedict's reagent gives the same verdict: propanal turns the blue Cu2+ solution to red Cu2O while being oxidized to propanoic acid; propanone has no adjacent OH, so again no reaction.

(b) Propanal + [H] → 1-propanol (1°). Propanone + [H] → 2-propanol (2°, rubbing alcohol).

Check. Reduction is the reverse of the Chapter 4 oxidations: 1-propanol oxidizes back to propanal, 2-propanol back to propanone. Whenever a question asks for the oxidation product of a ketone, the answer is "no reaction," not a carboxylic acid.

Example 3: Hemiacetal and acetal

Problem. Draw the products when ethanal reacts with one, then a second, molecule of methanol.

Solution.

Hemiacetal: CH3CH(OH)OCH3. The old C=O carbon now holds an OH and an OCH3.

Acetal: CH3CH(OCH3)2 + H2O. The OH was replaced by a second OCH3.

Check. Overall: C2H4O + 2 CH4O → C4H10O2 + H2O; atoms balance.

Example 4: Finding chiral carbons

Problem. Which of these has a chiral carbon: 2-propanol, 2-butanol, 3-pentanol?

Solution. Check the carbon holding OH in each:

  • 2-propanol: H, OH, CH3, CH3. Two groups are the same, so it is not chiral.
  • 2-butanol: H, OH, CH3, CH2CH3. All four are different, so it is chiral.
  • 3-pentanol: H, OH, CH2CH3, CH2CH3. Not chiral.

Check. Swap the two identical groups in 2-propanol or 3-pentanol and you get the same molecule back, so no mirror-image twin exists.

Example 5: D or L?

Problem. In a Fischer projection of glyceraldehyde (CHO at the top, CH2OH at the bottom), the OH on the middle carbon points to the left. Is it D or L?

Solution.

The middle carbon is the only chiral carbon, so it is also the one farthest from the CHO. OH on the left = L-glyceraldehyde. Its enantiomer, with the OH on the right, is D-glyceraldehyde.

Check. Make sure the projection is drawn the standard way (CHO on top) before reading left or right; flipping the drawing upside down would reverse the answer.

Example 6: Naming a molecule with two functional groups

Problem. Name the two compounds shown.

Solution.

(a) The molecule has a ketone C=O and an OH. Ketone outranks alcohol, so the suffix is -one and the OH becomes hydroxy-. Five carbons: the parent is pentanone. Number from the end that gives the C=O the lowest number: C=O on C2, OH on C4. Name: 4-hydroxy-2-pentanone.

(b) The molecule has an aldehyde (CHO at one end) and a ketone. Aldehyde outranks ketone, so the suffix is -al, the CHO carbon is C1, and the ketone becomes oxo-. Four carbons: butanal, with the ketone O on C3. Name: 3-oxobutanal.

Check. Rebuild each structure from the name. 4-Hydroxy-2-pentanone: CH3COCH2CH(OH)CH3, five carbons, matches. 3-Oxobutanal: OHC–CH2–CO–CH3, four carbons, matches. If you had numbered (a) from the other end you would get 2-hydroxy-4-pentanone, and the suffix group would not have the lowest number, so that name is wrong.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Giving the aldehyde carbon a number (1-butanal). It is always C1, so it is just butanal.
  • Saying ketones give a positive Tollens' test. They do not oxidize.
  • Calling a carbon with two identical groups (two CH3 groups or two H's) chiral.
  • Deciding D/L from the top chiral carbon. Use the one farthest from the C=O.
  • Numbering a hydroxy ketone from the end nearest the OH. The suffix group (the C=O) gets the lowest number: 4-hydroxy-2-pentanone, not 2-hydroxy-4-pentanone.
  • Writing an acetal with only one alcohol consumed. A hemiacetal uses one alcohol; an acetal uses two and releases one water. Hydrolysis of an acetal therefore gives back two alcohol molecules.
  • Counting a ring carbon of benzene or a C=O carbon as chiral. A carbon in a double bond has only three neighbors and can never be a chiral center.
  • Assuming enantiomers differ in melting point or solubility. They do not; only diastereomers have different physical properties.
  • Calling two drawings enantiomers before trying to rotate or flip one of them. If a rotation makes them match, they are the same molecule.
  • Writing a carboxylic acid as the oxidation product of a ketone. Ketones have no H on the carbonyl carbon; the answer is "no reaction."

Clinical Update: Rosa’s Story, Part 5 of 15

What the A1c measures

Rosa’s story for this chapter is on the Chapter 5 opening page. Quick recap for the questions below: the A1c measures how much hemoglobin carries glucose, which attaches through the aldehyde group of glucose’s open-chain form; Rosa’s mother once tested her urine for sugar with tablets that turned from blue to orange-red.

Case Question 5.1

In its open-chain form, is glucose an aldehyde or a ketone? Which carbon carries the C=O group?

Answer
An aldehyde (in Chapter 8 you will call a sugar like this an aldose). Carbon 1 is the aldehyde carbon, –CHO.

Case Question 5.2

The old urine tablets worked like Benedict’s reagent. What happens to glucose and to the copper in a positive test?

Answer
The aldehyde of glucose is oxidized (to a carboxylic acid group), and blue Cu2+ is reduced to brick-red Cu2O. Glucose is a reducing sugar.

Case Question 5.3

Open-chain D-glucose has four chiral carbons. Why does it matter to the body that glucose is chiral?

Answer
Enzymes and transporters are chiral, so they fit one stereoisomer. Cells use D-glucose; its mirror image, L-glucose, is not recognized by the same transporters and enzymes.

Where this chemistry is taught: Section 5.1, Section 5.4, Section 5.5. Rosa’s story continues at the start of Chapter 6.

Name CH3COCH(CH3)CH3.

Answer
3-methyl-2-butanone

Exercise 2

Rank by boiling point: butanal, 1-butanol, pentane (similar molar masses).

Answer
Pentane (36 °C) < butanal (75 °C) < 1-butanol (118 °C): dispersion < dipole–dipole < hydrogen bonding.

Exercise 3

What alcohol is formed when cyclopentanone is reduced? Is it 1°, 2°, or 3°?

Answer
Cyclopentanol, a 2° alcohol

Exercise 4

Is CH3C(OCH2CH3)2CH3 a hemiacetal, an acetal, or neither? What two compounds made it?

Answer
An acetal (ketal): one carbon with two O–C bonds and no OH. It came from propanone (acetone) and two molecules of ethanol.

Exercise 5

How many chiral carbons are in 2,3-dichlorobutane, CH3CHClCHClCH3?

Answer
Two: C2 and C3 each have H, Cl, CH3, and the rest of the chain.

Exercise 6

Name HOCH2CH2CH2CHO.

Answer
4-hydroxybutanal (the aldehyde gets the suffix and is C1)
