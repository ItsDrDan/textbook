The structure of an amino acid allows it to act as both an acid and a base. An amino acid has this ability because at a certain pH value (different for each amino acid) nearly all the amino acid molecules exist as zwitterions. If acid is added to a solution containing the zwitterion, the carboxylate group captures a hydrogen (H+) ion, and the amino acid becomes positively charged. If base is added, removal of the H+ ion from the amino group of the zwitterion produces a negatively charged amino acid. In both circumstances, the amino acid acts to maintain the pH of the system—that is, to remove the added acid (H+) or base (OH−) from solution.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\)

  1. Draw the structure for the anion formed when glycine (at neutral pH) reacts with a base.
  2. Draw the structure for the cation formed when glycine (at neutral pH) reacts with an acid.

Solution

  1. The base removes H+ from the protonated amine group.
  • The acid adds H+ to the carboxylate group.
Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)
  1. Draw the structure for the cation formed when valine (at neutral pH) reacts with an acid.
  2. Draw the structure for the anion formed when valine (at neutral pH) reacts with a base.
Answer
(a) The acid adds H+ to the carboxylate: +H3N–CH[CH(CH3)2]–COOH (net charge +1). (b) The base removes H+ from the ammonium group: H2N–CH[CH(CH3)2]–COO− (net charge −1).

The particular pH at which a given amino acid exists in solution as a zwitterion is called the isoelectric point (pI). At its pI, the positive and negative charges on the amino acid balance, and the molecule as a whole is electrically neutral. The amino acids whose side chains are always neutral have isoelectric points ranging from 5.0 to 6.5. The basic amino acids (which have positively charged side chains at neutral pH) have relatively high isoelectric points. Acidic amino acids (which have negatively charged side chains at neutral pH) have quite low isoelectric points (Table \(\PageIndex{1}\)).

Summary

Amino acids can act as both an acid and a base due to the presence of the amino and carboxyl functional groups. The pH at which a given amino acid exists in solution as a zwitterion is called the isoelectric point (pI).

Lecture Connection

This is the "draw it at three pH values" section. The whole skill is one rule: below the pI everything that can be protonated is; above the pI everything that can lose an H+ has. Learn the zwitterion as your reference point and count charges last.

The zwitterion

An amino acid contains both an acid (–COOH) and a base (–NH2) in the same molecule, so it reacts with itself: the carboxyl group hands its H+ to the amine. The result is a zwitterion, a molecule that carries a positive charge (–NH3+) and a negative charge (–COO−) at the same time but has a net charge of zero. That is why amino acids behave like salts: they are crystalline solids with high melting points and dissolve in water but not in nonpolar solvents.

  • At physiological pH (about 7.4) an amino acid with a neutral R group is a zwitterion: –NH3+ and –COO−, net charge 0.
  • In strong acid (low pH), the COO− picks up H+ → –COOH. Net charge +1.
  • In strong base (high pH), the NH3+ loses H+ → –NH2. Net charge −1.

Only strong acid puts an H+ back on the carboxylate, and only strong base takes the H+ off the ammonium group. In between, including blood at pH 7.4, the zwitterion is the main form.

Professor Tip

Use the zwitterion as your reference point and remember the lecture rule: at a pH below the pI the functional groups are protonated; at the pI the amino acid is the zwitterion; at a pH above the pI the functional groups are deprotonated. "Acid adds H+, base takes H+." Low pH: everything that can be protonated is. High pH: everything that can lose an H+ has. Count charges last: +1 for each –NH3+, −1 for each –COO−, and add them up.

Where the pI sits, and how a side chain moves it

Amino acids with a neutral R group have pI values near 6. A charged side chain moves the pI. Aspartate and glutamate carry a second carboxyl group that is also deprotonated at neutral pH, so they reach net zero only in quite acidic solution: pI ≈ 3.0 for Asp and 3.2 for Glu. At pH 7.4 both are well above their pI and carry a net charge of −1 (two –COO− and one –NH3+). Lysine and arginine carry a second basic nitrogen that stays protonated until the pH is high, so their pI values are about 9.7 and 10.8; at pH 7.4 they are below their pI and net +1. Histidine's imidazole ring is a weaker base, giving pI ≈ 7.6, so histidine is close to neutral in blood and is the side chain that does most of a protein's buffering.

Professor Tip

Use this rule of thumb: most amino acids have a pI of about 5.0–6.0; an acidic R group pulls the pI below 4.0; a basic R group pushes it above 6.0 (lysine about 9.7, arginine about 10.8). The decimals never matter; the pattern does. You will always be given the pI if a question depends on the exact number.

Three rules give the sign of the net charge in every exam question about pI. Draw the forms shown when the pH is far from the pI (strong acid or strong base); an amino acid with a neutral R group at pH 7.4 is still drawn as the zwitterion.

  • pH below pI → the solution is more acidic than the zwitterion "wants," so an extra H+ sits on the carboxyl group: –COOH and –NH3+, net positive (+1 for a neutral R group).
  • pH at pI → zwitterion, –COO− and –NH3+, net zero.
  • pH above pI → H+ has been removed from the ammonium group: –COO− and –NH2, net negative (−1 for a neutral R group).

Electrophoresis: charge you can see

Because charge depends on pH, a mixture of amino acids (or proteins) can be separated by placing it in a buffer of known pH on a gel between two electrodes. Anything with a net negative charge migrates toward the positive electrode, anything positive toward the negative electrode, and a molecule sitting at its pI does not move. At pH 6.0, for example, glutamate (net −) moves toward the positive electrode, lysine (net +) toward the negative electrode, and alanine (at its pI) stays put. Clinical labs use the same principle to separate hemoglobin variants such as sickle hemoglobin (HbS), which has lost two negative charges (one Glu → Val in each β chain) and therefore migrates more slowly toward the positive electrode than normal HbA, and to separate serum proteins into albumin and globulin fractions. A protein is also least soluble at its pI, because with no net charge the molecules no longer repel one another and clump; that is how casein is precipitated from milk in the lab by adding acid to pH 4.6 (see Section 10.3).

Worked example

Example 1: Charge of alanine relative to its pI

Problem. Alanine has pI ≈ 6.0. Draw its predominant form and give its net charge at pH 2, pH 6 and pH 10. In an electrophoresis gel buffered at each pH, which electrode would it move toward?

Solution. Compare each pH with the pI.

  • pH 2 (below pI): the solution is acidic enough to keep H+ on the carboxyl group. Form: +H3N–CH(CH3)–COOH. Charges: +1 and 0, net +1. It migrates toward the negative electrode.
  • pH 6 (at pI): zwitterion, +H3N–CH(CH3)–COO−. Charges +1 and −1, net 0. It does not move.
  • pH 10 (above pI): base has removed the H+ from the ammonium group. Form: H2N–CH(CH3)–COO−. Charges 0 and −1, net −1. It migrates toward the positive electrode.

Check. Charge should fall as pH rises (+1 → 0 → −1), and of the three forms drawn, the one with both charges (net charge 0) is the one at pH 6, near the pI. (The zwitterion is actually the main form over a wide range, from about pH 3 to 8; the pI is where it is at its maximum.) If you had put glutamate (pI 3.2) in the pH 6 gel instead, pH 6 would be above its pI, so it would carry a net negative charge and move toward the positive electrode while alanine stayed put. That difference is how electrophoresis separates them.

Example 2: Lysine at three pH values

Problem. Lysine has three groups that can carry a charge: the α-carboxyl group, the α-amino group, and the amino group at the end of its side chain, –(CH2)4NH2. Give the charge on each group and the net charge in strong acid (pH 1), at body pH (7.4), and in strong base (pH 12).

Solution. Use the course rules for each group, then add.

  • pH 1 (strong acid): everything that can be protonated is. α-COOH 0, α-NH3+ +1, side-chain –NH3+ +1. Net +2.
  • pH 7.4 (body pH): carboxyl groups are –COO− and amines are –NH3+. α-COO− −1, α-NH3+ +1, side-chain –NH3+ +1. Net +1.
  • pH 12 (strong base): every group that can lose an H+ has. α-COO− −1, α-NH2 0, side-chain –NH2 0. Net −1.

Check. Charge falls as pH rises (+2 → +1 → −1), and the +1 at pH 7.4 matches the table above for a basic amino acid. Compared with alanine, each answer is one unit more positive in acid and at body pH, because lysine has one extra amine.
