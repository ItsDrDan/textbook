Learning Objectives

  • Identify the general structure for an amide.
  • Identify the functional group for an amide.
  • Name amides with common names.
  • Name amides according to the IUPAC system.

The amide functional group has a nitrogen atom attached to a carbonyl carbon atom. If the two remaining bonds on the nitrogen atom are attached to hydrogen atoms, the compound is a simple amide. If one or both of the two remaining bonds on the atom are attached to alkyl or aryl groups, the compound is a substituted amide. In this course we classify amides the same way as amines: a 1° amide has –CONH2, a 2° amide has one carbon group on the N, and a 3° amide has two. There is no quaternary amide. On exams give the IUPAC name (ethanamide) and recognize the common one (acetamide).

The carbonyl carbon-to-nitrogen bond is called an amide linkage. This bond is quite stable and is found in the repeating units of protein molecules, where it is called a peptide linkage.

Simple amides are named as derivatives of carboxylic acids. The -ic ending of the common name or the -oic ending of the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) name of the carboxylic acid is replaced with the suffix -amide.

Solution

Lecture Connection

In lecture an amide is "one more carbonyl functional group": the acyl group RCO– bonded to nitrogen. You classify it the way you classified amines (count the carbon groups on N) and name it the way you named carboxylic acids (change the suffix to -amide), with any groups on nitrogen marked N-.

Recognizing and classifying amides

An amide has the carbonyl carbon bonded to N instead of the OH of a carboxylic acid or the OR of an ester. Amides are classified as 1°, 2°, or 3° just like amines, by the number of carbon groups on the nitrogen other than the carbonyl carbon. Reading a condensed formula: CH3CH2CONHCH3 and CH3NHCOCH2CH3 are the same amide written from opposite ends (CO next to N either way), while CH3NHCH2COCH3 is not an amide: its N is bonded to a CH2, and the C=O is a ketone one carbon away. Practice: which of CH3CH2CONH2, CH3CH2CONHCH2CH2CH3, CH3CH2NHCOCH2CH3, and CH3NHCH2COCH2CH2CH3 is not an amide? Only the last one.

Naming amides

  • Amide names blend carboxylic acid and amine naming. Name the acyl group as you would the carboxylic acid, but change -oic acid (IUPAC) or -ic acid (common) to -amide: ethanamide (acetamide), butanamide, benzamide.
  • Any R groups on the nitrogen get the N- prefix, alphabetically: N-methylethanamide, N-ethyl-N-methylethanamide, N,N-diethylbenzamide.
  • Substituents on the carbon chain get numbers, counting from the C=O carbon as C1, and are listed in the same alphabetical string: N,3-dimethylbutanamide has one methyl on N and one on C3. Practice: N,N-diethylhexanamide, N-ethyl-N,2-dimethylbutanamide, N-ethylpropanamide, N-methylbenzamide; draw each and check the class.

Example 1: Name and classify an amide

Problem. Name and classify C6H5CON(CH3)CH2CH3.

Solution. The C=O is bonded to N, so this is an amide of benzoic acid: parent benzamide. The N carries a methyl and an ethyl group, each named with the locant N and listed alphabetically: N-ethyl-N-methylbenzamide. Two groups on N besides the carbonyl make it a 3° amide.

Check. N for both groups because both are on nitrogen; a group on the ring would get a number instead. A 3° amide has no N–H, so this compound cannot hydrogen bond to itself and is a liquid, unlike benzamide (mp 132 °C; see Section 7.5).

What the amide group does (and does not do)

  • Amides are not basic. The C=O pulls electron density from the N, giving the C–N bond partial double-bond character, so the lone pair is not available to pick up H+. Amides do not form salts with HCl and do not turn litmus blue.
  • That partial double bond also means the amide C–N bond cannot rotate, which is important for the shape of proteins (Chapter 10). Like esters, amides can also be cyclic.
  • Everyday amides include acetaminophen (Tylenol), urea (the nitrogen waste in urine), and melatonin (the hormone that sets the sleep–wake cycle).

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Acetaminophen is the amide of 4-aminophenol: find the C=O, see the N on its far side, and notice the phenol OH across the ring. Urea, a diamide, is how the liver packages waste nitrogen; the blood urea nitrogen (BUN) test tracks kidney function, and a failing liver lets ammonia build up (hyperammonemia, Chapter 15). Sulfonamide antibiotics are aromatic amines carrying a sulfur analog of an amide, S(=O)2–NH. Because amides are neutral, none of these drugs is supplied as a hydrochloride salt; that label belongs to amines (Section 7.3).
