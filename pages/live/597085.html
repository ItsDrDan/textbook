Learning Objectives

  • Recognize aromatic compounds from structural formulas.
  • Name aromatic compounds given formulas.
  • Write formulas for aromatic compounds given their names.

Historically, benzene-like substances were called aromatic hydrocarbons because they had distinctive aromas. Today, an aromatic compound is any compound that contains a benzene ring or has certain benzene-like properties (but not necessarily a strong aroma). You can recognize the aromatic compounds in this text by the presence of one or more benzene rings in their structure. Some representative aromatic compounds and their uses are listed in Table \(\PageIndex{1}\), where the benzene ring is represented as C6H5.

Table \(\PageIndex{1}\): Some Representative Aromatic Compounds
Name Structure Typical Uses
aniline C6H5–NH2 starting material for the synthesis of dyes, drugs, resins, varnishes, perfumes; solvent; vulcanizing rubber
benzoic acid C6H5–COOH food preservative; starting material for the synthesis of dyes and other organic compounds; curing of tobacco
bromobenzene C6H5–Br starting material for the synthesis of many other aromatic compounds; solvent; motor oil additive
nitrobenzene C6H5–NO2 starting material for the synthesis of aniline; solvent for cellulose nitrate; in soaps and shoe polish
phenol C6H5–OH disinfectant; starting material for the synthesis of resins, drugs, and other organic compounds
toluene C6H5–CH3 solvent; gasoline octane booster; starting material for the synthesis of benzoic acid, benzaldehyde, and many other organic compounds

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Which compounds are aromatic?

Solution

  1. The compound has a benzene ring (with a chlorine atom substituted for one of the hydrogen atoms); it is aromatic.
  2. The compound is cyclic, but it does not have a benzene ring; it is not aromatic.
  3. The compound has a benzene ring (with a propyl group substituted for one of the hydrogen atoms); it is aromatic.
  4. The compound is cyclic, but it does not have a benzene ring; it is not aromatic.

In the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) system, aromatic hydrocarbons are named as derivatives of benzene. Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\) shows five examples. In these structures, it is immaterial whether the single substituent is written at the top, side, or bottom of the ring: a hexagon is symmetrical, and therefore all positions are equivalent.

Although some compounds are referred to exclusively by IUPAC names, some are more frequently denoted by common names, as is indicated in Table \(\PageIndex{1}\).

When there is more than one substituent, the corners of the hexagon are no longer equivalent, so we must designate the relative positions. There are three possible disubstituted benzenes, and we can use numbers to distinguish them (Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\)). We start numbering at the carbon atom to which one of the groups is attached and count toward the carbon atom that bears the other substituent group by the shortest path.

In Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\), common names are also used: the prefix ortho (o-) for 1,2-disubstitution, meta (m-) for 1,3-disubstitution, and para (p-) for 1,4-disubstitution. The substituent names are listed in alphabetical order. The first substituent is given the lowest number. Example \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Name each compound using both the common name and the IUPAC name.

Solution
  1. The benzene ring has two chlorine atoms (dichloro) in the first and second positions. The compound is o-dichlorobenzene or 1,2-dichlorobenzene.
  2. The benzene ring has a methyl (CH3) group. The compound is therefore named as a derivative of toluene. The bromine atom is on the fourth carbon atom, counting from the methyl group. The compound is p-bromotoluene or 4-bromotoluene.
  3. The benzene ring has two nitro (NO2) groups in the first and third positions. It is m-dinitrobenzene or 1,3-dinitrobenzene.
  4. Note: The nitro (NO2) group is a common substituent in aromatic compounds. Many nitro compounds are explosive, most notably 2,4,6-trinitrotoluene (TNT).

Answer

  2. o-bromoaniline; 2-bromoaniline

  • Sometimes an aromatic group is found as a substituent bonded to a nonaromatic entity or to another aromatic ring. The group of atoms remaining when a hydrogen atom is removed from an aromatic compound is called an aryl group. The most common aryl group is derived from benzene (C6H6) by removing one hydrogen atom (C6H5) and is called a phenyl group, from phene, an old name for benzene. Phenyl is the only aryl group you need in this course.

Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons

Some common aromatic hydrocarbons consist of fused benzene rings—rings that share a common side. These compounds are called polycyclic (or polynuclear) aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs); both names are used and mean the same compounds.

The three examples shown here are colorless, crystalline solids generally obtained from coal tar. Naphthalene has a pungent odor and is used in mothballs. Anthracene is used in the manufacture of certain dyes. Steroids, a large group of naturally occurring substances, contain four fused rings: three six-membered rings joined like those of phenanthrene plus a five-membered ring, and in steroids the rings are saturated, not aromatic (Chapter 9).

To Your Health: Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons and Cancer

The intense heating required for distilling coal tar results in the formation of PAHs. For many years, it has been known that workers in coal-tar refineries are susceptible to a type of skin cancer known as tar cancer. Investigations have shown that a number of PAHs are carcinogens. One of the most active carcinogenic compounds, benzopyrene, occurs in coal tar and has also been isolated from cigarette smoke, automobile exhaust gases, and charcoal-broiled steaks. It is estimated that more than 1,000 t of benzopyrene are emitted into the air over the United States each year. Only a few milligrams of benzopyrene per kilogram of body weight are required to induce cancer in experimental animals.

Biologically Important Compounds with Benzene Rings

Substances containing the benzene ring are common in both animals and plants, although they are more abundant in the latter. Plants can synthesize the benzene ring from carbon dioxide, water, and inorganic materials. Animals cannot synthesize it, but they are dependent on certain aromatic compounds for survival and therefore must obtain them from food. Phenylalanine and tryptophan (essential amino acids), tyrosine (which the body makes from phenylalanine), and vitamins K, B2 (riboflavin), and B9 (folic acid) all contain the benzene ring. Many important drugs, a few of which are shown in Table \(\PageIndex{2}\), also feature a benzene ring.

So far we have studied only aromatic compounds with carbon-containing rings. However, many cyclic compounds have an element other than carbon atoms in the ring. These compounds, called heterocyclic compounds, are discussed later. Some of these are heterocyclic aromatic compounds.

Key Takeaway

  • Aromatic compounds contain a benzene ring or have certain benzene-like properties; for our purposes, you can recognize aromatic compounds by the presence of one or more benzene rings in their structure.​​​​​​​

Lecture Connection

Lecture's version of this section is a short checklist: benzene is the parent, ortho/meta/para are for exactly two groups, four special parents (toluene, phenol, aniline, benzoic acid) own carbon 1, and when the ring hangs off a longer chain it becomes phenyl.

Naming substituted benzenes

  • Common names to know: toluene (methylbenzene), phenol (hydroxybenzene), aniline (aminobenzene), and benzoic acid.
  • Benzene is the "parent chain." For two substituents: ortho (1,2: next-door neighbors), meta (1,3: one ring carbon in between), para (1,4: directly opposite).

The checklist.

  • One substituent: name the group, then "benzene," with no number: chlorobenzene, nitrobenzene (–NO2), ethylbenzene. A few rings have their own parent names that you must memorize: toluene (–CH3), phenol (–OH), aniline (–NH2), and benzoic acid (–COOH).
  • Two substituents: there are only three possible arrangements, 1,2-, 1,3-, and 1,4-, called ortho (o-), meta (m-), and para (p-). You may use either the numbers or the prefix. If one of the groups gives the ring a special parent name, that group is on carbon 1 and is not named as a substituent: a chlorine straight across from the methyl of toluene is 4-chlorotoluene or p-chlorotoluene.
  • Three or more substituents: numbers only (no o/m/p). Number to give the lowest set of numbers, start from the special parent group if there is one, and list the substituents alphabetically: 2,4-dichlorotoluene; 1-bromo-3-chloro-5-nitrobenzene.

Phenyl and the polynuclear aromatics

  • When a benzene ring is attached to a middle carbon of a chain, or to a chain that holds the main functional group, it is named as a substituent: phenyl (C6H5–). Example: CH3CH(C6H5)CH2CH3 is 2-phenylbutane.
  • Polynuclear aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) have two or more fused benzene rings: naphthalene (2 rings, mothballs), anthracene (3 rings). Larger PAHs such as benzo[a]pyrene form when organic matter burns incompletely and are carcinogens in tobacco smoke and charred food.

One rule covers every case. Use "phenyl" when the ring is attached to a middle carbon of a chain (2-phenylbutane, drawn in the naming figure above) or when the chain carries the main functional group (2-phenylethanol). When an unbranched alkyl group is attached to the ring by its end carbon, the ring is the parent: ethylbenzene, not phenylethane. Both names describe real molecules correctly; the convention just keeps names short.

In a PAH, neighboring rings share an edge (two carbons), so naphthalene is C10H8, not C12. All the rings lie in one flat sheet, which lets PAH molecules stack like plates; that stacking is why even naphthalene is a solid at room temperature. PAHs are nonpolar and dissolve in body fat. When the liver tries to oxidize benzo[a]pyrene to make it water soluble for excretion, some of the intermediate products react with DNA, which is how a chemically "stable" aromatic compound becomes a carcinogen (a cancer-causing agent). Wood smoke, diesel exhaust, grilled meat with char, and above all tobacco smoke are the common sources.

Example 1: Naming polysubstituted benzenes

Problem. Name each compound. (a) A benzene ring with –CH3 and, straight across the ring, –Cl. (b) A benzene ring with –OH and, on the next carbon, –NO2. (c) A benzene ring with –Br on C1 and –CH2CH3 on C3. (d) A benzene ring with –CH3 and two Cl atoms, one next to the methyl and one straight across from it.

Solution.

  • (a) The methyl makes this a toluene, so –CH3 is C1 and the Cl is on C4: 4-chlorotoluene, or p-chlorotoluene.
  • (b) The OH makes it a phenol; OH is C1 and the nitro group is on C2: 2-nitrophenol, or o-nitrophenol.
  • (c) Neither group has a special parent, so the parent is benzene and the substituents are listed alphabetically: 1-bromo-3-ethylbenzene, or m-bromoethylbenzene.
  • (d) Three substituents, so numbers only. Toluene parent: CH3 on C1, the chlorines on C2 and C4: 2,4-dichlorotoluene.

Check. Two groups: o/m/p or numbers are both fine. Three or more: numbers only. A special parent group (toluene, phenol, aniline, benzoic acid) is always on C1 and is never listed as a substituent; in (a) "1-chloro-4-methylbenzene" is not wrong, but 4-chlorotoluene is the name you will see on labels.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Three aromatic stories play out in the operatory. First, benzocaine, the active ingredient in topical anesthetic gel applied before scaling or injections, is a para-disubstituted benzene: an amino group (–NH2) on carbon 1 and an ester group straight across on carbon 4. Spotting that 1,4 pattern is exactly the naming skill in this section. Second, tobacco smoke carries polynuclear aromatic hydrocarbons such as benzo[a]pyrene directly onto oral tissue, which is why smokers have a much higher rate of oral squamous cell carcinoma and of periodontitis (infection and bone loss around the teeth that follows untreated gingivitis). Smoking also narrows gingival blood vessels, so the bleeding that normally signals gum disease may be masked. Third, the resins you work with every day are alkene chemistry: pit-and-fissure sealants and composite restorations are methacrylate monomers (Bis-GMA and its thinner partners) whose C=C bonds open under the curing light and link into a hard addition polymer, and denture bases and orthodontic retainers are poly(methyl methacrylate), the acrylic in the polymer table.
