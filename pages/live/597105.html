  • Recognize an oxidation (more C–O bonds or fewer C–H bonds) and a reduction (the reverse) in an organic reaction.
  • Predict the products of hydrogenating an alkene and of reducing an aldehyde or ketone.
  • Predict the products of oxidizing 1° and 2° alcohols and aldehydes, and explain why 3° alcohols and ketones are not oxidized.
  • Describe the Tollens’ and Benedict’s tests, the role of NAD+/NADH in the body, and the thiol/disulfide pair in proteins.

What are oxidation and reduction?

Oxidation is i) the loss of electrons, ii) the gain of \(\ce{O}\), or iii) the loss of \(\ce{H}\). Reduction is the opposite, i.e., i) the gain of electrons, ii) the loss of \(\ce{O}\), or iii) the gain of \(\ce{H}\). Reduction and oxidation always occur together and are called redox reactions. The loss or gain of electrons is easily recognized in inorganic chemical reactions. For example, consider the following reaction:

\(\ce{ 4Fe + 3O2 -> 2Fe2O3}\),

\(\ce{Fe}\) lost three electrons to become \(\ce{Fe^{3+}}\) and \(\ce{O}\) gained two electrons to become \(\ce{O^{2-}}\) in this reaction.

Electron loss or gain is hard to see in organic molecules, which are held together by shared electrons. Instead, count bonds to carbon: an oxidation increases the number of C–O bonds and/or decreases the number of C–H bonds; a reduction does the opposite. Over a reaction arrow, [O] stands for any oxidizing agent and [H] for any reducing agent.

Catalytic hydrogenation of alkenes

Catalytic hydrogenation of vegetable oils

Vegetable oils are triglycerides: esters of glycerol with three long-chain fatty acids that contain one or more \(\ce{C=C}\) bonds. The \(\ce{C=C}\) bonds put kinks in the chains, so the chains cannot pack closely and the oils have low melting points. Partial hydrogenation of some of the \(\ce{C=C}\) bonds turns a liquid oil into a semisolid fat such as margarine, as shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\). The hydrogenated chains are straighter, so they pack together better and melt at a higher temperature.

Hydrogen (\(\ce{H2}\)) reduces aldehydes and ketones to alcohols in the presence of a metal catalyst such as finely divided \(\ce{Ni}\), \(\ce{Pd}\) or \(\ce{Pt}\), as shown in the following examples.

If a \(\ce{C=C}\) bond and a \(\ce{C=O}\) bond are present in the same molecule, catalytic hydrogenation reduces both, as shown below.

In the laboratory an aldehyde or ketone can also be reduced by a hydride reagent such as sodium borohydride (NaBH4). Whatever the reagent, the result is the same: H adds to the carbon and to the oxygen of the C=O, and on exams you write [H] over the arrow.

Reduction of aldehydes and ketones in biological systems

In the body the hydrogen comes from a coenzyme. The reduced coenzyme NADH (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) supplies the hydrogen and is oxidized to \(\ce{NAD^{+}}\) in the process. In equations it is written as NADH + H+ (Chapter 13). Its structure is shown below.

Enzyme-catalyzed conversion of acetaldehyde to ethanol during fermentation is an example of reduction using \(\ce{NADH}\).

Conversion of pyruvate to lactate during glycolysis under anaerobic (absence of oxygen) conditions is another example of reduction using \(\ce{NADH}\), as shown below.

Accumulation of lactate after strenuous exercise accompanies muscle fatigue, but lactate itself is no longer thought to be the main cause of that fatigue.

Reduction of disulfide \(\ce{S-S}\) bond in biological systems

The disulfide (\(\ce{S-S}\)) bond holds many proteins in shape and helps make hair straight or curly. A reducing agent, written [H], breaks an S–S bond into two –SH groups: R–S–S–R + [H] → 2 R–SH (Section 4.9). Oxidation, [O], joins them again, which is how a permanent wave sets a new shape in hair.

Oxidation of alcohols and aldehydes

Primary and secondary alcohols are oxidized by oxidizing agents such as potassium dichromate (\(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\)) in acid; on exams, write [O] over the arrow. In each case the oxidizing agent removes two hydrogens, the H of the O–H and the H on the carbon that carries the O–H, and a C=O bond forms in their place.

Primary alcohols are oxidized to aldehydes, and the aldehydes are easily oxidized further to carboxylic acids: 1° alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid (Section 4.5).

Secondary alcohols are oxidized to a ketone, as shown in the following example. Ketones are not oxidized further because the carbonyl carbon has no hydrogen left to remove.

Tertiary alcohols are not oxidized for the same reason, i.e., there is no hydrogen on the carbon to which the –OH is attached.

Tests for aldehydes and reducing sugar based on easy oxidation of aldehydes

Aldehydes are easily oxidized and ketones are not, and this difference is the basis of tests that tell them apart. In the first test, Tollens' reagent (\(\ce{[Ag(NH3)2]OH}\)) is mixed with the test sample. Ketones do not react, but the aldehyde is oxidized by the following reaction:

\[\ce{2[Ag(NH3)2]OH + R-CHO -> 2Ag(s) + 4NH3 + R-COOH + H2O}\nonumber \]

(In the basic reagent the acid is present as its carboxylate salt, RCOO−.)

The silver that forms coats the inside of the test tube as a silver mirror, as shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\).

Benedict's reagent is a blue solution of copper(II) ions (\(\ce{Cu^{2+}}\)) in a basic citrate solution. An aldehyde reduces the blue \(\ce{Cu^{2+}}\) to a brick-red precipitate of copper(I) oxide, \(\ce{Cu2O}\), and is itself oxidized to a carboxylate ion.

This test distinguishes aldehydes from ketones and is also positive for reducing sugars (carbohydrates) that contain an aldehyde group, as shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{3}\).

Orange dichromate ions, \(\ce{Cr2O7^{2-}}\), oxidize ethanol and are reduced to green chromium(III) ions, \(\ce{Cr^{3+}}\):

\[\ce{3CH3CH2OH + 2Cr2O7^{2-}(reddish orange) + 16H+ -> 3CH3COOH + 4Cr^{3+}(green) + 11H2O}\nonumber \]

Older breath-alcohol testers used this color change. Most devices used by police today measure breath alcohol with a fuel cell (which also oxidizes the ethanol) or with infrared light.

Oxidation of alcohols in living systems

Alcohols are oxidized by nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (\(\ce{NAD^+}\)) to aldehydes and ketones in the cells. For example, ethanol, methanol, and lactate are oxidized to ethanal, methanal, and pyruvate, respectively, as shown below.

Unpleasant effects of drinking alcohol, i.e., flushing, nausea, dizziness, sweating, headache, and low blood pressure, are caused by ethanal (acetaldehyde). Disulfiram (Antabuse) blocks aldehyde dehydrogenase, so acetaldehyde is not converted to acetic acid, causing the unpleasant effects of acetaldehyde to persist. Methanol is oxidized to methanal (formaldehyde) and then to formic acid. Formic acid, present in the body as formate, is the main poison: it causes metabolic acidosis and damages the optic nerve, which can cause blindness. Methanol is added to ethanol meant to be used as a solvent (denatured alcohol) to make it undrinkable. In methanol poisoning, the first-line antidote is fomepizole, a drug that blocks alcohol dehydrogenase. Ethanol, which competes with methanol for the same enzyme, was the older alternative. Either way, methanol is excreted unchanged before it can be converted to its toxic products.

Oxidation of thiol (\(\ce{-SH}\)) to disulfide (\(\ce{-S-S{-}}\)) linkage

Thiol (\(\ce{-SH}\)) is present in the amino acid cysteine. Two cysteine units in the same or different protein chains can oxidize their thiol groups to form disulfide (\(\ce{-S-S{-}}\)) linkage. The disulfide linkage plays an important part in defining the tertiary structure of proteins. This oxidation reaction can be carried out easily by a variety of oxidizing agents, including iodine (\(\ce{I2}\)), bromine (\(\ce{Br2}\)), and oxygen (\(\ce{O2}\)), as shown in an example below.

\[\ce{2R-SH + 1/2O2 -> RS-SR + H2O}\nonumber \]

Lecture Connection

In lecture, redox for carbonyls comes down to two rules and two test tubes. Rules: aldehydes oxidize to carboxylic acids, ketones do not; [H] reduces an aldehyde to a 1° alcohol and a ketone to a 2° alcohol. Test tubes: Tollens' reagent (silver mirror) and Benedict's reagent (blue to brick red), both of which say "aldehyde present."

Professor Tip

Use these definitions on exams: an oxidation is an increase in C–O bonds and/or a decrease in C–H bonds; a reduction is the reverse. Write [O] for any oxidizing agent and [H] for any reducing agent (H2 with a Ni, Pd or Pt catalyst, or NaBH4). The alcohol oxidations and the thiol/disulfide pair reviewed here are Chapter 4 material (Sections 4.5 and 4.9); the NAD+/NADH coenzyme returns in Chapter 13.

Oxidation: aldehydes yes, ketones no

  • Aldehyde → carboxylic acid. The aldehyde's C–H becomes a C–OH. Ketones cannot be oxidized because the ketone carbon has no H to give up. When an exam asks for the product of oxidizing a ketone, write no reaction.
  • Tollens' reagent (silver ion in ammonia solution) oxidizes an aldehyde selectively, even in the presence of other functional groups, and deposits metallic silver as a silver mirror. Ketones: no reaction.
  • Benedict's reagent changes color with compounds that have an aldehyde group or a ketone with an adjacent hydroxyl group (an α-hydroxy ketone): blue Cu2+ → red Cu2O(s). Simple ketones: no reaction. A urine-sugar test is this reaction, because glucose is an aldehyde (Chapter 8).

The two reagents both test for the aldehyde group, but they look different and do not respond to exactly the same compounds. The table summarizes what to expect.

In the Lab

Tollens’ test: aldehydes reduce Ag+ to a silver mirror on the tube; ketones give no reaction. Iodoform test: a methyl ketone (CH3–C=O) reacts with I2 in NaOH to give a yellow solid, iodoform (CHI3), with a medicinal odor (the equation is drawn in the lecture-slide figure in Section 5.1). You will combine these results with the odors from Section 5.2 to identify an unknown carbonyl compound.

Reduction: [H] adds two hydrogens across the C=O

Reduction is the opposite of oxidation. Hydrogen gas with a metal catalyst (Ni, Pd or Pt) or a hydride reagent such as sodium borohydride, NaBH4, adds two H atoms across the C=O: one to the carbon, one to the oxygen. The carbonyl carbon keeps whatever it already had, so the number of carbons attached to it tells you the class of the alcohol.

  • Aldehyde (C=O carbon holds one C and one H) → 1° alcohol.
  • Ketone (C=O carbon holds two C) → 2° alcohol.
  • You can never get a 3° alcohol by reducing a carbonyl, and the reverse is also true: a 3° alcohol has no H on the carbinol carbon, so it cannot be oxidized to a carbonyl (Chapter 4).

Practice (lecture style). An aldehyde is treated with [H]; the same aldehyde is treated with [O]. A ketone is treated with [H]; the same ketone with [O]. Products: aldehyde → 1° alcohol and carboxylic acid; ketone → 2° alcohol and no reaction. The matching-style question on the exam gives you exactly these four options.

Professor Tip

Oxidation and reduction are the same staircase walked in opposite directions: 1° alcohol ↔ aldehyde → carboxylic acid, and 2° alcohol ↔ ketone (stop). Every [O] step adds a C–O bond or removes a C–H; every [H] step does the reverse. If you can name the alcohol you would get back by reduction, you can also name the carbonyl you would get by oxidizing it.

The same redox in the body

Clinical Connection: Nursing

An aldehyde and the hangover. Alcohol dehydrogenase oxidizes ethanol (a 1° alcohol) to ethanal (acetaldehyde), the same alcohol → aldehyde oxidation you learned in Chapter 4, with NAD+ as the oxidizing agent the section describes. Aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH2) then oxidizes the aldehyde to acetate. Acetaldehyde is toxic and reactive; people with a common, less active ALDH2 variant accumulate it and experience facial flushing, a rapid heartbeat and nausea after a small drink, which is also how the disulfiram ("Antabuse") treatment mentioned in the section works. Testing for sugar. Copper-reduction tablets (Clinitest, a Benedict's-type reagent) detect any reducing sugar in urine, including galactose and lactose; glucose meters and dipsticks use the enzyme glucose oxidase and respond to glucose only (Chapter 8).

Summary

  • In organic chemistry, an oxidation increases C–O bonds or decreases C–H bonds; a reduction does the reverse. Write [O] or [H] over the arrow.
  • 1° alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid; 2° alcohol → ketone; 3° alcohols and ketones are not oxidized.
  • Reduction adds H across a C=C (hydrogenation, with Ni, Pd or Pt) or a C=O (aldehyde → 1° alcohol, ketone → 2° alcohol).
  • Tollens’ (silver mirror) and Benedict’s (brick-red Cu2O) tests detect aldehydes; ketones give no reaction.
  • In the body, NAD+/NADH carry out these oxidations and reductions, and two thiols are oxidized to a disulfide.
