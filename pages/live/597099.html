Learning Objectives

  • Explain, using the Brønsted–Lowry definition, what it means for an alcohol or a phenol to act as an acid.
  • Rank water, alcohols, thiols, phenols, and carboxylic acids from least to most acidic.
  • Explain in words why the phenoxide ion is more stable than an alkoxide ion.
  • Predict whether a given compound reacts with aqueous sodium hydroxide, and write the neutralization equation when it does.
  • Use the NaOH reaction as a laboratory test to distinguish a phenol from an alcohol.

Both ethanol and phenol contain an O–H group, and both can, in principle, give that hydrogen away as H+. In practice they behave very differently: ethanol is neutral in water and in your body, while phenol is acidic enough to burn skin and to dissolve in sodium hydroxide. The difference comes down to what is left behind after the proton leaves. This section gives you the order of acid strength, the reason behind it, and a bench test you will use in the lab. The same reasoning returns in Chapter 6, where carboxylic acids are the acids of organic chemistry, and in the pharmacy, where whether a drug carries a phenol group changes how it dissolves and how it is handled in the body.

Brønsted–Lowry acids: a short review

A Brønsted–Lowry acid is a proton (H+) donor; a base is a proton acceptor. When an acid HA gives its proton to water, the products are the hydronium ion and the conjugate base A−:

HA + H2O \(\rightleftharpoons\) H3O+ + A−

Strong acids (HCl, H2SO4) ionize completely. Weak acids ionize only slightly: the equilibrium lies mostly on the left, and most molecules keep their H. In this course you compare weak acids by ranking them, not with numbers: a stronger acid gives up its H+ more readily, and you can see the difference at the bench by which bases (sodium bicarbonate or sodium hydroxide) it reacts with.

The key idea that explains all of this section: an acid is stronger when its conjugate base is more stable. If the negative charge left behind is spread out or held by an electronegative atom, the molecule gives up its proton more readily. If the charge is stuck on one atom with nothing to help, the molecule holds on to its proton.

Alcohols are essentially neutral

An alcohol can lose the H of its O–H group to give an alkoxide ion, RO−:

CH3CH2OH + H2O \(\rightleftharpoons\) CH3CH2O− + H3O+

Ethanol is about as acidic as water itself, which is to say barely at all. Think about why: in the ethoxide ion the entire negative charge sits on one oxygen atom, exactly as it does in hydroxide, OH−. Nothing in the ethyl group helps carry the charge, so ethanol is no better at giving up a proton than water is. Alcohols with more alkyl branching, such as 2-methyl-2-propanol, are even a little weaker.

For a CHEM 3B student the practical conclusion is simple: treat alcohols as neutral. They do not turn litmus red, they do not change the pH of a solution, and they do not react with sodium hydroxide. An alkoxide is a stronger base than hydroxide, so the equilibrium between ethanol and NaOH lies on the ethanol side:

CH3CH2OH + NaOH → no reaction (the equilibrium strongly favors the reactants)

Phenols are weak acids

Phenol, C6H5OH, also loses the proton from its O–H group, giving the phenoxide ion, C6H5O−. Phenol is a weak acid: much weaker than acetic acid, but far more acidic than ethanol, and acidic enough to react with sodium hydroxide.

Why the big difference from ethanol? Look at the conjugate base. In ethoxide, the charge is trapped on oxygen. In phenoxide, the oxygen is bonded directly to the aromatic ring, and the ring's delocalized π electron system (Chapter 3) lets the extra electron pair spread out: part of the negative charge is shared with the ring carbons, especially the ones ortho and para to the oxygen. A charge spread over several atoms is more stable than a charge on one atom, so phenoxide forms more readily and phenol is the stronger acid. You do not need to draw the resonance structures for this course; what you need is the sentence: the aromatic ring delocalizes the negative charge of the phenoxide ion, so phenols are weak acids while alcohols are not.

Notice that this works only when the OH is bonded directly to a ring carbon. Benzyl alcohol, C6H5CH2OH, has a CH2 between the ring and the oxygen, so the ring cannot share the charge; benzyl alcohol behaves like any other alcohol.

Substituents change the acidity

Other groups on the ring shift a phenol's acidity a little, but every substituted phenol you will meet in this course (the cresols, thymol, eugenol, the phenol group of acetaminophen) stays a weak acid of about the same strength. For CHEM 3B, treat all phenols as weak acids that react with NaOH, and all alcohols as neutral compounds that do not.

Thiols: much more acidic than alcohols (close to phenols)

Thiols, R–SH, are about as acidic as phenols and much more acidic than alcohols. Sulfur is a larger atom than oxygen, so a negative charge on sulfur (the thiolate ion, RS−) is spread over a bigger volume and is more stable than the same charge on a small oxygen. Thiols therefore do react with NaOH to give sodium thiolate salts. In the body this matters for the amino acid cysteine (Chapter 10), whose SH group is the one most often found in an enzyme's active site.

Professor Tip

Anchor the ranking with two things you already know: vinegar (acetic acid) is obviously an acid, and rubbing alcohol obviously is not. Phenol sits in between: acidic enough to react with NaOH, not acidic enough to react with baking soda (NaHCO3). Everything else in this section follows from that one picture.

Reaction of phenol with sodium hydroxide: a lab test

Because phenol is an acid and hydroxide is a strong base, mixing them gives a classic neutralization: acid + base → salt + water.

C6H5OH + NaOH → C6H5O−Na+ + H2O

The salt, sodium phenoxide, is ionic, and like most sodium salts it dissolves freely in water. That change in solubility is what you see at the bench. Phenol itself is only moderately soluble in water (about 8 g per 100 mL), so a few drops of molten phenol added to water form a cloudy mixture or an oily layer. Add aqueous NaOH and the layer disappears: the phenol has been converted to its water-soluble salt. Cyclohexanol, an alcohol of about the same size, is also poorly soluble in water, but it is not an acid, so adding NaOH changes nothing; the alcohol layer remains.

The test is reversible, which is how chemists separate phenols from alcohols in a mixture. Dissolve the mixture in NaOH, and only the phenol goes into the water layer as its salt; the alcohol can be drawn off. Then add a stronger acid such as HCl to the water layer, and the phenol is regenerated:

C6H5O−Na+ + HCl → C6H5OH + NaCl

A weaker base is not enough. Sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3) neutralizes carboxylic acids but not the weaker phenols, so bicarbonate distinguishes a carboxylic acid from a phenol, and hydroxide distinguishes a phenol from an alcohol. You will use both ideas in the lab unknown.

In the Lab

In Experiment 4 you test each unknown with 5% NaOH: a phenol dissolves, an alcohol does not. Pair that result with the ferric chloride test (phenols give a purple to green color with Fe3+; alcohols do not) and the chromate oxidation test (1° and 2° alcohols turn the orange reagent green; 3° alcohols do not), and you can place an unknown in one of three boxes: 1°/2° alcohol, 3° alcohol, or phenol.

Worked examples

Example 1: Will it react with NaOH?

Problem. Predict whether each compound reacts with aqueous sodium hydroxide: (a) 2-propanol; (b) 4-methylphenol (p-cresol); (c) benzyl alcohol, C6H5CH2OH; (d) 1-butanethiol.

Solution. Ask one question for each: is the O–H (or S–H) acidic enough, that is, is it a phenol, a thiol or a carboxylic acid rather than an alcohol? (a) 2-Propanol is an alcohol: no reaction. (b) The OH of p-cresol is on the ring, so it is a phenol: reacts, giving sodium 4-methylphenoxide and water. (c) Benzyl alcohol has a CH2 between the ring and the OH; it is an alcohol: no reaction. (d) A thiol: reacts, giving sodium 1-butanethiolate and water.

Check. Only the compounds whose conjugate base has a way to stabilize its charge (the ring in b, the large sulfur atom in d) react. The presence of a benzene ring somewhere in the molecule (c) is not enough; the OH must be attached directly to it.

Example 2: Rank by acidity

Problem. Rank from least to most acidic: ethanol, acetic acid, phenol, 2-methyl-2-propanol.

Solution. Rank by family: alcohols are the weakest (about like water, and the more crowded 3° alcohol is weakest of all), phenols are weak acids, and carboxylic acids are the strongest organic acids, so: 2-methyl-2-propanol < ethanol < phenol < acetic acid.

Check. The order matches the stability of the conjugate bases: charge on a lone, crowded oxygen (the 3° alkoxide) < charge on a lone oxygen (ethoxide) < charge shared with a ring (phenoxide) < charge shared equally between two oxygens (acetate, Chapter 6). The two alcohols are so close that for this course both simply count as "neutral."

Example 3: Write the neutralization equation

Problem. Write the balanced equation for the reaction of 3-chlorophenol with sodium hydroxide, and name the organic product.

Solution. The phenol loses the H of its OH to hydroxide; the products are the sodium salt and water:

Cl–C6H4–OH + NaOH → Cl–C6H4–O−Na+ + H2O

The organic product is sodium 3-chlorophenoxide. The chlorine is a spectator here; it stays on the ring.

Check. Count atoms: six C, six H, one Cl, two O, and one Na on each side (the phenol contributes C6H5ClO and NaOH contributes NaOH; the products carry C6H4ClONa and H2O). The pattern is the same as every neutralization you learned in CHEM 3A: acid + base → salt + water.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

The phenol group shows up in drugs more often than you might expect, and its mild acidity is part of why. Acetaminophen carries a phenol OH (plus an amide, Chapter 7); that OH is the handle the liver uses to attach a sulfate or glucuronide group, making the drug water-soluble enough to be excreted in urine. Phenolic antiseptics and throat sprays rely on phenol's ability to pass through cell membranes (it is only weakly ionized at skin pH) and denature bacterial proteins; the same property is why concentrated phenol causes chemical burns and must be handled with gloves and eye protection. By contrast, ethanol is no more acidic than water, so it is never ionized at the body's pH of 7.4; ethanol's effects on a patient come from its oxidation by alcohol dehydrogenase and its action on the nervous system, not from any acidity.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Essential-oil mouthrinses contain the phenols thymol and, in some products, eugenol, along with the ether eucalyptol and the alcohol menthol. The phenols are the antiseptic part: slightly acidic, membrane-penetrating, and protein-denaturing, which is how they reduce the plaque biofilm that causes gingivitis (gum inflammation). The rinse is formulated near neutral pH, so the phenol stays mostly un-ionized and able to cross bacterial membranes. The same chemistry explains the warning on the label: phenols can irritate oral tissue, and patients with xerostomia (dry mouth) often cannot tolerate an alcohol-based phenolic rinse. Chlorhexidine, an amine-based antiseptic you will meet in Chapter 7, works by a different mechanism and is the usual prescription alternative.

Summary

  • A Brønsted–Lowry acid donates H+; a stronger acid gives it up more readily. An acid is stronger when its conjugate base is more stable.
  • Alcohols are about as acidic as water: for this course they are neutral and do not react with NaOH.
  • Phenols are weak acids because the aromatic ring delocalizes the negative charge of the phenoxide ion. A phenol solution has a pH below 7; an alcohol solution has about the same pH as water.
  • Thiols are much more acidic than alcohols, close to phenols, because the large sulfur atom spreads out the charge of the thiolate ion.
  • Phenol + NaOH → sodium phenoxide + water. The water-soluble salt dissolves, so NaOH solution distinguishes and separates phenols from alcohols; HCl regenerates the phenol.
  • Carboxylic acids are stronger still and are the only ones of these that react with bicarbonate (Chapter 6).

Exercises

Exercise 1

Rank from least to most acidic: ethanethiol, ethanol, acetic acid. Give a one-line reason for each step.

Answer
Ethanol < ethanethiol < acetic acid. Ethanol is an alcohol, about as acidic as water. Ethanethiol is a weak acid, about like phenol, because the large sulfur atom spreads out the charge of the thiolate ion. Acetic acid is a carboxylic acid, the strongest of the three, because the charge of acetate is shared by two oxygens.

Exercise 2

Write the equation for the ionization of 4-methylphenol in water and name the anion.

Answer
CH3C6H4OH + H2O \(\rightleftharpoons\) CH3C6H4O− + H3O+. The anion is the 4-methylphenoxide ion. The equilibrium lies far to the left because the phenol is a weak acid.

Exercise 3

Explain in one or two sentences why phenol is a much stronger acid than cyclohexanol, even though both have an OH on a six-carbon ring.

Answer
Phenol's ring is aromatic, so when the proton leaves, the negative charge of the phenoxide ion is delocalized over the oxygen and the ring carbons, which stabilizes the ion. Cyclohexanol's ring is saturated and cannot share the charge, so its alkoxide ion is much less stable and forms much less readily.

Exercise 4

Predict whether each reacts with aqueous NaOH, and write the products when it does: (a) 1-hexanol; (b) 2-chlorophenol; (c) ethanethiol; (d) cyclohexanol.

Answer
(a) No reaction (alcohol). (b) Reacts: sodium 2-chlorophenoxide, Cl–C6H4–O−Na+, plus H2O. (c) Reacts: sodium ethanethiolate, CH3CH2S−Na+, plus H2O. (d) No reaction (alcohol).

Exercise 5

Rank from weakest to strongest acid: 2-methyl-2-propanol, acetic acid, phenol, water.

Answer
2-Methyl-2-propanol < water < phenol < acetic acid. The carboxylic acid is strongest because its negative charge is shared equally by two oxygens (Chapter 6); the phenol comes next because its ring shares the charge; the alcohol and water keep the charge on a single oxygen.

Exercise 6

A test tube holds a mixture of phenol and 1-octanol in a little ether. Describe, in steps, how you could use NaOH solution and then HCl to separate the two compounds. Explain what happens at each step.

Answer
(1) Shake the mixture with aqueous NaOH. Phenol is neutralized to sodium phenoxide, an ionic salt that moves into the water layer; 1-octanol does not react and stays in the ether layer. (2) Separate the two layers; the ether layer now holds only 1-octanol. (3) Add HCl to the water layer. The stronger acid protonates the phenoxide ion, regenerating phenol, which is only slightly soluble and separates out: C6H5O−Na+ + HCl → C6H5OH + NaCl.

Exercise 7

An unknown dissolves in 5% NaOH but not in 5% NaHCO3, and it gives a purple color with FeCl3. Is it most likely an alcohol, a phenol, or a carboxylic acid? Explain.

Answer
A phenol. It is acidic enough to be neutralized by hydroxide (so not an alcohol) but not acidic enough for bicarbonate (so not a carboxylic acid, which would dissolve in NaHCO3). The FeCl3 color confirms a phenol.

Exercise 8

Acetaminophen contains a phenol OH, and ethanol contains an alcohol OH. At the body's pH of 7.4, which of the two is partly ionized? Which is not ionized at all for practical purposes? Explain.

Answer
Acetaminophen's phenol group is a weak acid, so at pH 7.4 only a small fraction of it (about 1%) is ionized; it is mostly un-ionized but measurably acidic. Ethanol is no more acidic than water, so essentially none of it is ionized; its acidity plays no role in the body.
