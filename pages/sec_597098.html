Learning Objectives

  • Identify thiols (mercaptans) by the presence of an SH group.
  • The mild oxidation of thiols gives disulfides.

Because sulfur is in the same group (6A) of the periodic table as oxygen, the two elements have some similar properties. We might expect sulfur to form organic compounds related to those of oxygen, and indeed it does. Thiols (also called mercaptans), which are sulfur analogs of alcohols, have the general formula RSH. Methanethiol (also called methyl mercaptan), has the formula CH3SH. Ethanethiol (ethyl mercaptan) is the most common odorant for liquid propane (LP) gas.

The mild oxidation of thiols gives compounds called disulfides.

\[\mathrm{2 RSH \xrightarrow{\,[O]\,} RSSR} \nonumber \]

The amino acids cysteine [HSCH2CH(NH2)COOH] and methionine [CH3SCH2CH2CH(NH2)COOH] contain sulfur atoms, as do all proteins that contain these amino acids. Disulfide linkages (–S–S–) between protein chains are extremely important in protein structure.

Thioethers, which are sulfur analogs of ethers, have the form general formula RSR′. An example is dimethylsulfide (CH3SCH3), which is responsible for the sometimes unpleasant odor of cooking cabbage and related vegetables. Note that methionine has a thioether functional group.

Career Spotlight: Paramedic

Paramedics give emergency care before a patient reaches the hospital. They treat burns from fuel and solvent fires (hydrocarbons, alcohols, ethers and esters) and alcohol and drug overdoses, and they give emergency drugs such as epinephrine. Knowing the functional groups in a substance helps predict how it burns, how it dissolves and how it acts in the body.

Summary

Thiols, thioethers, and disulfides are common in biological compounds.

Lecture Connection

Thiols are the sulfur twins of alcohols: name them like alcohols (with one spelling difference), expect weak intermolecular forces and strong odors, and know their one reaction, oxidation to a disulfide. That S–S bond is the same one that holds proteins in shape in Chapter 10.

Naming thiols

Thiols are named like alcohols: name the parent carbon chain, keep the full alkane name (do not drop the -e), add the suffix -thiol, and number the chain to give the SH the lowest number: CH3CH2SH is ethanethiol, CH3CH(SH)CH2CH3 is 2-butanethiol, (CH3)2CHCH2SH is 2-methyl-1-propanethiol. Common names use "mercaptan" (ethyl mercaptan), the word the section text uses and the one you will still see on gas-odorant and safety labels. Thiols are responsible for the smells of onions, garlic, rotten eggs and skunk spray, and methanethiol or ethanethiol is added to natural gas and LP gas so leaks can be smelled.

Example: Naming a thiol

Problem. Name CH3CH2CH(SH)CH3, and draw the condensed structure of 2-methyl-1-propanethiol.

Solution. The thiol has a four-carbon chain; number from the end nearer the SH so it lands on C2: 2-butanethiol. For 2-methyl-1-propanethiol, "propanethiol" means a three-carbon chain with SH on C1 and "2-methyl" puts a CH3 on C2: (CH3)2CHCH2SH.

Check. A thiol name keeps the "e" of the alkane (butane + thiol), unlike an alcohol name (butan + ol). "Butanthiol" is a spelling error that costs points.

Weak forces, strong smells

The S–H bond is barely polar, so thiols cannot hydrogen bond with each other and are weak even at dipole–dipole. Ethanethiol (62 g/mol) boils at 35 °C, more than 40 °C below the lighter ethanol (78 °C). Low intermolecular forces mean high volatility, which is exactly why thiols reach your nose so easily. Thiols are much more acidic than alcohols (about as acidic as phenol) but, like alcohols, far weaker acids than carboxylic acids; Section 4.10 puts them side by side.

Thiol oxidation and reduction

2 R–SH + [O] → R–S–S–R + H2O. Two thiol molecules lose their S–H hydrogens (they leave as water) and the sulfur atoms join to form a disulfide. The reaction fits the lecture's definition of oxidation (fewer bonds to hydrogen), and it runs backward under reducing conditions ([H]): disulfides are reduced back to thiols. This same S–S bond holds proteins in shape (the cysteine disulfide bridges of Chapter 10). When hair is "permed," the disulfide bonds in the hair are first reduced to thiols, the hair is wrapped on curlers, and the thiols are then oxidized to new disulfide bonds that lock in the curl.

Professor Tip

Thiol oxidation does not make a C=O. Alcohols oxidize at carbon (a C–OH becomes a C=O); thiols oxidize at sulfur (two S–H become one S–S). In this course, if a question asks for the oxidation product of a thiol, the answer is a disulfide, and the reactant is always two thiol molecules.

Thiols in the mouth

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Halitosis is thiol chemistry. Anaerobic bacteria on the tongue and in periodontal pockets break down sulfur-containing proteins (the cysteine and methionine the section text mentions) into volatile sulfur compounds: methanethiol (CH3SH), hydrogen sulfide (H2S) and dimethyl sulfide, the thioether named in the section. The low polarity and weak intermolecular forces you just learned for thiols are exactly why these molecules escape into the breath. Zinc-containing rinses work by binding the thiol sulfur as a nonvolatile zinc salt; thorough tongue cleaning removes the bacteria that make them.
