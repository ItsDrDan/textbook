Learning Objectives

  • Identify the general structure for an ester.
  • Use common names to name esters.
  • Name esters according to the IUPAC system.

Esters have the general formula RCOOR′, where R may be a hydrogen atom, an alkyl group, or an aryl group, and R′ may be an alkyl group or an aryl group but not a hydrogen atom. (If it were a hydrogen atom, the compound would be a carboxylic acid.) Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\) shows models for two common esters.

Esters occur widely in nature. Unlike carboxylic acids, esters generally have pleasant odors and are often responsible for the characteristic fragrances of fruits and flowers. Once a flower or fruit has been chemically analyzed, flavor chemists can attempt to duplicate the natural odor or taste. Both natural and synthetic esters are used in perfumes and as flavoring agents.

Fats and vegetable oils are esters of long-chain fatty acids and glycerol. Esters of phosphoric acid are of the utmost importance to life.

Names of Esters

Although esters are covalent compounds and salts are ionic, esters are named in a manner similar to that used for naming salts. The group name of the alkyl or aryl portion is given first and is followed by the name of the acid portion. In both common and International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) nomenclature, the -ic ending of the parent acid is replaced by the suffix -ate (Table \(\PageIndex{1}\)).

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Give the common and IUPAC names for each compound.

Solution

The alkyl group attached directly to the oxygen atom is a butyl group (in green).

The part of the molecule derived from the carboxylic acid (in red) has three carbon atoms. It is called propionate (common) or propanoate (IUPAC). The ester is therefore butyl propionate or butyl propanoate.

  1. An alkyl group (in green) is attached directly to the oxygen atom by its middle carbon atom; it is an isopropyl group. The part derived from the acid (that is, the benzene ring and the carbonyl group, in red) is benzoate. The ester is therefore isopropyl benzoate (both the common name and the IUPAC name).

Valley Connection: Tart Raisins and Fruity Peaches

The Fresno County town of Selma proudly calls itself the “Raisin Capital of the World.”1 California raisins come from Fresno, Madera, Tulare, Kern, and Kings Counties, with Fresno and Madera producing the most. About 1,500 growers make up the industry, and roughly 86% of the raisins sold in the United States are California raisins.2

The acid side. The main acid in grape berries is tartaric acid, or 2,3-dihydroxybutanedioic acid: a four-carbon dicarboxylic acid with two COOH groups and two OH groups. Few plants build up much of it. Grapes also make malic acid early on but break much of it down as they ripen, while tartaric acid is barely touched. It is stored in the berry as a potassium salt, a carboxylate salt like the ones you saw form when carboxylic acids react with bases. At harvest, grape juice holds about 4 to 8 grams of tartaric acid per liter, which helps give it a pH of roughly 2.9 to 3.8.3 That is why even very sweet, ripe grapes still carry a tart edge.

The ester side. Peaches are also among Fresno County’s top ten crops.4 A ripe peach smells sweet because of volatile esters. In one study of eight peach varieties, the compounds found in all of them included acetate esters such as butyl acetate, hexyl acetate, and cis-3-hexenyl acetate, and the lactone γ-decalactone was among the strongest contributors to aroma.5 Lactones are considered the classic “peach” compounds.6 A lactone is simply a cyclic ester: the –COO– link forms inside a ring when an OH group and a COOH group on the same molecule join.

Think about it: Hexyl acetate gives peaches part of their fruity smell, but you taste tartaric acid rather than smell it. Name the acid and alcohol that form hexyl acetate, and explain why the ester reaches your nose but tartaric acid does not.

Answer
Hexyl acetate forms from acetic acid and 1-hexanol. The ester has no O–H group, so its molecules cannot hydrogen-bond to each other; that makes it volatile enough to evaporate and reach your nose. Tartaric acid has two COOH and two OH groups, so it hydrogen-bonds strongly and is a nonvolatile solid. It stays in the fruit, where you sense it as sourness.

Lecture Connection

An ester name is two words, and the lecture's trick for getting them in the right order is to find the C=O: the side with the C=O came from the acid and gives the second word (-oate); the group on the single-bonded oxygen came from the alcohol and gives the first word (-yl). The same split lets you "un-make" any ester, which is all of Section 6.8 in one move.

Reading and naming an ester

An ester is abbreviated RCOOR′ or RCO2R′ (CH3COOCH2CH3 is ethyl acetate); the first O after the C is the C=O and the second is single-bonded. Unlike a carboxylic acid, an ester can be cyclic, because its single-bonded O can be part of a ring (a lactone, Section 6.7). Naming takes two words:

  • First word: the name of R′, the group attached to the single-bonded O, with a -yl ending.
  • Second word: the name of the acid part (RCOO–, the acyl group plus its oxygen) with the -oate suffix (IUPAC) or -ate on the common acid name (acetate, butyrate), as in the section's table.

Each word can carry its own prefixes for substituents on that chain: ethyl 2-methylbutanoate; 2-chloroethyl benzoate. Examples: methyl ethanoate (methyl acetate); pentyl ethanoate (pentyl acetate, the smell of pears).

Professor Tip

In an ester, the side with the C=O came from the carboxylic acid. The side attached through the single-bonded O came from the alcohol. Find the C=O, split the molecule at the single-bonded O, and you can name it or "un-make" it in seconds.

Example: Naming esters from their structures

Problem. Name (a) CH3CH2CH2COOCH2CH3 (the pineapple-scented ester) and (b) the wintergreen ester whose structure is a benzene ring carrying –COOCH3 on one carbon and –OH on the carbon next to it.

Solution. (a) Find the C=O. The carbon chain on the C=O side has four carbons (counting the C=O carbon), so the acid part is butanoate. The group on the single-bonded oxygen is CH2CH3, an ethyl group. Alcohol part first: ethyl butanoate. (b) The C=O is attached to the ring, so the acid part is benzoate, and the group on the single-bonded oxygen is methyl: methyl benzoate. The ring also carries an –OH on C2 (C1 is the ring carbon with the ester): methyl 2-hydroxybenzoate, better known as methyl salicylate.

Check. Un-make each ester. (a) Ethanol + butanoic acid: 2 + 4 carbons = 6, matching the formula. (b) Methanol + salicylic acid (2-hydroxybenzoic acid): the –OH on the ring is a phenol, which stays untouched; only the COOH was esterified. The names rebuild the same pieces, so they are consistent.

Example: Ester names with substituents on both halves

Problem. (a) Name CH3CH(CH3)CH2COOCH2CH2Cl. (b) Draw 1-methylethyl 3-oxobutanoate.

Solution. (a) Find the C=O and split at the single-bonded O. Acid side (counting the C=O carbon): four carbons with a methyl on C3, so the acyl word is 3-methylbutanoate. Alcohol side: a two-carbon group with Cl on the carbon farther from the oxygen (C2 of that group): 2-chloroethyl. Name: 2-chloroethyl 3-methylbutanoate. (b) The second word, 3-oxobutanoate, is a four-carbon acyl group with a ketone C=O on C3: CH3COCH2C(=O)O–. The first word, 1-methylethyl (the IUPAC spelling of isopropyl), is attached to the single-bonded oxygen through its middle carbon: CH3COCH2COOCH(CH3)2.

Check. Each word is numbered separately, each from its own attachment point: the acyl chain from the C=O carbon, the alkyl group from the carbon bonded to the oxygen. Un-make (a): 2-chloroethanol + 3-methylbutanoic acid; the carbons add up to 2 + 5 = 7, matching the formula.

Isomers of acids and esters

With enough carbons, both acids and esters have skeletal isomers (butanoic acid and 2-methylpropanoic acid). The COOH group itself has no positional isomers, since it is always C1 (other substituents on the chain can still move); esters do, because the ester link can sit between different carbons of the chain (methyl propanoate and ethyl ethanoate are both C4H8O2). An acid and an ester with the same number of carbons are functional group isomers: butanoic acid and methyl propanoate are both C4H8O2.

Example: Classifying isomers of acids and esters

Problem. Classify each pair as skeletal isomers, positional isomers, functional group isomers or the same compound: (a) pentanoic acid and 3-methylbutanoic acid; (b) methyl butanoate and ethyl propanoate; (c) ethyl propanoate and pentanoic acid; (d) CH3CH2COOCH3 and CH3OOCCH2CH3.

Solution. (a) Both are C5H10O2 acids; one chain is straight, the other branched: skeletal isomers. (b) Both are C5H10O2 esters with five carbons in a row, but the ester link sits after the fourth carbon in one and after the third in the other: positional isomers. (c) Same formula, C5H10O2, different functional groups: functional group isomers. (d) The second formula is the first written from the other end (CH3O–C(=O)–CH2CH3): the same compound, methyl propanoate.

Check. The COOH group of a carboxylic acid cannot change position, because it is always C1 (substituents on the chain still can: 2-chloro- and 3-chlorobutanoic acid); the ester linkage can, because the single-bonded O can sit between different carbons. Always count the carbons on each side of the C=O before deciding.

Esters in the operatory

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Most of the plastics in a dental operatory are esters. Tooth-colored composite fillings are made from methacrylate esters such as bis-GMA (a large dimethacrylate) mixed with glass filler; the blue curing light starts an addition polymerization of the C=C bonds (Chapter 3), and the ester groups stay in the hardened resin. Acrylic denture bases are PMMA, poly(methyl methacrylate), polymerized from methyl methacrylate, the methyl ester of 2-methylpropenoic acid. A small amount of unreacted monomer can remain in a new denture or a chairside reline, and the ester monomer is what causes the burning sensation or contact sensitivity some patients report; soaking the appliance in water for a day lets the residual monomer leach out. Before the composite is placed, the enamel is roughened with 37% phosphoric acid (the acid-etch step), which dissolves a few micrometers of hydroxyapatite in the same way plaque acid does (Section 6.4), but under control. Two more esters you will handle: benzocaine gel, the ethyl ester of PABA used to numb tissue before scaling or injections (its hydrolysis is in Section 6.8), and methyl salicylate (oil of wintergreen), the ester of salicylic acid and methanol that flavors many mouthwashes.
