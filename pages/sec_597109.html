Learning Objectives

  • Name carboxylic acids with common names.
  • Name carboxylic acids according to IUPAC nomenclature.

Carboxylic acids occur widely in nature, often combined with alcohols or other functional groups, as in fats, oils, and waxes. They are components of many foods, medicines, and household products (Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\)). Not surprisingly, many of them are best known by common names based on Latin and Greek words that describe their source.

The simplest carboxylic acid, formic acid (HCOOH), was first obtained by the distillation of ants (Latin formica, meaning “ant”). The bites and stings of some ants inject formic acid.

The next higher homolog is acetic acid, which is made by fermenting cider and honey in the presence of oxygen. This fermentation produces vinegar, a solution containing 4%–10% acetic acid, plus a number of other compounds that add to its flavor. Acetic acid is probably the most familiar weak acid used in educational and industrial chemistry laboratories.

Pure acetic acid solidifies at 16.6°C, only slightly below normal room temperature. In the poorly heated laboratories of the late 19th and early 20th centuries in northern North America and Europe, acetic acid often “froze” on the storage shelf. For that reason, pure acetic acid (sometimes called concentrated acetic acid) came to be known as glacial acetic acid, a name that survives to this day.

The third homolog, propionic acid (CH3CH2COOH), is seldom encountered in everyday life. The fourth homolog, butyric acid (CH3CH2CH2COOH), is one of the most foul-smelling substances imaginable. It is found in rancid butter and is one of the ingredients of body odor. By recognizing extremely small amounts of this and other chemicals, bloodhounds are able to track fugitives. Models of the first four carboxylic acids are shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\).

The acid with the carboxyl group attached directly to a benzene ring is called benzoic acid (C6H5COOH).

The common names of carboxylic acids use Greek letters (α, β, γ, δ, and so forth), not numbers, to designate the position of substituent groups in acids. These letters refer to the position of the carbon atom in relation to the carboxyl carbon atom. On exams, use the IUPAC name with numbers (4-chlorobutanoic acid, not γ-chlorobutyric acid); the letters α and β are still used in biochemistry to label the first two carbons after the carboxyl group.

In the nomenclature system of the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC), the parent hydrocarbon is the one that corresponds to the longest continuous chain (LCC) containing the carboxyl group. The -e ending of the parent alkane is replaced by the suffix -oic and the word acid. For example, the carboxylic acid derived from pentane is pentanoic acid (CH3CH2CH2CH2COOH). As with aldehydes, the carboxyl carbon atom is counted first; numbers are used to indicate any substituted carbon atoms in the parent chain.

Greek letters are used with common names; numbers are used with IUPAC names.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Give the common and IUPAC names for each compound.

  1. ClCH2CH2CH2COOH

Solution

  1. The LCC contains four carbon atoms; the compound is therefore named as a substituted butyric (or butanoic) acid.

    The chlorine atom is attached to the γ-carbon in the common system or C4 in the IUPAC system. The compound is γ-chlorobutyric acid or 4-chlorobutanoic acid.

  2. The LCC contains four carbon atoms; the compound is therefore named as a substituted butyric (or butanoic) acid.

The bromine (Br) atom is at the α-carbon in the common system or C2 in the IUPAC system. The compound is α-bromobutyric acid or 2-bromobutanoic acid.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Write the condensed structural formula for β-chloropropionic acid.

Solution

Propionic acid has three carbon atoms: C–C–COOH. Attach a chlorine (Cl) atom to the parent chain at the beta carbon atom, the second one from the carboxyl group: Cl–C–C–COOH. Then add enough hydrogen atoms to give each carbon atom four bonds: ClCH2CH2COOH.

Key Takeaways

  • Simple carboxylic acids are best known by common names based on Latin and Greek words that describe their source (e.g., formic acid, Latin formica, meaning “ant”).
  • Greek letters, not numbers, designate the position of substituted acids in the common naming convention.
  • IUPAC names are derived from the LCC of the parent hydrocarbon with the -e ending of the parent alkane replaced by the suffix -oic and the word acid.

Lecture Connection

The lecture introduces carboxylic acids, esters and (next chapter) amides as one family built on the acyl group, R–C(=O)–. Naming an acid is the easiest nomenclature in the course, because the –COOH carbon is always C1 and always wins the suffix; the work is in the prefixes, which now include hydroxy-, alkoxy- and oxo-.

The acyl group family

Every compound in this chapter, and the amides of Chapter 7, is built on the same unit: a carbon double-bonded to an oxygen and bonded to an R group, with one more bond available. This unit, R–C(=O)–, is called an acyl group. What is attached to the acyl carbon decides the family. An –OH gives a carboxylic acid, an –OR′ gives an ester, and an –NH2 (or –NHR, –NR2) gives an amide. (An acyl group bonded to a chlorine is an acid chloride, a reactive lab reagent you will not need to name.) The acid, ester and amide are often called carboxylic acid derivatives because an acid can be converted into each of the others by swapping the group on the acyl carbon.

Condensed formulas. A carboxylic acid's carboxyl group is abbreviated RCOOH or RCO2H (CH3CH2COOH is propanoic acid). In the abbreviation, the first O after the C is the C=O and the second is single-bonded. A carboxyl group is always terminal, at the end of a chain, so the COOH carbon can never be part of a ring and never needs a position number. It follows that carboxylic acids have skeletal isomers (butanoic acid and 2-methylpropanoic acid) but no positional isomers of the COOH group itself; an acid and an ester with the same formula are functional group isomers (Section 6.5).

Two more words you will hear in the metabolism chapters: the carbon next to the –COOH group is the α (alpha) carbon, and the carbon bonded to the α carbon is the β (beta) carbon (β-oxidation, β-hydroxybutyrate).

Naming carboxylic acids

  1. Find the longest chain containing the –COOH group.
  2. –COOH is the highest-priority functional group of all, and it is always C1, so omit the 1 from the name.
  3. Change the -e of the parent alkane to -oic acid.
  4. Apply the other nomenclature rules to build the prefix: CH3CH(CH3)CH(CH3)CH2CH2COOH is 4,5-dimethylhexanoic acid.

Common names to know: formic acid (methanoic; from the Latin formica, ant), acetic acid (ethanoic; vinegar) and benzoic acid. A carboxyl group on a benzene ring is benzoic acid; the ring carbon attached to the carboxyl carbon is C1, and the other aromatic nomenclature rules apply: 2-bromobenzoic acid.

Substituents and benzoic acid derivatives

Because carboxylic acids have the highest naming priority, every other group appears as a prefix, with the same prefixes you used for aldehydes and ketones: a ketone C=O becomes oxo- and an –OH becomes hydroxy-. CH3COCH2COOH is 3-oxobutanoic acid (acetoacetic acid, a ketone body you will meet in Chapter 15). CH3CH(OH)COOH is 2-hydroxypropanoic acid (lactic acid). A short checklist for naming a substituted acid:

  1. Find the longest chain that contains the COOH carbon; that carbon is always C1, so no position number is written for the acid itself.
  2. Name each substituent with its prefix and locant: chloro-, bromo-, fluoro- (halogens); methyl-, ethyl- (alkyl); hydroxy- (–OH); methoxy-, ethoxy- (–OR, an alkoxy group); oxo- (a C=O inside the chain).
  3. List the prefixes alphabetically (ignore di-, tri-), then the chain name ending in -oic acid.

Examples: CH3CH(OCH3)COOH is 2-methoxypropanoic acid; CH3CHBrCH2CH2COOH is 4-bromopentanoic acid; CH3COCH(CH3)COOH is 2-methyl-3-oxobutanoic acid. Ring acids are named as derivatives of benzoic acid, with the ring carbon carrying the COOH as C1 and the lowest possible numbers for the other groups; ortho/meta/para are still used in common names. 2-Hydroxybenzoic acid is salicylic acid (the parent of aspirin), and 4-aminobenzoic acid is PABA (released when benzocaine is hydrolyzed, Section 6.8).

Acids you will meet by their common names

Several small carboxylic acids have common names that you will hear far more often than their IUPAC names, in the kitchen, the clinic and the metabolism chapters. The table lists the ones to know and where each turns up in the body. At body pH (about 7.4) each exists mainly as its carboxylate ion (Section 6.10), which is why clinical reports say lactate, citrate and pyruvate rather than the acid names.
