So far we have represented monosaccharides as linear molecules, but many of them also adopt cyclic structures. This conversion occurs because of the ability of aldehydes and ketones to react with alcohols:

You might wonder why the aldehyde reacts with the OH group on the fifth carbon atom rather than the OH group on the second carbon atom next to it. Recall that cyclic alkanes containing five or six carbon atoms in the ring are the most stable. The same is true for monosaccharides that form cyclic structures: rings consisting of five or six carbon atoms are the most stable.

When a straight-chain monosaccharide, such as any of the structures shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\), forms a cyclic structure, the carbonyl oxygen atom may be pushed either up or down, giving rise to two stereoisomers, as shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\). The structure shown on the left side of Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\), with the OH group on the first carbon atom projected downward, represents what is called the alpha (α) form. The structure on the right side, with the OH group on the first carbon atom pointed upward, is the beta (β) form. These two stereoisomers of a cyclic monosaccharide are known as anomers; they differ in structure around the anomeric carbon—that is, the carbon atom that was the carbonyl carbon atom in the straight-chain form.

It is possible to obtain a sample of crystalline glucose in which all the molecules have the α structure or all have the β structure. The α form melts at 146°C and has a specific rotation of +112°, while the β form melts at 150°C and has a specific rotation of +18.7°. When the sample is dissolved in water, however, a mixture is soon produced containing both anomers as well as the straight-chain form, in dynamic equilibrium (part (a) of Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\)). You can start with a pure crystalline sample of glucose consisting entirely of either anomer, but as soon as the molecules dissolve in water, they open to form the carbonyl group and then reclose to form either the α or the β anomer. The opening and closing repeats continuously in an ongoing interconversion between anomeric forms and is referred to as mutarotation (Latin mutare, meaning “to change”). At equilibrium, the mixture consists of about 36% α-D-glucose, 64% β-D-glucose, and less than 0.02% of the open-chain aldehyde form. The observed rotation of this solution is +52.7°.

Even though only a small percentage of the molecules are in the open-chain aldehyde form at any time, the solution will nevertheless exhibit the characteristic reactions of an aldehyde. As the small amount of free aldehyde is used up in a reaction, there is a shift in the equilibrium to yield more aldehyde. Thus, all the molecules may eventually react, even though very little free aldehyde is present at a time.

Commonly, (e.g., in Figures \(\PageIndex{1}\) and \(\PageIndex{2}\)) the cyclic forms of sugars are depicted using a convention first suggested by Walter N. Haworth, an English chemist. The molecules are drawn as planar hexagons with a darkened edge representing the side facing toward the viewer. The structure is simplified to show only the functional groups attached to the carbon atoms. Any group written to the right in a Fischer projection appears below the plane of the ring in a Haworth projection, and any group written to the left in a Fischer projection appears above the plane in a Haworth projection.

The difference between the α and the β forms of sugars may seem trivial, but such structural differences are often crucial in biochemical reactions. This explains why we can get energy from the starch in potatoes and other plants but not from cellulose, even though both starch and cellulose are polysaccharides composed of glucose molecules linked together.

Summary

Monosaccharides that contain five or more carbon atoms form cyclic structures in aqueous solution. Two cyclic stereoisomers can form from each straight-chain monosaccharide; these are known as anomers. In an aqueous solution, an equilibrium mixture forms between the two anomers and the straight-chain structure of a monosaccharide in a process known as mutarotation.

Lecture Connection

In lecture the ring is "hemiacetals again": one of the sugar's own OH groups adds to its own C=O, exactly the Chapter 5 reaction, and the new chiral carbon it creates (the anomeric carbon) can be α or β. The skill the exam tests is converting a Fischer projection to a Haworth ring and back, in three steps.

Why the ring forms, and which ring

Recall from Chapter 5 that when an alcohol adds to an aldehyde, a hemiacetal forms. In water, one of a sugar's own OH groups attacks its C=O (C1 in glucose) and forms a cyclic hemiacetal. Use the alcohol that will make a six-membered ring (five-membered rings sometimes form). The old carbonyl carbon becomes a new chiral center, the anomeric carbon (the "goggle eyes" carbon from Chapter 5). This is the same reaction you learned for aldehydes and ketones, just happening inside one molecule: an alcohol adds to a carbonyl to give a carbon with both an OH and an OR (an intramolecular hemiacetal). For glucose the attacking OH is on C5, so the ring contains five carbons plus one oxygen: a six-membered pyranose ring (named for the cyclic ether pyran). For fructose the ketone is at C2; when the C5 OH attacks it, the ring is five-membered (four carbons plus the oxygen), a furanose (named for furan), with the anomeric carbon at C2. This is the fructose ring you will draw and the one found in sucrose. Open-chain and ring forms are in equilibrium, but in water almost all of the sugar (more than 99%) is in a ring at any moment.

Haworth projections and the α/β call

  • A Haworth projection shows the ring as a flat hexagon (or pentagon) with vertical lines for the groups; the real molecule is not flat. Groups on the right in the Fischer projection point down, and groups on the left point up.
  • The new OH on the anomeric carbon is α if it is trans to the CH2OH (for a D-sugar: down, opposite the CH2OH) and β if it is cis to the CH2OH (up, same side as the CH2OH).
  • α and β forms interconvert in solution through the open chain; the cyclization exists as an equilibrium called mutarotation.

Professor Tip

Two things to read off any Haworth drawing. (1) The back CH2OH: D sugars have it up, L sugars have it down. (2) The anomeric OH compared with that CH2OH: the symbol α crosses over itself, so α = trans (opposite sides); the symbol β has both bumps on the same side, so β = cis (same side). Say "CH2OH up, OH up, same side: beta" and you will not mix them up.

Haworth projection rules (the ring drawn flat, viewed edge-on, with the ring oxygen at the back right and C1 at the right):

  1. Number clockwise starting from the anomeric carbon (C1) at the right.
  2. Right in Fischer → down in Haworth; left in Fischer → up. For D-glucose: C2 OH down, C3 OH up, C4 OH down.
  3. For every D sugar the terminal CH2OH (C6) points up on C5. (It points down for an L sugar.)
  4. The new anomeric OH on C1 can be down (α) or up (β). The two anomers are not enantiomers of each other; they differ only at the one new chiral center created when the ring closed.

From Fischer to Haworth in three steps, and back

This is the way it is done in lecture; a color-coded handout is in the Chapter 8 module in Canvas, and the videos in the Chapter Guide walk through glucose, mannose, and a ketose.

  1. Rotate the Fischer projection onto its side so the C=O is on the right. Everything that was on the left of the Fischer projection is now up; everything that was on the right is now down.
  2. Twist the chain around into a hexagon. By convention the carbonyl carbon is the right-most point of the ring. The CH2OH ends up up for a D sugar and down for an L sugar.
  3. Close the ring: the C5 OH bonds to C1, and the new OH on C1 is drawn down (α) or up (β). Draw both when asked for "the anomers." Practice: draw both anomers of D-mannose.

From Haworth back to Fischer: rotate the ring 90° counterclockwise in your mind so that C1 is at the top, then read each position: up in the Haworth becomes left in the Fischer, down becomes right. The CH2OH tells you D (was up) or L (was down), and the anomeric OH tells you α or β. Practice: a galactose ring drawn with the CH2OH up and the anomeric OH up is ___-D-galactose or ___-L-galactose? CH2OH up means D; anomeric OH up, same side as the CH2OH, means β.

Mutarotation

If you dissolve pure crystalline α-D-glucose in water, within an hour or two the solution contains both anomers. The ring is opening to the free aldehyde and re-closing, and each time it re-closes the OH can end up down or up. The result is the equilibrium mixture in the figure above (about 36% α, 64% β). The open chain is a tiny fraction, but it matters: it is the form that can be oxidized (Section 8.5) and the form that reacts with proteins (see the nursing box).

Clinical Connection: Nursing

HbA1c (glycated hemoglobin) is open-chain chemistry inside a red blood cell: the small open-chain fraction of glucose has a free aldehyde, which reacts with an amine group of hemoglobin (the N-terminal valine of the β chain) and stays attached for the life of the cell, about 120 days. Higher average glucose means a larger glycated fraction, so HbA1c (normal below 5.7%; 6.5% or higher supports a diagnosis of diabetes) reports the average over two to three months, not today's value. Mutarotation keeps feeding that trace of aldehyde, which is why even a sugar that is 99.9% ring can still do aldehyde chemistry.

Example 1: α or β?

Problem. In a Haworth drawing of D-glucose, the CH2OH points up and the OH on C1 points down. Which anomer is it?

Solution

Anomeric OH opposite the CH2OH (down) = α-D-glucose.

Check. For a D sugar the CH2OH is up, so "OH down" and "opposite the CH2OH" say the same thing: α.

Example 2: Haworth projection of a ketose

Problem. Draw β-D-fructose as a five-membered (furanose) ring. The Fischer projection of D-fructose has CH2OH at C1, C=O at C2, and OH groups left (C3), right (C4), right (C5), with CH2OH at C6.

Solution. The ring forms when the C5 OH adds to the C2 ketone, so the ring contains C2, C3, C4, C5, and the oxygen: a furanose. Draw the pentagon with the ring O at the back and the anomeric carbon (C2) at the right. Apply the usual rules: right in Fischer → down, left → up. C3 OH (left) is up; C4 OH (right) is down. C5 is in the ring and carries the rest of the chain, C6 CH2OH, which points up because the sugar is D. The anomeric carbon C2 carries two groups: the C1 CH2OH and the new OH. For the β anomer the OH is up, cis to the C6 CH2OH, so the C1 CH2OH goes down.

Check. A ketose ring has an extra CH2OH hanging on the anomeric carbon; that is how you spot a fructose unit in sucrose. Count: C1 and C6 outside the ring, C2–C5 inside, six carbons total.

Example 3: Haworth projection of an L sugar

Problem. Draw α-L-galactose.

Solution. Start from D-galactose (right, left, left, right) and flip every center to get L-galactose: left, right, right, left. Now convert: left → up, right → down, so C2 OH up, C3 OH down, C4 OH down. The C5 OH is on the left, so the C6 CH2OH points down (L). For α the anomeric OH is trans to the CH2OH; since the CH2OH is down, the α OH is up.

Check. The whole drawing is the α-D-galactose drawing turned upside down position by position, which is what a mirror image should look like. The CH2OH down is the giveaway for L; then "α = trans" places the anomeric OH.
