Learning Objectives

  • To explain how an amino acid can act as both an acid and a base.

The structure of an amino acid allows it to act as both an acid and a base. An amino acid has this ability because at a certain pH value (different for each amino acid) nearly all the amino acid molecules exist as zwitterions. If acid is added to a solution containing the zwitterion, the carboxylate group captures a hydrogen (H+) ion, and the amino acid becomes positively charged. If base is added, ion removal of the H+ ion from the amino group of the zwitterion produces a negatively charged amino acid. In both circumstances, the amino acid acts to maintain the pH of the system—that is, to remove the added acid (H+) or base (OH−) from solution.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\)

  1. Draw the structure for the anion formed when glycine (at neutral pH) reacts with a base.
  2. Draw the structure for the cation formed when glycine (at neutral pH) reacts with an acid.

Solution

  1. The base removes H+ from the protonated amine group.

  • The acid adds H+ to the carboxylate group.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)
  1. Draw the structure for the cation formed when valine (at neutral pH) reacts with an acid.
  2. Draw the structure for the anion formed when valine (at neutral pH) reacts with a base.

The particular pH at which a given amino acid exists in solution as a zwitterion is called the isoelectric point (pI). At its pI, the positive and negative charges on the amino acid balance, and the molecule as a whole is electrically neutral. The amino acids whose side chains are always neutral have isoelectric points ranging from 5.0 to 6.5. The basic amino acids (which have positively charged side chains at neutral pH) have relatively high isoelectric points. Acidic amino acids (which have negatively charged side chains at neutral pH) have quite low isoelectric points (Table \(\PageIndex{1}\)).

Table \(\PageIndex{1}\): Examples of Some Representative Amino Acids
Amino Acid Classification pI
alanine nonpolar 6.0
valine nonpolar 6.0
serine polar, uncharged 5.7
threonine polar, uncharged 5.6
arginine positively charged (basic) 10.8
histidine positively charged (basic) 7.6
lysine positively charged (basic) 9.8
aspartic acid negatively charged (acidic) 3.0
glutamic acid negatively charged (acidic) 3.2

Amino acids undergo reactions characteristic of carboxylic acids and amines. The reactivity of these functional groups is particularly important in linking amino acids together to form peptides and proteins, as you will see later in this chapter. Simple chemical tests that are used to detect amino acids take advantage of the reactivity of these functional groups. An example is the ninhydrin test in which the amine functional group of α-amino acids reacts with ninhydrin to form purple-colored compounds. Ninhydrin is used to detect fingerprints because it reacts with amino acids from the proteins in skin cells transferred to the surface by the individual leaving the fingerprint.

Summary

Amino acids can act as both an acid and a base due to the presence of the amino and carboxyl functional groups. The pH at which a given amino acid exists in solution as a zwitterion is called the isoelectric point (pI).

Lecture Connection

An amino acid does the chemistry of its two functional groups, and you already know both: the amine neutralizes acids and the carboxylic acid neutralizes bases (Section 10.2), and the two of them condense to form an amide, which between amino acids is called a peptide bond (Section 10.4). The reactions we use in lab to detect amino acids and proteins come from the same two groups.

Three reactions, two functional groups

  • Acid–base. Adding H+ protonates the carboxylate; adding base deprotonates the ammonium group. Nothing new forms, only a different charge state. This is the chemistry of pI, worked out in Section 10.2.
  • Condensation to a peptide. The –COO− of one amino acid and the –NH3+ of the next lose water and form an amide. This is the only reaction that builds a protein, and hydrolysis is its reverse (Section 10.4).
  • Oxidation of cysteine. Two –SH groups are oxidized to a disulfide, –S–S–, the same thiol chemistry you met in Chapter 4. In a protein this is the disulfide bond of tertiary structure (Section 10.8).

Precipitating a protein at its pI

A protein is least soluble at its isoelectric point: with no net charge, the molecules stop repelling one another and clump. Milk's main protein, casein, has a pI near 4.6, so adding acid to milk drop by drop until the pH reaches 4.6 curdles it; the curd is almost pure casein. The same chemistry is behind cheese making, yogurt (bacteria make the lactic acid), and the way stomach acid curdles milk protein. Heat is not needed; the change in charge is enough.

In the Lab

  • Experiment 11 – Amino Acids: classify amino acids by R group, then separate them by paper chromatography, make the spots visible with ninhydrin (it reacts with the free α-amino group to give a purple product, the reaction the section text describes), and identify an unknown from its Rf value (distance moved by the spot ÷ distance moved by the solvent). Polar, charged side chains stick to the wet paper and travel less; nonpolar side chains travel farther with the solvent.
  • Experiment 12 – Peptides and Proteins (part 1): isolate casein from milk by adding acid until it reaches its isoelectric point, then tell amino acids from proteins with two color tests. Ninhydrin needs a free amino group, so it is strongest for free amino acids. The biuret test needs two or more peptide bonds, so blue Cu2+ turns violet only with peptides and proteins, not with free amino acids. The denaturation part of this experiment belongs with Section 10.10.
