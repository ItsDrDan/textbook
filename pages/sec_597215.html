Learning Objectives
  • List the steps of protein digestion.

Protein digestion begins in the stomach (Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\)), where the action of gastric juice hydrolyzes about 10% of the peptide bonds. Gastric juice is a mixture of water (more than 99%), inorganic ions, hydrochloric acid, and various enzymes and other proteins.

The hydrochloric acid (HCl) in gastric juice is secreted by glands in the stomach lining. The pH of freshly secreted gastric juice is about 1.0, but the contents of the stomach may raise the pH to between 1.5 and 2.5. HCl helps to denature food proteins; that is, it unfolds the protein molecules to expose their chains to more efficient enzyme action. The principal digestive component of gastric juice is pepsinogen, an inactive enzyme produced in cells located in the stomach wall. When food enters the stomach after a period of fasting, pepsinogen is converted to its active form—pepsin—in a series of steps initiated by the drop in pH. Pepsin catalyzes the hydrolysis of peptide linkages within protein molecules. It has a fairly broad specificity but acts preferentially on linkages involving the aromatic amino acids tryptophan, tyrosine, and phenylalanine, as well as methionine and leucine.

Protein digestion is completed in the small intestine. Pancreatic juice, carried from the pancreas via the pancreatic duct, contains inactive enzymes such as trypsinogen and chymotrypsinogen. They are activated in the small intestine as follows (Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\)): The intestinal mucosal cells secrete the proteolytic enzyme enteropeptidase, which converts trypsinogen to trypsin; trypsin then activates chymotrypsinogen to chymotrypsin (and also completes the activation of trypsinogen). Both of these active enzymes catalyze the hydrolysis of peptide bonds in protein chains. Chymotrypsin preferentially attacks peptide bonds involving the carboxyl groups of the aromatic amino acids (phenylalanine, tryptophan, and tyrosine). Trypsin attacks peptide bonds involving the carboxyl groups of the basic amino acids (lysine and arginine). Pancreatic juice also contains procarboxypeptidase, which is cleaved by trypsin to carboxypeptidase. The latter is an enzyme that catalyzes the hydrolysis of peptide linkages at the free carboxyl end of the peptide chain, resulting in the stepwise liberation of free amino acids from the carboxyl end of the polypeptide.

Aminopeptidases in the intestinal juice remove amino acids from the N-terminal end of peptides and proteins possessing a free amino group. Figure \(\PageIndex{3}\) illustrates the specificity of these protein-digesting enzymes. The amino acids that are released by protein digestion are absorbed across the intestinal wall into the circulatory system, where they can be used for protein synthesis.

Lecture Connection

Proteins supply only about 10% of the body’s energy; their main value is as a source of amino acids for protein synthesis and for the other nitrogen-containing molecules the cell needs. The lecture wants the digestion sequence (where, which enzyme, what it cuts), then two ideas that have no chemistry of their own but show up on every exam: the amino acid pool and nitrogen balance.

Digestion, step by step

  • Mouth. Saliva contains no enzyme that hydrolyzes proteins, so protein digestion starts in the stomach.
  • Stomach. Hydrochloric acid (pH about 2) denatures the protein, unfolding it so the enzymes can reach the backbone, and activates the inactive pepsinogen into pepsin, which hydrolyzes proteins into polypeptides.
  • Small intestine. The pancreas secretes trypsin, chymotrypsin, and carboxypeptidase as inactive zymogens so that they do not digest the pancreas itself. Each has its own specificity: trypsin cleaves at the carboxyl side of lysine and arginine (the amino acids with a 1° amine in the side chain); chymotrypsin cleaves at the carboxyl side of the large hydrophobic residues tyrosine, tryptophan, phenylalanine, and leucine; carboxypeptidase removes amino acids one at a time from the C-terminus; and aminopeptidase removes them from the N-terminus.
  • Absorption. The free amino acids move into the bloodstream by active transport through the intestinal wall (Chapter 9) and travel in the portal vein to the liver.

The amino acid pool and nitrogen balance

The amino acid pool is the total supply of free amino acids available for use in the body. It has three sources: dietary proteins; protein turnover, the repetitive process in which body proteins are degraded and resynthesized; and biosynthesis of amino acids in the liver (nonessential amino acids only, Section 15.11). It is drained by protein synthesis, by the making of nitrogen-containing molecules such as heme, nucleotides, and neurotransmitters, and by degradation for energy (Sections 15.2 and 15.4). Amino acids cannot be stored in the body or directly excreted, so those in excess are degraded to substances that can be processed through the common metabolic pathways.

Nitrogen balance occurs when the amount of nitrogen taken in as protein equals the amount excreted in waste (mostly as urea). Two kinds of imbalance occur. Negative nitrogen balance: protein degradation exceeds protein synthesis and tissue is wasted; causes are protein-poor diets, starvation, and wasting illnesses (and, in the hospital, severe burns, sepsis, major surgery, immobilization, and corticosteroid treatment). Positive nitrogen balance: protein synthesis exceeds degradation and large amounts of tissue are built; causes are growth, pregnancy, and convalescence from an emaciating illness.

Example 1: Predict the nitrogen balance

Problem. A 24-year-old with a 30% body-surface burn is in the ICU, on a high-protein feeding plan. A 7-year-old is healthy and growing. A 60-year-old is on bed rest after a hip fracture and is taking a corticosteroid. For each, predict the nitrogen balance and name the metabolic process that explains it.

Solution. Burn patient: negative. Stress hormones (cortisol, glucagon, epinephrine) drive muscle protein breakdown to supply amino acids for gluconeogenesis and tissue repair; transamination and the urea cycle run hard, so urinary urea exceeds dietary nitrogen even with extra protein. Growing child: positive; new tissue is being built, so more nitrogen is retained than excreted. Bed-rest patient on a corticosteroid: negative. Immobilized muscle is not maintained, and corticosteroids stimulate protein breakdown and gluconeogenesis.

Check. Ask “is net body protein going up or down?” Up means positive; down means negative. The urea cycle output follows the opposite way: more urea, more negative.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Protein needs in the hospital. Burns, sepsis, and ICU stays put patients in negative nitrogen balance; dietitians raise protein intake and, when the gut cannot be used, supply amino acids by total parenteral nutrition (TPN), an IV infusion of amino acids, glucose, and lipid. A 24-hour urine urea nitrogen is one way to measure how negative the balance is. Insulin, by contrast, cannot be given by mouth for exactly the reason on this page: it is a peptide, and pepsin and trypsin would hydrolyze it like any other protein (Chapter 10).

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Nutrition and the oral tissues. Protein malnutrition (negative nitrogen balance) means poor collagen synthesis and poor healing of the gingiva after periodontal therapy; the periodontal ligament and gingiva are collagen-rich tissues that turn over constantly and draw on the amino acid pool. Deficiencies of vitamin B12 or folate, like the B6 deficiency in Section 15.2, show up early in fast-dividing oral tissues as glossitis and angular cheilitis, so the mouth can be the first place a nutritional problem is seen. A patient recovering from a long illness or major surgery is in positive nitrogen balance and heals well; one who is frail, on a very restricted diet, or wasting from illness may not.
