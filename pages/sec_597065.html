Lecture Connection

The section above contrasts hexane with sodium chloride as "organic versus inorganic." In lecture the same table is read as molecular versus ionic, which is the comparison that actually predicts properties.

Read the table as molecular versus ionic

In this course

Every property in the source's table (low melting and boiling points, insoluble in water but soluble in gasoline, flammable, nonconducting) belongs to hexane because it is a molecular compound held together by weak intermolecular forces, not because it came from petroleum. Inorganic compounds can be molecular too (water, carbon dioxide, ammonia), and they share those properties. On an exam, say "molecular" or "ionic," and reserve "organic" for "contains carbon." The full comparison table is in Section 1.2.

Two reminders from Chapter 1 that this chapter leans on: hexane is nonpolar, so dispersion forces are the only attraction between its molecules (Section 1.8), and "flammable" is the one property that makes a hydrocarbon dangerous to handle.
