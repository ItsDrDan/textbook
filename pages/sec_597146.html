  • Define glycerophospholipids and understand the structural features that allow them to organize into lipid bilayers.
  • Understand the classification of glycerophospholipids and their role in infant respiratory distress syndrome.

What are glycerophospholipids?

Glycerophospholipids are triesters of propane-1,2,3-triol (glycerol): 1st and 2nd ester bonds with fatty acids, and the 3rd ester bond with phosphate, as illustrated in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\). The phosphate group is usually a diester: one ester bond joins it to the primary alcohol of glycerol, and a second ester bond joins it to a small molecule such as choline, ethanolamine, serine, or inositol.

Phospholipids have two hydrophobic tails, the hydrocarbon chains of two fatty acids, and a polar head. The head contains the ester groups, a phosphate with a negative charge, and often a positive charge on the nitrogen of the small molecule attached to the phosphate, as shown in the figure on the right. The 1st fatty acid attached to the primary alcohol group is usually a saturated fatty acid, and the 2nd fatty acid attached to the secondary alcohol group of glycerol is usually an unsaturated fatty acid.

Classes of glycerophospholipids

The glycerol esterified with two fatty acids and one phosphoric acid is also called phosphatidyl. Phosphatidyls with phosphate esterified with choline are called phosphatidylcholines (lecithin). Phosphatidyls with phosphate esterified with ethanolamine are called phosphatidylethanolamines (common name cephalins); those esterified with serine are phosphatidylserines. Another category is phosphatidylinositols with inositol-esterified phosphate, as shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\).

Lipid bilayer

Recall the general solubility rule "like dissolves like". Nonpolar oil droplets floating on polar water eventually merge into bigger droplets. Glycerophospholipids are amphiphilic, having an overall cylindrical shape with nonpolar tails of fatty acid chains and polar heads of ester groups on the glycerol part and ionic groups on phosphate and amine groups. Glycerophospholipids form a bilayer in the watery environment of living things based on the principle "like dissolves like", as illustrated in Figure \(\PageIndex{3}\).

The hydrophobic tails of fatty acids form the interior of the bilayer, and the hydrophilic polar heads make the outer layers in contact with water. The molecules are in a fluid (dynamic) state due to thermal energy (Figure \(\PageIndex{3}\) middle) but held together by intermolecular interactions: dipole-dipole interaction in the polar heads and London dispersion forces in the hydrophobic tails. Saturated fatty acids pack tightly together, making a rigid bilayer. However, adding unsaturated fatty acids with kinks makes the packing loose, resulting in a more fluid bilayer, as illustrated in Figure \(\PageIndex{3}\) right.

The glycerophospholipid bilayer is a significant component of the cell membrane, the membranes around the nucleus, and some cell organelles. The hydrophobic nature of the interior of the bilayer becomes a barrier to the movement of ions and water in and out of the cell. Still, the bilayer's fluid nature allows the diffusion process to move some particles across the membrane. Other components of the cell membrane, such as cholesterol, adjust the rigidity, and proteins control the movement of particles in and out of the cell as needed. The structure of cell membranes is described in a later section.

Snake venom

Snakes inject their venom through fangs when they bite their prey, as shown in the figure on the right. The venom of eastern diamondback rattlesnakes and Indian cobras contains phospholipase enzymes that catalyze the hydrolysis of fatty acid on the secondary hydroxyl group of glycerol. The resulting phospholipids, which have lost one fatty acid and are called lysophospholipids, are no longer cylindrical and do not fit correctly in the lipid bilayer, causing the red blood cell membrane to rupture. This poses a significant health risk and may kill humans. The structure of the lysophospholipid example is shown in the figure on the left.

Infant respiratory distress syndrome (IRDS)

In the breathing process, the exchange of \(\ce{O2}\) and \(\ce{CO2}\) takes place in air sacs called alveoli within the lungs, as illustrated in Figure \(\PageIndex{4}\). Lung (pulmonary) surfactant lines the alveoli. It lowers the surface tension of the watery lining and makes the alveoli easier to inflate. Surfactant is mostly phospholipid, with some proteins. Its main component is a lecithin (phosphatidylcholine) with two saturated tails, dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine (DPPC); other phospholipids are also present. Remember that lecithin means phosphatidylcholine. Phosphatidic acid, shown in the figure on the right, is the simplest glycerophospholipid: its phosphate carries no amino alcohol.

Babies born too early (preterm) may not yet make enough surfactant. The risk is highest in babies born before about 28 weeks of gestation and is still significant up to about 34 weeks. Without enough surfactant, the air sacs collapse and have to reopen with each breath, a condition called infant respiratory distress syndrome. This makes breathing difficult, may damage the alveoli, and may lead to hypoxia and acidosis due to less oxygen inhalation and less carbon dioxide exhalation.

One older test of fetal lung maturity measures the lecithin-to-sphingomyelin (L/S) ratio in the amniotic fluid; an L/S ratio of about 2 or more suggests the lungs are mature. Prevention and treatment include corticosteroids given to the mother before an expected preterm birth (to speed lung development), surfactant given into the baby's lungs, and breathing support such as a ventilator.

Molecule in the Clinic: Propofol, the “milky” anesthetic

Propofol is the most common drug for starting general anesthesia and for procedural sedation. It is a phenol that barely dissolves in water, so it is given IV as a white oil-in-water emulsion of soybean oil, glycerol and egg lecithin. Because the emulsion is also a good food for bacteria, opened vials must be used promptly or discarded.

Your turn: Lecithin is a glycerophospholipid. How does it keep the oil droplets dispersed in water?

Answer
Its two fatty-acid tails dissolve in the oil droplet and its charged phosphate–choline head faces the water, coating each droplet with a polar surface so the droplets do not merge.

Lecture Connection

In lecture a glycerophospholipid is "a triacylglycerol with the third fatty acid swapped for a phosphodiester bonded to an amino alcohol." Learn the four membrane and signaling families by their backbone, and learn why a polar head on two nonpolar tails is exactly what a membrane needs.

Professor Tip

Lipids are grouped by solubility (they don't dissolve in water), not by one functional group. To classify one, ask: Is there a glycerol? A phosphate? Four fused rings? An ester or an amide? Then follow the table. A second question sorts the storage and membrane lipids: what is the backbone? Glycerol means a glycerolipid (triacylglycerol or glycerophospholipid); sphingosine means a sphingolipid; and the amide bond is the giveaway for sphingosine.

Phospholipids: a charged head on two tails

Phospholipids are lipids that contain a phosphorus atom; the alcohol attached to the phosphate is usually an amino alcohol, which is ionized at physiological pH 7.4. Glycerophospholipids (phospho-acyl-glycerols) are the main component of most cell membranes. Start with a triacylglycerol and replace the third fatty acid with a phosphate that is also esterified to the amino alcohol; the phosphate with its two OR groups is a phosphodiester. With choline, HOCH2CH2N+(CH3)3, the product is phosphatidylcholine, better known as lecithin; with ethanolamine you get phosphatidylethanolamine (cephalin), and with serine, phosphatidylserine. Lecithin and cephalin are abundant in brain and nerve tissue and in egg yolk. The phosphate (negative) and the ammonium nitrogen (positive) make a strongly polar, charged head; the two fatty acid chains are nonpolar tails. That head-and-tail design is what makes these molecules build membranes and act as emulsifiers (lecithin in egg yolk holds oil and vinegar together in mayonnaise).

Professor Tip

You need lecithin (phosphatidylcholine) and cephalin (phosphatidylethanolamine; phosphatidylserine is a close relative) and the general pattern: glycerol, two fatty acid esters, one phosphodiester, one ionized amino alcohol. Phosphatidylinositol, the snake-venom phospholipase and lysophospholipids are background.

Example 1: Classify the lipid

Problem. A lipid contains glycerol, two fatty acids, a phosphate, and choline. What is it, and where is it found?

Solution

A glycerophospholipid (phosphatidylcholine, also called lecithin). It is a major component of cell membranes, and it is the emulsifier in egg yolk.

Check. Backbone glycerol rules out a sphingolipid; the phosphate rules out a triacylglycerol and a glycolipid; two tails plus a charged head makes it amphipathic, as a membrane lipid should be.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Lung surfactant, the subject of the section's infant respiratory distress syndrome discussion, is mostly a glycerophospholipid, dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine: a lecithin with two saturated 16:0 tails. Spread as a monolayer on the watery lining of the alveoli, its heads in the water and its tails in the air, it lowers surface tension and keeps alveoli from collapsing at the end of each breath. Premature infants who cannot make enough are given surfactant through the endotracheal tube, a job shared by respiratory therapists and NICU nurses. The products used are natural surfactants taken from animal lungs: beractant (Survanta), poractant alfa (Curosurf) and calfactant (Infasurf).1 To prevent the problem, a pregnant patient at risk of delivering within seven days, between 24 and 34 weeks, is usually given two injections of the corticosteroid betamethasone 24 hours apart.2 The same head-and-tail chemistry makes lipid emulsions work: IV lipid nutrition and the anesthetic propofol are oil-in-water emulsions stabilized by egg-yolk phospholipids, which is why they look milky white.

Sources: 1. U.S. National Library of Medicine, DailyMed labels: Survanta (beractant), Curosurf (poractant alfa), Infasurf (calfactant). 2. CRICO (Harvard Medical Institutions), OB Guideline 11: Use of Antenatal Corticosteroids for Fetal Maturation, 2026.

Summary

  • A glycerophospholipid is glycerol esterified to two fatty acids and a phosphate, which is also esterified to an ionized amino alcohol.
  • Lecithin has choline as the amino alcohol; cephalins have ethanolamine.
  • The molecule has a polar, charged head and two nonpolar tails, so in water it forms a lipid bilayer, the basis of cell membranes.
  • Lung surfactant is mostly a lecithin with two saturated tails; premature infants who lack it develop respiratory distress syndrome.
