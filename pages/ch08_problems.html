Problem 8.55

Which pair of sugars are epimers?

  2. D-glucose and D-fructose
  3. D-galactose and D-mannose

Problem 8.58

Which statement about sucrose is correct?

  1. It is hydrolyzed by lactase to glucose and galactose.
  2. It mutarotates in water because its glucose ring can open.
  3. Its glycosidic bond joins C1 of glucose to C2 of fructose, so neither unit has a free hemiacetal.
  4. It gives a positive Benedict's test because fructose is a ketose.
Answer
C. Both anomeric carbons (glucose C1 and fructose C2) are in the acetal link, so no ring can open: sucrose does not mutarotate and is non-reducing. Sucrase, not lactase, hydrolyzes it to glucose and fructose.

Problem 8.59

Compare and contrast. Amylose and cellulose are both unbranched polymers of D-glucose. Compare their glycosidic links, overall chain shape, result in the iodine test, and digestibility by humans, and explain each difference from the structure.

Answer
Amylose has α(1→4) links; the α link (anomeric OH down) bends the chain so it coils into a helix. The helix traps iodine, giving a blue-black color, and human amylase fits the α(1→4) link, so amylose is digested to maltose and glucose. Cellulose has β(1→4) links (anomeric OH up); every other glucose is flipped, so the chain is straight and chains hydrogen-bond side by side into fibers. There is no helix, so iodine gives no color, and no human enzyme hydrolyzes the β(1→4) link, so cellulose passes through as fiber. Same monomer; one stereocenter in the link changes everything.

Problem 8.60

Predict. Three test tubes contain (1) sorbitol, (2) sucrose, and (3) sucrose that was first boiled with dilute HCl and then neutralized. Predict the Benedict's test result for each and explain.

Answer
(1) Negative: sorbitol is a sugar alcohol with no C=O and no hemiacetal, so nothing can be oxidized. (2) Negative: sucrose's α1–β2 acetal link ties up both anomeric carbons. (3) Positive (brick-red Cu2O): acid hydrolyzed the acetal, releasing glucose and fructose, both reducing sugars (fructose rearranges to an aldose in the basic reagent).

Problem 8.61

Problem 8.62

Analyze the figure. The Haworth projection shows Sugar X, a D-aldohexose. (a) Is it the α or β anomer, and how do you know? (b) Convert the ring positions back to a Fischer projection (right or left for C2, C3, C4) and identify the sugar. (c) Is Sugar X a reducing sugar?

Answer
(a) β: the anomeric OH on C1 points up, on the same side as the CH2OH on C5. (b) Down in Haworth = right in Fischer, up = left. C2 OH down → right; C3 OH up → left; C4 OH up → left; C5 carries the CH2OH up, so D. The pattern right, left, left, right is D-galactose, so Sugar X is β-D-galactose (the C4 epimer of glucose). (c) Yes. C1 is a free hemiacetal (OH plus ring O on the same carbon), so the ring can open to an aldehyde and reduce Benedict's reagent.

More Practice: Clinical and Integrative

Problem 8.63

Dental hygiene. A patient sips a sucrose-sweetened soda all afternoon. A plaque pH probe shows the pH dropping to 4.8 within ten minutes of each sip. (a) Which product of bacterial metabolism causes the drop, and from which carbohydrate? (b) Why is staying below pH 5.5 harmful to enamel? (c) The patient asks whether chewing xylitol gum instead would help. Use the structure of xylitol to answer.

Answer
(a) Lactic acid, made when Streptococcus mutans and other plaque bacteria ferment the glucose and fructose released from sucrose. (b) Below the critical pH of about 5.5, acid dissolves hydroxyapatite (demineralization) faster than saliva can redeposit it (remineralization); continuous sipping never lets the pH recover. (c) Yes. Xylitol is a five-carbon sugar alcohol, the reduction product of xylose: it has OH groups but no aldehyde or ketone, so bacteria cannot ferment it to acid, and it even inhibits S. mutans. Chewing also stimulates saliva, which buffers the acid.

Problem 8.64

Nursing. A patient with diabetes has a fingerstick glucose of 95 mg/dL this morning but an HbA1c of 8.4%. (a) Explain, using the chemistry of glucose, how hemoglobin becomes glycated. (b) Why can the two results both be correct? (c) Which form of glucose, ring or open chain, is responsible, and roughly what fraction of glucose is in that form?

Answer
(a) Glucose mutarotates through its open-chain form, which has a free aldehyde at C1. That aldehyde reacts with an amine group on hemoglobin (the N-terminal valine of the β chain) and the product rearranges to a stable attachment: the same reducing-sugar reactivity that gives a positive Benedict's test. (b) A fingerstick reports glucose right now; HbA1c reports the fraction of hemoglobin glycated over the 120-day life of red blood cells, so a normal reading today is consistent with high average glucose over the past two to three months. (c) The open-chain aldehyde, which is less than 0.1% of the glucose at any instant; it is constantly regenerated by ring opening, so the reaction still proceeds.

Problem 8.65

Nursing. A newborn on a milk-based formula develops vomiting and jaundice. A copper-reduction test on the urine is strongly positive, but a glucose-oxidase dipstick is negative. (a) What does this pair of results tell you about the sugar in the urine? (b) Which disaccharide in the formula is the source, which enzyme hydrolyzes it in the intestine, and which monosaccharide accumulates if the infant has galactosemia? (c) What kind of formula would the infant need?

Answer
(a) The urine contains a reducing sugar that is not glucose: the copper test (Benedict's-type chemistry) reacts with any sugar whose ring can open to an aldehyde, while glucose oxidase is specific for glucose. (b) Lactose, a β(1→4) disaccharide of galactose and glucose, is hydrolyzed by lactase. In classic galactosemia the enzyme that converts galactose for use in the body is deficient, so galactose (a reducing aldohexose) accumulates and spills into the urine. (c) A lactose-free (for example, soy-based) formula, so no galactose is ingested.

Problem 8.66

Integrative (Chapter 5). In Chapter 5 you learned that hemiacetals form in water and that acetals need acid and water to hydrolyze. (a) Identify the hemiacetal and the acetal carbons in maltose. (b) Explain why α-D-glucose in water converts to the β form but maltose does not convert to glucose unless acid or maltase is present.

Answer
(a) The right-hand glucose unit's C1 carries an OH and the ring oxygen: a hemiacetal. The left-hand unit's C1 is bonded to two oxygens (its ring O and the bridging O to C4 of the other unit) with no OH: an acetal, the glycosidic bond. (b) A hemiacetal is in equilibrium with its open-chain carbonyl in neutral water, so the ring opens and re-closes, giving mutarotation. An acetal is stable in neutral or basic water; breaking the C–O bond of the glycosidic link requires acid catalysis (acid hydrolysis) or an enzyme (maltase) that does the same job at body temperature and neutral pH.

Problem 8.67

Integrative (Chapters 4 and 5). Classify the alcohol at C6 of glucose as primary, secondary, or tertiary, and use the oxidation rules for alcohols and aldehydes to explain (a) why C6 can end up as a COOH in glucuronic acid, (b) why C2 through C5 cannot become carboxylic acids, and (c) why the liver's enzyme must work on the ring form to make glucuronic acid rather than glucaric acid.

Answer
C6 (CH2OH) is a primary alcohol. (a) A primary alcohol oxidizes to an aldehyde and then to a carboxylic acid, so C6 can become COOH. (b) C2–C5 are secondary alcohols (CHOH with two carbon neighbors); a secondary alcohol oxidizes only to a ketone and stops, because there is no H left on the carbon to remove. (c) In the open chain, C1 is a free aldehyde and is oxidized even more easily than C6, which would give the aldaric acid (glucaric acid). In the ring, C1 is tied up as a hemiacetal or, in a glycoside, as an acetal, so an enzyme that oxidizes C6 leaves C1 untouched and the product is the alduronic acid, glucuronic acid.

Problem 8.69

D-Galactose has the Fischer pattern right, left, left, right for the OH groups on C2, C3, C4, C5. Describe the Fischer projection of L-galactose and name the relationship between the two.

Answer
Flip every chiral center: CHO on top, then OH left (C2), right (C3), right (C4), left (C5), CH2OH at the bottom. The C5 OH on the left makes it L. D- and L-galactose are enantiomers (non-superimposable mirror images), because all four centers are opposite.

Problem 8.70

Describe the Haworth projection of α-D-mannose (D-mannose: OH left at C2, left at C3, right at C4, right at C5). State up or down for the C2, C3, and C4 OH groups, the C6 CH2OH, and the anomeric OH.

Answer
Left → up, right → down: C2 OH up, C3 OH up, C4 OH down. The sugar is D, so the CH2OH on C5 points up. For α the anomeric OH on C1 is trans to the CH2OH, so it points down. Compared with α-D-glucose only the C2 OH is different (up instead of down), as expected for the C2 epimer.

Problem 8.71

Describe the Haworth projection of β-L-glucose, and explain how it differs from β-D-glucose.

Answer
L-Glucose is the mirror image of D-glucose: left, right, left, left. So C2 OH up, C3 OH down, C4 OH up, and the C6 CH2OH points down (L). β means the anomeric OH is cis to the CH2OH, so with the CH2OH down the C1 OH is also down. Every position is the reverse of β-D-glucose (C2 down, C3 up, C4 down, CH2OH up, C1 OH up).

Problem 8.72

Describe the five-membered Haworth ring of α-D-fructose. Which carbons are in the ring, which carbon is anomeric, and which way do the C3 OH, C4 OH, C6 CH2OH, and anomeric OH point?

Answer
The C5 OH adds to the C2 ketone, so the ring holds C2, C3, C4, C5 and the oxygen; C2 is the anomeric carbon (a hemiketal). C3 OH (left in Fischer) up; C4 OH (right) down; C6 CH2OH up (D). For α the anomeric OH is trans to the C6 CH2OH, so it points down, and the C1 CH2OH on C2 points up.

Problem 8.73

Analyze the figure. Disaccharide Z is drawn below. (a) Identify the two monosaccharide units. (b) Name the glycosidic linkage (α or β, and the carbon numbers it joins). Problem 8.75

Classify each reaction as condensation or hydrolysis, and name the small molecule lost or consumed.

  2. lactose → galactose + glucose (lactase)
  3. amylose → many maltose units (amylase)

Problem 8.78

True or false? (If false, correct it.) α-D-Glucose and β-D-glucose are enantiomers.

Answer
False. They differ at only one chiral center, the anomeric carbon C1, so they are diastereomers, specifically anomers. The enantiomer of α-D-glucose is α-L-glucose, in which every center is reversed.

Problem 8.80

Nursing. A patient with type B blood is about to receive a transfusion. (a) What kind of molecule on the red blood cell surface defines the ABO blood type, and which monosaccharide marks type B? (b) Why can type B cells not be given to a type A patient? (c) Type O carries only the core chain. Explain why type O red cells can be given to patients of any ABO type.

Answer

Cumulative Problem 8.1 (Chapters 4, 5 and 8)

Open-chain D-glucose is OHC(CHOH)4CH2OH. How many chiral carbons does it have, and which two groups react with each other to close the six-membered ring?

  1. four; the C5 –OH adds to the C1 aldehyde to form a hemiacetal
  2. five; the C6 –OH adds to the C1 aldehyde to form an acetal
  3. four; the C1 aldehyde is oxidized by the C5 –OH
  4. six; the C2 –OH and C5 –OH form an ether
Answer
A. C2, C3, C4 and C5 each carry H, OH and two different carbon chains, so there are four chiral carbons (C1 is a flat aldehyde and C6 has two H atoms). The C5 hydroxyl adds to the C1 aldehyde, exactly as an alcohol adds to an aldehyde in Chapter 5, giving a cyclic hemiacetal. The new OH on C1 can point down (α) or up (β), so ring closure creates a fifth chiral center, the anomeric carbon.

Cumulative Problem 8.2 (Chapters 5 and 8)

Maltose gives a positive Benedict’s test; sucrose does not, yet both are disaccharides of similar size. Explain using what you know about hemiacetals and acetals.

Answer
A sugar is reducing when it has a free hemiacetal that can open to an aldehyde, which Benedict’s Cu2+ oxidizes (Chapter 5). In maltose the two glucose units are joined through C1 of one unit only; the other unit’s C1 is still a hemiacetal, so it opens, is oxidized, and the blue solution turns brick-red. In sucrose the link is between C1 of glucose and C2 of fructose, the anomeric carbons of both units, so both are locked as acetals with nothing left to open. No free aldehyde, no reaction. This is also why sucrose is used in the lab as a non-reducing control.

Cumulative Problem 8.3 (Chapters 1, 2 and 8)

Glucose (180 g/mol) dissolves in water to the extent of about 90 g per 100 mL; hexane (86 g/mol) does not dissolve at all. Explain, and predict whether glucose will dissolve in hexane.

Answer
Glucose has five –OH groups plus a ring oxygen, every one of which hydrogen bonds with water, so the molecule is pulled into solution even though it is twice the mass of hexane. Hexane is a nonpolar alkane (Chapter 2) with only dispersion forces and nothing to offer water. By “like dissolves like” glucose will not dissolve in hexane: its polar surface gains nothing from a nonpolar solvent. The same OH-rich surface is why sugars are hygroscopic (they pull moisture from the air) and why glucose can travel freely in blood but needs a transporter to cross a lipid membrane.

Cumulative Problem 8.4 (Chapters 5 and 8)

Classify glucose, fructose and ribose by carbonyl type and number of carbons. Which pairs are isomers of each other? In the ring form, which of the three forms a five-membered ring?

Answer
Glucose: an aldohexose (aldehyde, six carbons). Fructose: a ketohexose (ketone at C2, six carbons). Ribose: an aldopentose (aldehyde, five carbons). Glucose and fructose are constitutional isomers (both C6H12O6); ribose (C5H10O5) is not an isomer of either. Fructose closes between its C5 –OH and the C2 ketone to form a five-membered (furanose) ring; ribose also forms a five-membered ring, and that ribose ring is the sugar in RNA and in ATP (Chapters 12 and 13). Glucose forms a six-membered (pyranose) ring.

Cumulative Problem 8.5 (Chapters 5, 6 and 8)

Lactose is hydrolyzed by the enzyme lactase. (a) Name the products. (b) What kind of bond is broken, and what general reaction type is this? Compare it with the hydrolysis of an ester. (c) What happens in a person who lacks lactase?

Answer
(a) Glucose and galactose. (b) The β-1,4-glycosidic bond, which is an acetal linkage; breaking it with water is hydrolysis, the same reaction type that splits an ester into an acid and an alcohol (Chapter 6), and in both cases one molecule of water is consumed per bond. (c) Undigested lactose passes to the colon, where bacteria ferment it to gases and acids, producing bloating, cramps and diarrhea: lactose intolerance. Lactase tablets supply the enzyme; lactose-free milk has already been treated with it.

Cumulative Problem 8.6 (Chapters 4, 5, 6 and 8)

In the dental office. Streptococcus mutans in plaque ferments sucrose to lactic acid, which dissolves tooth enamel below pH 5.5. Sugar-free gum is sweetened with xylitol, HOCH2(CHOH)3CH2OH, which the bacteria cannot ferment. A urine test strip for glucose turns color because of the same chemistry as Benedict’s test. (a) What functional group class is xylitol, and how does it differ from a sugar? (b) What class of compound is lactic acid, and why does it attack enamel? (c) What reaction does the glucose test strip detect?

Answer
(a) Xylitol is a sugar alcohol (a polyol): every carbon carries an OH and there is no carbonyl group, so it cannot form a ring, is not a reducing sugar, and the bacterial enzymes that handle sugars cannot use it. (b) Lactic acid is a carboxylic acid (Chapter 6). The H+ it releases reacts with the hydroxyapatite of enamel, dissolving calcium and phosphate; below pH 5.5 enamel loses mineral faster than saliva can replace it. (c) Oxidation of glucose’s aldehyde group (Chapter 5). The strip uses the enzyme glucose oxidase instead of copper, but the principle is the same: a reducing sugar is oxidized and a dye changes color.
