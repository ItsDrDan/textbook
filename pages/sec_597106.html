  • Identify isomers and differentiate constitutional isomers from sterioisomes.

What is stereochemistry?

Stereochemistry is the study of the relative arrangement of atoms in molecules and their manipulation. A major area of stereochemistry is the study of isomers which is introduced below.

Isomers

Isomers are species with the same atoms in the same numbers, i.e., the same molecular formula but are arranged differently in space.

Several types of isomers can be classified in different ways. Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\) presents one scheme of classifying isomers.

Constitutional isomers

Constitutional isomers have a different sequence of bonds that may result in another skeleton, i.e., skeletal isomers, different reactive features, i.e., functional groups isomers, or the same reactive features but placed at different locations, i.e., positional isomers.

Skeletal isomers

Skeletal isomers have different carbon skeletons. For example, hexane, 2-methylpentane, 3-methylpentane, and 2,2-dimethylbutane are skeletal isomers having the same molecular formula \(\ce{C6H14}\).

Functional group isomers

Functional group isomers have different reactive features, i.e., different functional groups. For example, propan-1-ol is alcohol, and methoxyethane is ether, but they are isomers having the same molecular formula \(\ce{C3H8O}\).

Positional isomers

Positional isomers have the same reactive features, i.e., the same functional groups, but the groups are attached at different positions. For example, propan-1-ol -a primary alcohol having an alcohol group linked to terminal \(\ce{C}\) and propan-2-ol -a secondary alcohol having an alcohol group attached to a non-terminal \(\ce{C}\), are isomers having the same molecule formula \(\ce{C3H8O}\).

Stereoisomers

  • Stereoisomers have the same sequence of bonds but the atoms or groups of atoms are oriented differently in space.
    • Conformations are the different orientations of atoms that result from rotation around single bonds. Different conformations that exist at distinct potential energy minima are called conformational isomers or conformers.
    • Configurational isomers are stereoisomers that can be interconnected only by breaking and making some bonds. The specific arrangement of atoms in a configuration isomer is also called configuration.

Conformational isomers

Conformations are the result of rotation around single bonds of a molecule. For example, rotation around \(\ce{C-C}\) bond of ethane places a set of three \(\ce{H's}\) on one \(\ce{C}\) at different positions relative to \(\ce{H's}\) on the other \(\ce{C}\), as illustrated below.

Typically, rotation around single bonds occurs rapidly at room temperature. So, the conformations usually exist as a mixture and can not be easily separated. All conformers of the same compound are not equal in energy, and there are energy barriers for interconversion; however, the energy barriers are usually low, allowing the conformations to interconvert rapidly at room temperature.

Different conformations that exist at distinct potential energy minima are called conformational isomers or conformers.

For example, the two conformations of butane shown on the right margin are conformational isomers because they exist at distinct potential energy minima. The distinction between conformations and conformational isomers will be clarified later, when the possible energies of different conformations of ethane and butane are analyzed in a subsequent section.

Configurational isomers

Configurational isomers are stereoisomers that can be interconnected only by breaking and making some bonds. For example, cis-but-2-ene and trans-but-2-ene are configurational isomers in which \(\ce{-CH3}\) groups connected to two \(\ce{C's}\) of a double bond are oriented differently as shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\). Another example is L-glyceraldehyde and D-glyceraldehyde in which four different groups are connected to the same \(\ce{C}\) but oriented differently as shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\).

The configurational isomers are subdivided into enantiomers and diastereomers.

Enantiomers

A pair of stereoisomers that are related to each other as non-superimposable mirror images are Enantiomers.

For example, D-glyceraldehyde and L-glyceraldehyde shown above are enantiomers of each other. Imagine there is a mirror between D- and L-glyceraldehyde shown above. You will notice that they are mirror images of each other. If you try to overlap L-glyceraldehyde onto D-glyceraldehyde, two groups may overlap, but the other two will not overlap, no matter how you may rotate the molecule. D- and L- represent two different configurations of glyceraldehyde shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\). Enantiomers are chiral molecules.

Chirality

An object or molecule that cannot be superimposed on its mirror image by any translation, rotational, or conformational changes is a chiral object. This geometric property is called chirality.

Achiral is not chiral, i.e., the objects or molecules that are identical to their mirror image are achiral.

For example, an amino acid with four different groups attached to the same carbon and hand are chiral, having a non-superimposable mirror image as illustrated in Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\). Like left and right hands that have a thumb and fingers in the same order, but are mirror images and not the same, chiral molecules have the same things attached in the same order, but are mirror images and not the same.

Diastereomers

Stereoisomers that are not enantiomers are diastereomers.

For example, cis-but-2-ene and trans-but-2-ene shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\) are diastereomers because they have the same formula, and the same atom-connectivity, but methyl groups are oriented in the same direction in cis- and in opposite directions in trans-isomer, they do not mirror each other. The cis- and trans- represent two different configurations of but-2-ene as shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\).

Lecture Connection

The lecture reduces chirality to four skills: decide whether an object or molecule is chiral, find and count the chiral carbons (star them), tell enantiomers from diastereomers, and read D or L from a Fischer projection. Everything in this block is practice for those four.

In this course

The section text classifies isomers broadly and counts conformers (rotation about single bonds) and cis–trans alkenes among the stereoisomers, calling cis- and trans-2-butene diastereomers. For CHEM 3B: constitutional isomers are the skeletal, positional and functional group isomers of Chapter 4; cis–trans isomers are the geometric isomers of Chapter 3; conformers are not tested. When an exam says enantiomers or diastereomers, it is asking about molecules with chiral carbons, and that is the use of the words in this block.

Handedness: chiral or achiral?

Your two hands are mirror images, yet you cannot lay one on top of the other and have every finger match. Any object with that property is chiral (from the Greek for hand): a glove, a shoe, a screw. An object that can be superimposed on its mirror image is achiral: two socks from a pair are mirror images that are superimposable, so a sock is achiral, and so is a plain coffee mug. Molecules work the same way, and the lecture's test is the same three steps: draw the molecule in 3D, draw its mirror image, and ask whether you can rotate the molecule to make it align with its mirror image. If you can, it is achiral. Stereoisomers always have the same connectivity of atoms but a different arrangement in space; constitutional isomers differ in connectivity. The most common reason a molecule is chiral is a stereocenter (chiral carbon, chirality center): a carbon bonded to four different groups. Mark it with a star (*). Swap any two of those groups and you get the mirror-image molecule, not the same one.

How to find and count chiral carbons

Go carbon by carbon and ask one question: does this carbon carry four different things? You can skip carbons quickly when:

  • it has two or more H atoms (CH2 and CH3 groups are never chiral);
  • it is part of a double or triple bond (C=O, C=C and the ring carbons of benzene have only three neighbors), so a carbon of a multiple bond is never a chirality center;
  • it carries two identical groups, such as two CH3 groups or, in a ring, two identical paths around the ring.

That leaves only CH carbons with four single bonds to check. Count them; call the total n.

Example: Counting chiral carbons in a drug

Problem. How many chiral carbons does ibuprofen have? How many does citric acid have? How many stereoisomers are possible for each?

Solution. Ibuprofen. Skip every CH2 and CH3 and every ring carbon (each ring carbon has only three neighbors). Two CH carbons remain. The CH of the isobutyl group holds H, CH2–ring, CH3 and CH3: two methyls, not chiral. The CH next to the COOH holds H, CH3, COOH and the aromatic ring: four different groups, chiral. Total 1; 21 = 2 stereoisomers (one pair of enantiomers). Citric acid, HOOC–CH2–C(OH)(COOH)–CH2–COOH. The only candidate is the central carbon: OH, COOH, CH2COOH and CH2COOH. The two arms are identical, so it is not chiral. Total 0; citric acid has no stereoisomers.

Check. A quick test for any candidate carbon: imagine swapping two of its groups. If the result is the same molecule (as it is when two groups are identical), the carbon is not chiral.

How many stereoisomers? Each chiral carbon can be arranged two ways, so a molecule with n chiral carbons has at most 2n stereoisomers. One chiral carbon gives one pair of enantiomers (2). Two chiral carbons give up to 4 stereoisomers: two pairs of enantiomers, and any two that are not mirror images are diastereomers. Enantiomers have identical melting points, boiling points and solubilities and differ only in how they interact with other chiral things (enzymes, receptors, other chiral molecules). Diastereomers are different compounds with different physical properties. Glucose, with four chiral carbons, is one of 24 = 16 aldohexoses (Chapter 8).

Enantiomer, diastereomer or the same molecule?

Enantiomers are stereoisomers that are mirror images and not superimposable. Diastereomers are stereoisomers that are not mirror images and not superimposable; they need two or more chiral carbons. When you are given two drawings, use this routine. (1) Check that the two have the same connectivity; if not, they are constitutional isomers, not stereoisomers. (2) Draw or imagine the mirror image of the first drawing. (3) Try to rotate and flip the second drawing to match. Do not forget that you can rotate and flip molecules; a drawing that looks different may be the same molecule seen from another side. If the second drawing is the exact mirror image and cannot be rotated to match, the two are enantiomers. If the two have two or more chiral carbons and differ at some but not all of them (one wedge or dash, or one Fischer substituent, flipped), they are diastereomers. If one can be rotated onto the other, they are identical.

With Fischer projections the same routine is faster, because the drawing convention is fixed: the mirror image of a Fischer projection swaps every horizontal group left-for-right. Compare the four projections below, all of the same four-carbon hydroxy aldehyde with two chiral carbons.

  • A and B: every horizontal group is swapped, so B is the exact mirror image of A: enantiomers. The same is true of C and D.
  • A and C: only the C2 groups are swapped; C3 is the same. They are stereoisomers but not mirror images: diastereomers. Any pair that is not a mirror-image pair (A/C, A/D, B/C, B/D) is a diastereomer pair.
  • D/L labels: read the bottom chiral carbon (C3). A and C have the OH on the right, so both are D; B and D are L. Notice that two D compounds (A and C) can still be different molecules: the D/L label describes one carbon, not the whole molecule.

Example: Enantiomers, diastereomers or identical?

Problem. Use the four Fischer projections A–D above. Classify each pair as enantiomers, diastereomers or identical: (a) A and B; (b) B and C; (c) A and a projection that has OH on the right at C2 and OH on the right at C3.

Solution. (a) Compare carbon by carbon. At C2, A has OH right and B has OH left; at C3, A has OH right and B has OH left. Every horizontal group is swapped, so B is the mirror image of A: enantiomers. (b) At C2, B has OH left and C has OH left (same); at C3, B has OH left and C has OH right (different). Only one of the two chiral carbons is flipped, so the two are stereoisomers but not mirror images: diastereomers. (c) The description matches A exactly at both carbons: identical.

Check. Count the differences. Two chiral carbons, both flipped = enantiomers; one flipped = diastereomers; none flipped = the same molecule. The rule scales: with three chiral carbons, all three must be flipped for an enantiomer, and any other mix of flips is a diastereomer.

Reading a Fischer projection: D and L

A Fischer projection takes a 3D tetrahedral carbon and re-draws it flat: it places the most oxidized group (CHO for a sugar) at the top, uses vertical lines for bonds that go back (away from you), and uses horizontal lines for bonds that come forward (toward you). The chiral carbon sits at the crossing point. The D- and L- system designates the handedness of the molecule: D (from the Latin dexter, right) when the OH on the chiral carbon is on the right side, L (laevus, left) when it is on the left. The D,L designation is determined by the position of the OH group attached to the chiral carbon farthest from the carbonyl group, which is the bottom-most chiral carbon in a correctly drawn projection. The letters are a family label traced back to glyceraldehyde, the simplest sugar (the section's own D/L example); they are not a count of how many OH groups point each way.

Professor Tip

Before you read left or right, check that the projection is drawn the standard way, CHO (or the most oxidized carbon) on top. Flipping the drawing upside down reverses the answer. And read the bottom chiral carbon, not the top one; the top one is the most common wrong choice on exams.

Chirality matters

Nearly all of biochemistry is one-handed. The amino acids in your proteins are L, the sugars you metabolize are D, and enzymes and receptors are themselves chiral. Many drugs are chiral, and often they must interact with a chiral receptor to be effective, so one enantiomer of a drug may treat a disease while its mirror image is ineffective or even harmful. Naproxen (the pain reliever in Aleve) has a toxic enantiomer, so care is taken to manufacture it as a single enantiomer. Ibuprofen is sold as a 50:50 mixture of its two enantiomers, called a racemic mixture, but mainly one enantiomer relieves pain. Your nose is chiral too: the two enantiomers of carvone, a ketone, interact differently with smell receptors, and one smells of spearmint while the other smells of caraway seed.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

One-handed drugs. Levothyroxine (the thyroid hormone replacement) is the single L enantiomer; only the L enantiomer of DOPA (levodopa) treats Parkinson's disease; esomeprazole is one enantiomer of omeprazole sold on its own; and one enantiomer of ibuprofen does most of the work. Same atoms, same bonds, different hands, different effects. When you read a drug name that begins with levo- or es-, you are reading a chirality label.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Cooling is chiral. Menthol, the cooling agent in mouthwash, has three chiral carbons, so 23 = 8 stereoisomers are possible. Only one of them, (–)-menthol, gives the strong minty cooling; its enantiomer, (+)-menthol, smells musty and barely cools. Your cold receptors, like all proteins, are chiral, and they fit one hand of menthol far better than the other.
