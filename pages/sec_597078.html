As noted before, alkenes are hydrocarbons with carbon-to-carbon double bonds (R2C=CR2) and alkynes are hydrocarbons with carbon-to-carbon triple bonds (R–C≡C–R). Collectively, they are called unsaturated hydrocarbons because they have fewer hydrogen atoms than does an alkane with the same number of carbon atoms, as is indicated in the following general formulas:

The double bond is shared by the two carbons and does not involve the hydrogen atoms, although the condensed formula does not make this point obvious. Note that the molecular formula for ethene is C2H4, whereas that for ethane is C2H6.

The first two alkenes in Table \(\PageIndex{1}\), ethene and propene, are most often called by their common names—ethylene and propylene, respectively (Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\)). Ethylene is a major commercial chemical. The US chemical industry produces about 25 billion kilograms of ethylene annually, more than any other synthetic organic chemical. More than half of this ethylene goes into the manufacture of polyethylene, one of the most familiar plastics. Propylene is also an important industrial chemical. It is converted to plastics, isopropyl alcohol, and a variety of other products.

Although there is only one alkene with the formula C2H4 (ethene) and only one with the formula C3H6 (propene), there are several alkenes with the formula C4H8.

Here are some basic rules for naming alkenes from the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC):

  1. The longest chain of carbon atoms containing the double bond is considered the parent chain. It is named using the same stem as the alkane having the same number of carbon atoms but ends in -ene to identify it as an alkene. Thus the compound CH2=CHCH3 is propene.
  2. If there are four or more carbon atoms in a chain, we must indicate the position of the double bond. The carbon atoms are numbered so that the first of the two that are doubly bonded is given the lower of the two possible numbers. The compound CH3CH=CHCH2CH3, for example, has the double bond between the second and third carbon atoms. Its name is 2-pentene (not 3-pentene).
  3. Substituent groups are named as with alkanes, and their position is indicated by a number. Thus, the structure below is 5-methyl-2-hexene. Note that the numbering of the parent chain is always done in such a way as to give the double bond the lowest number, even if that causes a substituent to have a higher number. The double bond always has priority in numbering.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Name each compound.

Solution

  1. The longest chain containing the double bond has five carbon atoms, so the compound is a pentene (rule 1). To give the first carbon atom of the double bond the lowest number (rule 2), we number from the left, so the compound is a 2-pentene. There is a methyl group on the fourth carbon atom (rule 3), so the compound’s name is 4-methyl-2-pentene.
  2. The longest chain containing the double bond has five carbon atoms, so the parent compound is a pentene (rule 1). To give the first carbon atom of the double bond the lowest number (rule 2), we number from the left, so the compound is a 2-pentene. There is a methyl group on the third carbon atom (rule 3), so the compound’s name is 3-methyl-2-pentene.

Lecture Connection

The source gives the three IUPAC rules; lecture adds the ring rules, the tie-breaker, and two drawing conventions that cost students points every semester. The one rule to tattoo on your arm: the multiple bond gets the lowest number, even if a branch ends up higher.

Naming alkenes, lecture style

An unsaturated hydrocarbon contains at least one carbon–carbon double or triple bond, so it holds fewer hydrogens than the alkane with the same number of carbons. Alkenes (C=C) have the general formula CnH2n and alkynes (C≡C) have CnH2n−2. The two carbons of a double bond are trigonal planar (120° angles); the two carbons of a triple bond are linear (180°).

  • Pick the longest chain that contains the double or triple bond.
  • Number from the end that gives the multiple bond the lowest number, even if a branch ends up with a higher number.
  • Change the ending: -ene (C=C) or -yne (C≡C). Put the number of the first carbon of the multiple bond in front of the parent: 2-pentene (also written pent-2-ene). Two double bonds: -diene, as in 1,3-butadiene.
  • Rings: cyclohexene. C1 and C2 are the double-bond carbons, and no number is needed for the double bond itself. Number through the double bond toward the first substituent: a methyl group on the carbon next to the double bond gives 3-methylcyclohexene, never 6-methylcyclohexene. A methyl group on a double-bond carbon makes it C1: 1-methylcyclopentene.
  • Alkynes follow exactly the same rules with -yne: HC≡CCH2CH3 is 1-butyne; CH3C≡CCH3 is 2-butyne.
  • Tie-breaker: if the multiple bond is the same distance from both ends, begin numbering at the end closest to the first branch.

Two drawing conventions: in a condensed formula, always write the multiple bond in (CH3CH=CH2, never CH3CHCH2), and in a line structure draw the two carbons of a triple bond and their neighbors in a straight line, because the alkyne carbons are linear. You will also hear common names: ethylene (ethene), propylene (propene), and acetylene (ethyne). They are not IUPAC names, so treat them as slang you can recognize but should not write on an exam.

In this course

The source text writes the locant in front (2-pentene). Newer IUPAC style puts it next to the suffix (pent-2-ene). Both are accepted on exams and in the games; use whichever you read more easily, but be consistent inside one name. Common names (ethylene, propylene, acetylene) are for recognizing, not writing: if a question says "name," give the IUPAC name.

Example 1: Numbering for the double bond, not the branch

Problem

Name CH3CH(CH3)CH2CH=CH2, and explain why "2-methyl-4-pentene" is wrong.

Solution

The longest chain containing the C=C has five carbons: a pentene. Numbering from the right puts the double bond at C1; from the left it would be C4. The double bond wins, so number from the right: the double bond is C1–C2 and the methyl lands on C4. Name: 4-methyl-1-pentene (4-methylpent-1-ene).

Check

"2-Methyl-4-pentene" gives the branch the low number and the double bond the high one, which breaks the first rule of alkene naming. Formula check: C6H12 fits CnH2n for an alkene with six carbons (five in the chain plus the methyl). ✓
