Learning Objectives

  • Classify monosaccharides (aldose/ketose and number of carbons, e.g., aldohexose).
  • Assign D or L from a Fischer projection, and count chiral carbons.
  • Distinguish enantiomers, diastereomers, epimers, and functional group isomers among sugars.
  • Convert a Fischer projection to a Haworth ring and label the α and β anomers.
  • Identify reducing sugars and predict oxidation and reduction products (aldonic, alduronic, and aldaric acids; sugar alcohols).
  • Describe the glycosidic links and monomers in common disaccharides and polysaccharides, and write condensation and hydrolysis equations for them.

Carbohydrates are the sugars and the polymers built from them. In this chapter you will meet only a handful of molecules (glucose, galactose, fructose, three disaccharides, four polysaccharides), but the chemistry you already know shows up everywhere: aldehydes and ketones, hemiacetals and acetals, oxidation of aldehydes and alcohols, and chirality. If you can draw a hemiacetal from Chapter 5 and assign D/L from Chapter 5, you can do everything here.

Key Ideas

1. Monosaccharides

Carbohydrates are made of C, H, and O; they are also called saccharides ("sugars"). Chemically they are polyhydroxy aldehydes or polyhydroxy ketones, and the simplest ones are the monosaccharides. A monosaccharide (simple sugar) is a small polyhydroxy aldehyde or ketone: one C=O plus an OH on almost every other carbon. The general formula is CnH2nOn (usually n = 3 to 6), which looks like carbon plus water; early chemists thought they were "hydrates of carbon," and the name stuck. Carbohydrates are about 65% of the diet and a major source of energy; plants make them by photosynthesis, animals store them short-term as glycogen, and they supply carbons for other biomolecules and the sugar backbone of DNA and RNA.

  • The suffix -ose marks a carbohydrate. If the C=O is an aldehyde (at C1), the sugar is an aldose. If it is a ketone (usually at C2), it is a ketose.
  • Count the carbons: triose (3), tetrose (4), pentose (5), hexose (6). Combine the two words: glucose = aldohexose, fructose = ketohexose, ribose = aldopentose, glyceraldehyde = aldotriose, dihydroxyacetone = ketotriose.
  • An aldose and a ketose with the same number of carbons (glucose and fructose, both C6H12O6) are functional group isomers, a type of constitutional isomer (Key Idea 5).
  • D or L: look at the bottom-most chiral carbon, the one farthest from the C=O. OH drawn on the right = D; on the left = L. Naturally occurring sugars are almost always the D isomer.
  • Know glucose, galactose, and fructose. Galactose differs from glucose only at C4.

The three monosaccharides to know. D-Glucose (blood sugar, dextrose) is the most abundant monosaccharide; the body keeps its blood level in a narrow range (Key Idea 9), and excess is stored as glycogen or converted to fat. D-Galactose is half of the milk sugar lactose; it is part of brain and nerve tissue (so it is sometimes called brain sugar) and of the carbohydrates that define blood types (Key Idea 10). In galactosemia an enzyme needed to use galactose is missing, galactose accumulates, and untreated infants develop cataracts and liver damage. D-Fructose (fruit sugar) is the ketohexose in honey and fruit and the other half of sucrose; it is the sweetest common sugar, almost twice as sweet as table sugar with the same calories per gram. High-fructose corn syrup is made from corn starch: the starch is hydrolyzed to glucose and an enzyme then converts part of the glucose to fructose.

Fischer projections. A Fischer projection draws the carbon chain vertically with the C=O at (or nearest) the top. Each horizontal line is a bond that points toward you; the vertical bonds point away. Every crossing point is a chiral carbon. Number the carbons from the top, starting with the end closest to the C=O. To assign D or L, find the chiral carbon farthest from the C=O (the penultimate carbon, next to the bottom CH2OH): OH on the right = D, OH on the left = L. The other OH groups do not matter for D/L; they decide which sugar it is.

Professor Tip

Say the glucose pattern out loud as "right, left, right, right" for the OH groups on C2, C3, C4, C5. Galactose is "right, left, left, right" (C4 flipped). Mannose is "left, left, right, right" (C2 flipped). Fructose has the same C3–C5 pattern as glucose ("left, right, right") but with a ketone at C2 instead of an aldehyde at C1. In Carb Lab you can build each one from these patterns.

2. Rings: hemiacetals again

Recall from Chapter 5 that when an alcohol adds to an aldehyde, a hemiacetal forms. In water, one of a sugar's own OH groups attacks its C=O (C1 in glucose) and forms a cyclic hemiacetal. Use the alcohol that will make a six-membered ring (five-membered rings sometimes form). The old carbonyl carbon becomes a new chiral center, the anomeric carbon (the "goggle eyes" carbon from Chapter 5).

This is the same reaction you learned for aldehydes and ketones, just happening inside one molecule: an alcohol adds to a carbonyl to give a carbon with both an OH and an OR (an intramolecular hemiacetal). For glucose the attacking OH is on C5, so the ring contains five carbons plus one oxygen: a six-membered pyranose ring (named for the cyclic ether pyran). For fructose the ketone is at C2; when the C5 OH attacks it, the ring is five-membered (four carbons plus the oxygen), a furanose (named for furan), with the anomeric carbon at C2. This is the fructose ring you will draw and the one found in sucrose. Open-chain and ring forms are in equilibrium, but in water almost all of the sugar (more than 99%) is in a ring at any moment.

  • A Haworth projection shows the ring as a flat hexagon (or pentagon) with vertical lines for the groups; the real molecule is not flat. Groups on the right in the Fischer projection point down, and groups on the left point up.
  • The new OH on the anomeric carbon is α if it is trans to the CH2OH (for a D-sugar: down, opposite the CH2OH) and β if it is cis to the CH2OH (up, same side as the CH2OH).
  • α and β forms interconvert in solution through the open chain; the cyclization exists as an equilibrium called mutarotation.

Professor Tip

Two things to read off any Haworth drawing. (1) The back CH2OH: D sugars have it up, L sugars have it down. (2) The anomeric OH compared with that CH2OH: the symbol α crosses over itself, so α = trans (opposite sides); the symbol β has both bumps on the same side, so β = cis (same side). Say "CH2OH up, OH up, same side: beta" and you will not mix them up.

Haworth projection rules (the ring drawn flat, viewed edge-on, with the ring oxygen at the back right and C1 at the right):

  1. Number clockwise starting from the anomeric carbon (C1) at the right.
  2. Right in Fischer → down in Haworth; left in Fischer → up. For D-glucose: C2 OH down, C3 OH up, C4 OH down.
  3. For every D sugar the terminal CH2OH (C6) points up on C5. (It points down for an L sugar.)
  4. The new anomeric OH on C1 can be down (α) or up (β). The two anomers are not enantiomers of each other; they differ only at the one new chiral center created when the ring closed.

From Fischer to Haworth in three steps (the way it is done in lecture; a color-coded handout is in the Chapter 15 module in Canvas, and the videos below walk through glucose, mannose, and a ketose):

  1. Rotate the Fischer projection onto its side so the C=O is on the right. Everything that was on the left of the Fischer projection is now up; everything that was on the right is now down.
  2. Twist the chain around into a hexagon. By convention the carbonyl carbon is the right-most point of the ring. The CH2OH ends up up for a D sugar and down for an L sugar.
  3. Close the ring: the C5 OH bonds to C1, and the new OH on C1 is drawn down (α) or up (β). Draw both when asked for "the anomers."

From Haworth back to Fischer: rotate the ring 90° counterclockwise in your mind so that C1 is at the top, then read each position: up in the Haworth becomes left in the Fischer, down becomes right. The CH2OH tells you D (was up) or L (was down), and the anomeric OH tells you α or β.

Mutarotation. If you dissolve pure crystalline α-D-glucose in water, within an hour or two the solution contains both anomers. The ring is opening to the free aldehyde and re-closing, and each time it re-closes the OH can end up down or up. The result is the equilibrium mixture above. The open chain is a tiny fraction, but it matters: it is the form that can be oxidized (Section 3) and the form that reacts with proteins (see the nursing box on HbA1c).

3. Reactions

  • Reducing sugars have a free hemiacetal (an anomeric OH) that can open to an aldehyde. They give a positive Benedict's test. All monosaccharides are reducing sugars, and so are most disaccharides except sucrose.
  • Oxidation of the aldehyde end by a weak oxidizing agent (Tollens' or Benedict's) gives a sugar acid (glucose → gluconic acid). Stronger oxidants and enzymes can also oxidize the CH2OH end (Key Idea 6).
  • Reduction of the C=O (NaBH4, or H2 with a metal catalyst) gives a sugar alcohol (glucose → sorbitol, xylose → xylitol).

The redox chemistry of a monosaccharide is just the redox chemistry of its aldehyde and alcohol groups from Chapters 4 and 5, applied to one molecule.

Here is why the word "reducing" is used. A sugar whose ring can open exposes an aldehyde, and aldehydes are easily oxidized to carboxylic acids (Chapter 5). When the sugar is oxidized, something else must be reduced: in Benedict's reagent, blue Cu2+ is reduced to brick-red Cu2O; in Tollens' reagent, Ag+ is reduced to a silver mirror. The sugar is the reducing agent, so we call it a reducing sugar. Ketoses count too: in the basic test solution, fructose undergoes a structural rearrangement (called tautomerization) to an aldose, and the aldose then reacts. A sugar whose anomeric carbon is locked in an acetal (a glycosidic bond, Section 7) cannot open, so it is non-reducing.

Reduction to sugar alcohols. Reducing the C=O of a sugar (hydrogen gas with a metal catalyst, or an enzyme in the body) turns the aldehyde or ketone into one more CH2OH or CHOH group. The product is a sugar alcohol (alditol), named by changing -ose to -itol ("ald-it-ol": the aldehyde turned into an alcohol): glucose → sorbitol (also called glucitol), mannose → mannitol, xylose → xylitol, galactose → galactitol. Sugar alcohols taste sweet (sorbitol is about 60% as sweet as sucrose and is found in berries, cherries, plums, and pears), keep the OH groups and the water solubility of sugars, and are used as sugar substitutes in candy and chewing gum and as moisturizing agents in foods and cosmetics. But they have no carbonyl to open and close: no ring, no mutarotation, no positive Benedict's test. Section 6 and its figure put all of the oxidation and reduction products in one place.

4. Disaccharides and polysaccharides

A disaccharide is two monosaccharides joined by a glycosidic bond (glycosidic linkage): one monosaccharide acts as the hemiacetal and the other as the alcohol, and the product is an acetal that links the anomeric carbon of the first sugar to an OH of the second. You name the bond by the anomer (α or β: an α bond points trans to the CH2OH, a β bond cis) and the two carbon numbers it connects, written α(1→4) or, as on the slides, α-1,4. A disaccharide whose second unit still has a free hemiacetal is named by that anomer too (α-maltose, β-maltose), and any disaccharide with a hemiacetal is a reducing sugar. The table collects everything you need for the three dietary disaccharides.

Lactose intolerance is the loss of lactase, from a genetic difference, the normal decline with age, or injury to the intestinal lining. Undigested lactose cannot be absorbed; gut bacteria ferment it, causing cramps and diarrhea.

Polysaccharides contain many monosaccharide units. They come in three kinds: storage polysaccharides (starch, glycogen), structural polysaccharides (cellulose; chitin in insect shells), and acidic polysaccharides (hyaluronic acid in joint fluid). The four you must know are all long chains of glucose, and the differences that matter are the anomer in the link (α or β) and how much branching there is. Starch, the reserve carbohydrate of plants, is a mixture: about 20% amylose (water soluble) and 80% amylopectin (insoluble); hydrolysis of either gives only glucose.

Professor Tip

All four polysaccharides we cover are made of glucose. What makes them different is the α vs. β linkage and the branching, and that is exactly what decides whether we can digest them. A memory hook for the branching: the two branched ones both end in "n" (amylopectin, glycogen); amylose and cellulose are linear.

5. Epimers and functional group isomers

All the hexoses share the formula C6H12O6, so they are isomers of one another. Sorting out which kind of isomer uses the vocabulary from Chapters 2 and 5. Work from the outside in: same connectivity or not? If the same, mirror images or not?

RelationshipTestSugar example
Functional group isomers (a type of constitutional isomer)Same formula, atoms connected differently; different functional groupD-glucose (an aldehyde) and D-fructose (a ketone)
EnantiomersSame connectivity; non-superimposable mirror images; every chiral center is oppositeD-glucose and L-glucose
DiastereomersSame connectivity; stereoisomers that are not mirror images; some but not all centers oppositeD-glucose and D-galactose; D-glucose and D-mannose; also α- and β-D-glucose (anomers)
Epimers (a special diastereomer)Differ at exactly one chiral carbonD-glucose and D-galactose (C4 epimers); D-glucose and D-mannose (C2 epimers)
  • Epimers are diastereomers that differ at only one chiral carbon: D-glucose and D-galactose (C4), and D-glucose and D-mannose (C2).
  • Glucose (an aldose) and fructose (a ketose) are both C6H12O6: they are functional group isomers.
  • Galactose and mannose are both epimers of glucose, but they are not epimers of each other: they differ at two carbons (C2 and C4), so they are simply diastereomers.

6. The three kinds of sugar acids

An aldose has two carbons that can be oxidized to a carboxylic acid: the aldehyde at C1 (easy, as for any aldehyde) and the primary alcohol at C6 (harder; needs a strong oxidant or, in the body, an enzyme). The secondary alcohols in between are never oxidized to acids. Which end gets oxidized decides the name, and the oxidizing agent decides which end: weak oxidizing agents (Tollens', Benedict's) take C1 only; strong oxidizing agents take both ends at once; enzymes can take the C6 alcohol while leaving the aldehyde alone.

TypeCarbon(s) oxidized to COOHFrom glucoseHow it forms; why it matters
Aldonic acidC1 (the aldehyde)gluconic acidMild oxidation: Benedict's or Tollens' reagent, or glucose oxidase in test strips. This is the reaction behind "reducing sugar."
Aldaric acidC1 and the last carbon (C6)glucaric acidStrong oxidation (for example, warm nitric acid); both ends become COOH, a polyhydroxy dicarboxylic acid.
Uronic (alduronic) acidOnly the last carbon (C6); C1 stays an aldehydeglucuronic acidMade by liver enzymes with C1 protected in a ring. Attached to drugs and bilirubin (glucuronidation) to make them water soluble for excretion. Still a reducing sugar because C1 is untouched.

Professor Tip

Take the names apart. Ald-on-ic: the aldehyde was oxidized to the acid and the alcohols were left alone. Ald-ar-ic: the aldehyde and the end alcohol are both acids. Ald-uron-ic: the aldehyde is still there; uron marks the alcohol end that became the acid. Ald-it-ol: the aldehyde turned into an alcohol (-ol). Swap in the sugar's stem (gluc-, galact-, mann-) and you can name every product.

7. Building and breaking glycosidic bonds

A disaccharide forms by condensation: the anomeric OH of one sugar (a hemiacetal) reacts with an OH of another sugar to form an acetal, the glycosidic bond, and releases H2O. Hydrolysis (by acid or by enzymes such as maltase, lactase, and amylase) adds water back and cleaves the C–O glycosidic bond, re-forming the two monosaccharides. Remember the two definitions you will use for the rest of the course: condensation, two molecules join and a small molecule (H2O) is lost; hydrolysis, water splits a molecule. When you are asked where the bonds break and form: in condensation the new bond is C1(anomeric)–O–C4 and the hemiacetal becomes an acetal; in hydrolysis that same C–O bond breaks, the acetal goes back to a hemiacetal, and the other sugar gets its alcohol OH back. The glycosidic link looks like an ether (C–O–C), but because one of those carbons also carries the ring oxygen it is an acetal, which is what makes it hydrolyzable.

You saw both reactions in Chapter 5: hemiacetal + alcohol → acetal + water, and acetal + water (acid catalyst) → hemiacetal + alcohol. Here the "alcohol" is one of the OH groups of a second sugar. Two facts follow from acetal chemistry:

  • A glycosidic bond is stable in base and in neutral water, but it is hydrolyzed by aqueous acid or by a specific enzyme. That is why sucrose in a sealed bottle does not fall apart, but sucrose heated with dilute acid in the lab, or met by sucrase in the small intestine, does.
  • Enzymes are shape-specific. Amylase, maltase, and sucrase hydrolyze α links; lactase hydrolyzes the β(1→4) link of lactose; no human enzyme hydrolyzes the β(1→4) links of cellulose.

Mass is conserved, so you can always balance the equation: two hexoses (2 × C6H12O6) condense to one disaccharide (C12H22O11) plus one H2O, and hydrolysis runs the same equation in reverse. A polysaccharide of n glucose units has lost (n − 1) waters: (C6H10O5)n.

8. Physical properties of monosaccharides

Everything about how a sugar behaves physically comes from its many OH groups and hydrogen bonding, the same reasoning you used for alcohols in Chapter 4.

  • Very water soluble. Five or six OH groups on a six-carbon molecule hydrogen bond strongly with water. Glucose dissolves at roughly 900 g per liter of water at room temperature; sucrose dissolves even more. Sugars are insoluble in nonpolar solvents such as hexane.
  • Crystalline solids with high melting points for molecules of their size (glucose about 146 °C, sucrose about 186 °C) because every OH group hydrogen bonds to its neighbors in the crystal. Compare hexane, a liquid that boils at 69 °C.
  • They decompose instead of boiling. Heat a sugar past its melting point and it darkens and caramelizes (the OH groups and C=O react with each other) rather than distilling. Sugars have no boiling point.
  • Sweet taste, but the relative sweetness varies greatly. Relative to sucrose (defined as 100), fructose is about 170, glucose about 75, maltose about 30, lactose about 15. Sugar alcohols are sweet too (xylitol about 100, sorbitol about 60), which is why they replace sugar in "sugar-free" products. Sucralose (Splenda) is sucrose with three OH groups replaced by Cl; it tastes about 600 times sweeter and is not digested.

When an exam asks "would you expect monosaccharides to have a high melting point / be water soluble, and why?", the answer is the IMF answer: they are polar (dipole–dipole forces) and have many hydroxyl groups that both donate and accept hydrogen bonds, with each other in the solid and with water in solution.

9. Glucose in the blood: the numbers nurses use

Blood glucose is reported in the United States in milligrams per deciliter (mg/dL); 1 dL = 100 mL. A normal fasting level is about 70–99 mg/dL. Below about 70 mg/dL is hypoglycemia (low blood sugar); a fasting value of 126 mg/dL or higher on repeated tests is one of the criteria for diabetes mellitus, and a persistently high level is hyperglycemia. (Many other countries use mmol/L; 18 mg/dL = 1 mmol/L, so 90 mg/dL is 5.0 mmol/L. Check the unit before you interpret a value.) Glucose meters and test strips use the enzyme glucose oxidase, which oxidizes C1 of glucose to gluconic acid, the aldonic acid from Section 6; the strip measures the electrons released. It is reducing-sugar chemistry made specific for glucose.

Normal blood glucose is often quoted on the slides as 70–110 mg/dL; the fasting range used by most laboratories today is 70–99 mg/dL. A glucose tolerance test measures blood glucose for several hours after a measured dose of glucose. Insulin regulates the level by moving glucose into tissues and promoting glycogen formation; in diabetes too little insulin is made or it works poorly, so patients monitor their diet and glucose, and some must inject insulin.

10. Carbohydrates on cell surfaces: blood types

Short chains of monosaccharides attached to membrane proteins and lipids act as cell-identity tags. The ABO blood types are the best-known example. Every red blood cell carries a core chain of three or four monosaccharides (the O antigen). Type A cells add a fourth sugar, an amino sugar (N-acetylgalactosamine); type B cells add a D-galactose instead; type AB cells carry both chains; type O carries only the core. The immune system makes antibodies against the sugar it lacks, so a transfusion of the wrong type causes the cells to clump (agglutinate), a potentially fatal reaction. In the United States type O is the most common blood type and type A the second. Only the last sugar on a short chain differs, which is why blood typing is, at bottom, carbohydrate chemistry.

Health Career Connection

  • Dental hygiene – caries: Oral bacteria such as Streptococcus mutans ferment sucrose and other sugars into lactic acid. When plaque pH falls below about 5.5, enamel starts to demineralize. Bacteria also use sucrose to build sticky glucose polymers that help plaque attach to teeth.
  • Dental hygiene – xylitol: Xylitol is a sugar alcohol (reduced xylose). It tastes sweet, but cariogenic bacteria cannot ferment it to acid, so it is used in sugar-free gums and mints.
  • Nursing – IV fluids and glucose testing: "Dextrose" in D5W IV fluid is D-glucose. Glucose test strips use enzyme-based oxidation of glucose, an updated version of the reducing-sugar idea.
  • Nursing – lactose intolerance and galactosemia: Without the enzyme lactase, lactose is not hydrolyzed and is fermented by gut bacteria. In galactosemia, an enzyme for using galactose is missing, so infants must avoid lactose.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

D5W is 5% dextrose in water: 5 g of D-glucose per 100 mL (about 170 kcal/L). HbA1c (glycated hemoglobin) is reducing-sugar chemistry inside a red blood cell: the open-chain fraction of glucose has a free aldehyde, which reacts with an amine group of hemoglobin (the N-terminal valine of the β chain) and stays attached for the life of the cell, about 120 days. Higher average glucose means a larger glycated fraction, so HbA1c (normal below 5.7%; 6.5% or higher supports a diagnosis of diabetes) reports the average over two to three months, not today's value. Above the kidney's threshold (about 180 mg/dL) glucose spills into the urine (glucosuria). Dipsticks use glucose oxidase and detect only glucose; the older copper-reduction tablet test reacts with any reducing sugar, which is why it was used to screen infants' urine for galactose (galactosemia). The liver attaches glucuronic acid (the alduronic acid of glucose) to bilirubin and to drugs such as acetaminophen to make them water soluble (glucuronidation); newborns do this slowly, one cause of neonatal jaundice. Two sugar alcohols are drugs: IV mannitol pulls water from tissue by osmosis (an osmotic diuretic); oral sorbitol holds water in the gut (an osmotic laxative). Heparin, the IV anticoagulant, is a sulfated polysaccharide of uronic acids and amino sugars, too large and charged to be absorbed from the gut.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Dental caries (tooth decay) is carbohydrate chemistry gone wrong. Streptococcus mutans and other plaque bacteria ferment sucrose, glucose, and fructose to lactic acid. Within minutes of a sugary snack, plaque pH falls below the critical pH of about 5.5 and hydroxyapatite in enamel begins to demineralize; saliva's bicarbonate buffer needs roughly 30 to 60 minutes to restore the pH so remineralization can resume (the Stephan curve). Frequent sipping keeps the pH low. Sucrose is doubly bad: bacterial enzymes also polymerize its glucose into sticky glucans, the scaffold of plaque biofilm. Starch that sticks to teeth is hydrolyzed by salivary amylase to maltose and glucose that bacteria can use. Xylitol and sorbitol, the sugar alcohols in sugar-free gum, taste sweet but have no carbonyl and cannot be fermented to acid; xylitol also enters S. mutans and inhibits its growth, and chewing raises salivary flow. Sucrose-sweetened liquid pediatric medicines and bottles of juice or formula at bedtime cause early childhood caries ("baby-bottle caries"). Two polysaccharides are tools of the trade: alginate impression material, a polymer of uronic acids from brown algae that gels with calcium ions, and carboxymethylcellulose, which thickens toothpaste and artificial saliva for patients with xerostomia (dry mouth).

In the Lab

  • Experiment 8a – Types of Carbohydrates and 8b – Tests for Carbohydrates
  • Benedict’s test: reducing sugars turn blue Cu2+ into a green, gold, or brick-red Cu2O precipitate (more sugar = redder). Ketoses also react because fructose rearranges to an aldose in base. Sucrose is negative.
  • Seliwanoff’s test: ketoses (fructose) give a deep red color quickly; aldoses give only a light pink slowly.
  • Fermentation: yeast ferments most mono- and disaccharides (CO2 bubbles), but not lactose (galactose ferments slowly, if at all).
  • Iodine test: amylose (starch) traps iodine in its helix and turns blue-black.
  • You will hydrolyze di- and polysaccharides, test foods, and identify an unknown sugar.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Classify and count chiral carbons

Problem. D-ribose is an open-chain five-carbon sugar with a CHO at C1. Classify it and count its chiral carbons.

Solution

Aldehyde + 5 carbons = aldopentose. C2, C3, and C4 each have four different groups, so there are 3 chiral carbons. C1 (CHO) and C5 (CH2OH) are not chiral.

Check. An aldose with n carbons has n − 2 chiral carbons in its open chain: 5 − 2 = 3.

Example 2: α or β?

Problem. In a Haworth drawing of D-glucose, the CH2OH points up and the OH on C1 points down. Which anomer is it?

Solution

Anomeric OH opposite the CH2OH (down) = α-D-glucose.

Check. For a D sugar the CH2OH is up, so "OH down" and "opposite the CH2OH" say the same thing: α.

Example 3: Reducing sugar?

Problem. Which give a positive Benedict's test: glucose, fructose, maltose, sucrose?

Solution

Glucose, fructose, and maltose: positive. (Fructose is a ketose, but under the basic test conditions it rearranges to an aldose, so it still reacts.) Sucrose: negative. Its α1–β2 link ties up both anomeric carbons, so no ring can open.

Check. One question decides it: is there a free hemiacetal (anomeric OH)? Yes for the three monosaccharides and the right-hand unit of maltose; no for sucrose.

Example 4: Hydrolysis

Problem. What monosaccharides form when sucrase hydrolyzes sucrose?

Solution

Sucrose + H2O → D-glucose + D-fructose (the mixture is "invert sugar").

Check. C12H22O11 + H2O gives 2 C6H12O6: one hexose from each side of the acetal link.

Example 5: Read a Fischer projection

Problem. A sugar is drawn as a Fischer projection: CHO at the top, then four chiral carbons with OH on the left (C2), right (C3), right (C4), and left (C5), and CH2OH at the bottom. Classify it, assign D or L, and decide how it is related to D-glucose.

Solution. CHO at C1 makes it an aldose; six carbons make it an aldohexose. The chiral carbon farthest from the C=O is C5, and its OH is on the left, so the sugar is L. Compare it with D-glucose (OH right, left, right, right): this sugar is left, right, right, left, which is the opposite at every one of the four centers. Opposite at every center means mirror images: it is L-glucose, the enantiomer of D-glucose.

Check. Four chiral carbons were compared and all four differ. If only one had differed, the answer would have been "epimer"; if two or three, "diastereomer." The L assignment agrees, because an enantiomer of a D sugar must be L.

Example 6: Fischer to Haworth

Problem. Draw β-D-galactose as a Haworth projection, starting from its Fischer projection (OH on C2 right, C3 left, C4 left, C5 right).

Solution. Draw the pyranose ring with the oxygen at the back right and C1 at the right; number clockwise. Apply "right → down, left → up": C2 OH down, C3 OH up, C4 OH up. The sugar is D, so the C6 CH2OH on C5 points up. The anomer is β, so the new OH on C1 points up, on the same side as the CH2OH. Fill each remaining position with H.

Check. The drawing should look exactly like the β-D-glucose drawing in Section 2 except at C4, where the OH is up instead of down, because galactose is the C4 epimer of glucose. One flipped position, one epimer: consistent.

Example 7: Condensation and hydrolysis of a glycosidic bond

Problem. (a) Two D-glucose molecules condense to form maltose. Write the balanced equation and name the type of functional group created. (b) Starch is boiled with dilute acid until it tests negative with iodine and positive with Benedict's reagent. Explain both results.

Solution. (a) The anomeric (C1) OH of α-D-glucose, a hemiacetal, condenses with the C4 OH of a second glucose: 2 C6H12O6 → C12H22O11 + H2O. The new C1–O–C4 link is an acetal, called an α(1→4) glycosidic bond. (b) Acid hydrolyzes acetals. The long α(1→4) chains of amylose are cut into maltose and finally glucose, so the helix that traps iodine is gone (negative iodine test), and the free hemiacetal of every glucose molecule can open to an aldehyde (positive Benedict's test).

Check. Atoms balance: 12 C, 24 H, 12 O on each side of (a). In (b), intact starch would have given the opposite pair of results (iodine positive, Benedict's essentially negative), so the change in both tests confirms hydrolysis.

Example 8: Predict the oxidation or reduction product

Problem. Name the product and its class when D-galactose is (a) treated with Benedict's reagent, (b) oxidized only at C6 by an enzyme, (c) oxidized at both C1 and C6, and (d) reduced with hydrogen and a catalyst.

Solution. Use the glucose pattern and change the stem to "galact-": (a) C1 aldehyde → COOH gives galactonic acid, an aldonic acid (Cu2+ is reduced to Cu2O). (b) C6 CH2OH → COOH with C1 untouched gives galacturonic acid, an alduronic (uronic) acid. (c) Both ends oxidized gives galactaric acid, an aldaric acid. (d) C1 CHO → CH2OH gives galactitol, a sugar alcohol.

Check. The suffixes tell you the carbons: -onic (C1), -uronic (C6), -aric (both), -itol (reduced). Only (b) and the starting sugar still have an aldehyde, so only they would still be reducing sugars.

Example 9: Draw the enantiomer

Problem. D-Mannose has the Fischer pattern left, left, right, right for the OH groups on C2–C5. Draw (describe) the Fischer projection of L-mannose and state how the two are related to each other and to D-glucose.

Solution. An enantiomer is the mirror image, so every chiral center flips. L-Mannose: CHO at the top, then OH on the right at C2, right at C3, left at C4, left at C5, CH2OH at the bottom. The bottom-most chiral carbon (C5) now has its OH on the left, which is what L means. D- and L-mannose are enantiomers. Compared with D-glucose (right, left, right, right), D-mannose differs only at C2 (its C2 epimer); L-mannose differs from D-glucose at C3, C4, and C5, so it is simply a diastereomer of D-glucose.

Check. Flipping every center must flip the D/L label, and it did. If you flip only the C5 OH you have not drawn the enantiomer; you have drawn a different sugar (the C5 epimer of D-mannose, which happens to be an L sugar but is not the mirror image).

Example 10: Haworth projection of a ketose

Problem. Draw β-D-fructose as a five-membered (furanose) ring. The Fischer projection of D-fructose has CH2OH at C1, C=O at C2, and OH groups left (C3), right (C4), right (C5), with CH2OH at C6.

Solution. The ring forms when the C5 OH adds to the C2 ketone, so the ring contains C2, C3, C4, C5, and the oxygen: a furanose. Draw the pentagon with the ring O at the back and the anomeric carbon (C2) at the right. Apply the usual rules: right in Fischer → down, left → up. C3 OH (left) is up; C4 OH (right) is down. C5 is in the ring and carries the rest of the chain, C6 CH2OH, which points up because the sugar is D. The anomeric carbon C2 carries two groups: the C1 CH2OH and the new OH. For the β anomer the OH is up, cis to the C6 CH2OH, so the C1 CH2OH goes down.

Check. A ketose ring has an extra CH2OH hanging on the anomeric carbon; that is how you spot a fructose unit in sucrose. Count: C1 and C6 outside the ring, C2–C5 inside, six carbons total.

Example 11: Haworth projection of an L sugar

Problem. Draw α-L-galactose.

Solution. Start from D-galactose (right, left, left, right) and flip every center to get L-galactose: left, right, right, left. Now convert: left → up, right → down, so C2 OH up, C3 OH down, C4 OH down. The C5 OH is on the left, so the C6 CH2OH points down (L). For α the anomeric OH is trans to the CH2OH; since the CH2OH is down, the α OH is up.

Check. The whole drawing is the α-D-galactose drawing turned upside down position by position, which is what a mirror image should look like. The CH2OH down is the giveaway for L; then "α = trans" places the anomeric OH.

Example 12: Read a disaccharide drawing

Problem. For Disaccharide W below: (a) identify the two monosaccharides; (b) name the glycosidic linkage (α or β, and the carbon numbers); (c) locate the anomeric carbons and state whether W is a reducing sugar; (d) write the products of hydrolysis.

Solution. (a) Read each ring back to a Fischer pattern (down = right, up = left). Left ring: C2 down, C3 up, C4 down (it is bonded to the bridging O through C1, and its C4 OH is down): right, left, right, and the CH2OH is up, so it is D-glucose. Right ring: the same pattern at C2 and C3, CH2OH up, so it is also D-glucose. (b) The bridge runs from C1 of the left ring to the oxygen on C4 of the right ring. The bond leaving C1 points up, cis to the CH2OH, so the link is β(1→4). (c) C1 of the left ring is an acetal (bonded to two oxygens, no OH): locked. C1 of the right ring still carries an OH and the ring O: a free hemiacetal, so the ring can open to an aldehyde and W is a reducing sugar (it is β-cellobiose, the repeating unit of cellulose). (d) W + H2O → 2 D-glucose, by acid or by a β-glucosidase; human maltase cannot do it.

Check. Compare with maltose (Figure 8.3): same two sugars, same carbons joined; the only difference is that the bond at C1 points down (α) in maltose and up (β) here. That one change is the difference between starch and cellulose.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Deciding D/L from C2. Use the chiral carbon farthest from the C=O (C5 in glucose).
  • Mixing up α and β. Compare the anomeric OH with the CH2OH: opposite = α, same side = β.
  • Calling sucrose a reducing sugar.
  • Thinking cellulose is made of a different sugar than starch. Both are glucose; only the linkage differs.
  • Calling α- and β-D-glucose enantiomers. They differ at one carbon (C1) only, so they are diastereomers (anomers). Enantiomers differ at every chiral carbon (D-glucose vs. L-glucose).
  • Calling galactose and mannose epimers of each other. Each is an epimer of glucose, but they differ from each other at two carbons.
  • Thinking a sugar alcohol such as sorbitol can form a ring or give a positive Benedict's test. With no C=O there is no hemiacetal and nothing to oxidize.
  • Forgetting the water. Condensation of two hexoses releases one H2O (C12H22O11, not C12H24O12); hydrolysis consumes it.
  • Drawing the CH2OH up for an L sugar, or forgetting that the α/β call is made relative to the CH2OH. For an L sugar the CH2OH is down, so the α (trans) OH is up.
  • Calling the glycosidic bond a hemiacetal. The bond itself is an acetal (the anomeric carbon has two OR groups); the hemiacetal is the free anomeric carbon that may be left on the other unit.
  • Flipping only one OH when asked for an enantiomer. An enantiomer flips every chiral center; flipping one gives an epimer.

Exercise 1

Classify D-fructose and give its number of chiral carbons (open chain).

Answer
Ketohexose; 3 chiral carbons (C3, C4, C5)

Exercise 2

What sugar alcohol forms when D-glucose is reduced? Is it a reducing sugar?

Answer
D-sorbitol (glucitol). No. It has no C=O or hemiacetal left to oxidize.

Exercise 3

A polysaccharide has α(1→4) links with α(1→6) branches about every 10 glucose units and is stored in liver. Name it.

Answer
Glycogen

Exercise 4

Lactose is hydrolyzed by lactase. Name the products and the linkage that was broken.

Answer
D-galactose and D-glucose; a β(1→4) glycosidic bond

Exercise 5

D-Glucose and D-mannose differ only in the arrangement at C2. What term describes this pair?

Answer
Epimers (a special kind of diastereomer)

Exercise 6

The liver attaches glucuronic acid to drugs and bilirubin to make them water soluble for excretion. Which carbon of glucose was oxidized to make it?

Answer
C6 (the CH2OH becomes COOH); it is a uronic acid.

Exercise 7

A patient's fasting blood glucose is reported as 6.0 mmol/L. Convert it to mg/dL and state whether it is in the normal fasting range (70–99 mg/dL).

Answer
6.0 mmol/L × 18 mg/dL per mmol/L = 108 mg/dL. That is above the normal fasting range (but below the 126 mg/dL diabetes criterion).

Exercise 8

Pure β-D-glucose is dissolved in water. After an hour, what fraction of the molecules is still β, and what process explains the change?

Answer
About 64% β (36% α, a trace of open chain). Mutarotation: the ring opens to the aldehyde and re-closes with the C1 OH up or down until equilibrium is reached.
