Learning Objectives

  • Classify monosaccharides (aldose/ketose and number of carbons, e.g., aldohexose).
  • Assign D or L from a Fischer projection, and count chiral carbons.
  • Distinguish enantiomers, diastereomers, epimers, and functional group isomers among sugars.
  • Convert a Fischer projection to a Haworth ring and label the α and β anomers.
  • Identify reducing sugars and predict oxidation and reduction products (aldonic, alduronic, and aldaric acids; sugar alcohols).
  • Describe the glycosidic links and monomers in common disaccharides and polysaccharides, and write condensation and hydrolysis equations for them.

Carbohydrates are the sugars and the polymers built from them. In this chapter you will meet only a handful of molecules (glucose, galactose, fructose, three disaccharides, four polysaccharides), but the chemistry you already know shows up everywhere: aldehydes and ketones, hemiacetals and acetals, oxidation of aldehydes and alcohols, and chirality. This guide is the short version; the teaching, figures, worked examples, and clinical boxes behind each Key Idea sit on the section pages, in the Lecture Connection block after each section's text.

Chapter Summary

The big ideas of the chapter, one paragraph each. Each paragraph ends with the section where it is taught in full.

1. Monosaccharides

Carbohydrates (saccharides) are polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones, CnH2nOn, about 65% of the diet. Classify by carbonyl (aldose, CHO at C1; ketose, C=O at C2) and carbon count (triose to hexose): glucose is an aldohexose, fructose a ketohexose, ribose an aldopentose. In a Fischer projection, horizontal bonds come toward you and every cross is a chiral carbon; D if the OH on the bottom-most chiral carbon is on the right, L if on the left. Natural sugars are D. Know glucose ("right, left, right, right"), galactose (C4 flipped), and fructose (same C3–C5 as glucose, ketone at C2). Details and examples: Sections 8.1, 8.2, 8.3.

2. Rings: hemiacetals again

One of the sugar's own OH groups adds to its C=O to give a cyclic hemiacetal: a six-membered pyranose for glucose (C5 OH to C1), a five-membered furanose for fructose (C5 OH to C2). The old carbonyl carbon is the new chiral anomeric carbon. Haworth rules: right in Fischer → down, left → up; the CH2OH is up for D, down for L; the anomeric OH is α = trans to the CH2OH (the symbol crosses itself) or β = cis (bumps on the same side). Fischer to Haworth in three steps: rotate, twist, close. In water the anomers interconvert through the open chain (mutarotation; about 36% α, 64% β, a trace of aldehyde). Details, the three-step method, and examples: Section 8.4.

3. Reactions

A reducing sugar has a free hemiacetal that can open to an aldehyde and reduce Benedict's (Cu2+ → red Cu2O) or Tollens' reagent; all monosaccharides and most disaccharides qualify, sucrose does not; ketoses react because they rearrange to aldoses in base. Weak oxidation of C1 gives a sugar acid (glucose → gluconic acid); reduction of the C=O gives a sugar alcohol (glucose → sorbitol, xylose → xylitol, named -ose → -itol), which has no ring, no mutarotation, and no positive Benedict's test. Details, the reducing-sugar table, and the lab tests: Section 8.5.

4. Disaccharides and polysaccharides

A glycosidic bond is an acetal linking the anomeric carbon of one sugar to an OH of another; name it by the anomer and the carbons, α(1→4) or α-1,4. Maltose = glucose + glucose, α(1→4), reducing; lactose = galactose + glucose, β(1→4), reducing; sucrose = glucose + fructose, α,β(1→2), non-reducing because both anomeric carbons are tied up. Each is hydrolyzed by its own intestinal enzyme (maltase, lactase, sucrase); missing lactase is lactose intolerance. The four polysaccharides are all glucose: amylose α(1→4) linear, amylopectin α(1→4)/α(1→6) branched, glycogen the same but more branched (animal storage), cellulose β(1→4) linear and indigestible to humans. Details, tables, and figures: Sections 8.6 and 8.7.

5. Epimers and functional group isomers

Work from the outside in: same connectivity? If not, constitutional isomers (glucose and fructose are functional group isomers). If yes, mirror images? Enantiomers differ at every chiral carbon (D- and L-glucose); diastereomers differ at some but not all (including the α/β anomers); epimers differ at exactly one (glucose/galactose at C4, glucose/mannose at C2). Galactose and mannose are not epimers of each other. Details and examples: Section 8.2.

6. The three kinds of sugar acids

Oxidize C1 only (weak oxidant: Benedict's, Tollens', glucose oxidase) → aldonic acid (gluconic); oxidize C1 and C6 (strong oxidant) → aldaric acid (glucaric); oxidize C6 only (enzymes, C1 protected in the ring) → alduronic acid (glucuronic, used by the liver to excrete drugs and bilirubin); reduce C1 → alditol (sorbitol). Take the names apart: ald-on-ic (alcohols left alone), ald-ar-ic (both ends are acids), ald-uron-ic (aldehyde still there), ald-it-ol. Details and figure: Section 8.5.

7. Building and breaking glycosidic bonds

Condensation: hemiacetal (anomeric OH) + alcohol OH of a second sugar → acetal (the glycosidic bond) + H2O; 2 C6H12O6 → C12H22O11 + H2O. Hydrolysis (acid or a specific enzyme; stable in base and neutral water) runs it backward and re-forms the hemiacetal. Amylase, maltase, and sucrase cut α links; lactase cuts lactose's β(1→4); no human enzyme cuts cellulose. Details: Section 8.6.

8. Physical properties of monosaccharides

Many OH groups mean strong hydrogen bonding: very water soluble, insoluble in hexane, crystalline solids with high melting points that caramelize rather than boil. Sweetness varies (sucrose 100: fructose about 170, glucose 75, maltose 30, lactose 15; xylitol about 100, sorbitol 60). Details: Section 8.5.

9. Glucose in the blood: the numbers nurses use

Blood glucose is reported in mg/dL; normal fasting about 70–99 mg/dL (the slides say 70–110), below 70 is hypoglycemia, 100–125 fasting is prediabetes, 126 or above fasting is a diabetes criterion; 18 mg/dL = 1 mmol/L. Meters use an enzyme (glucose oxidase or glucose dehydrogenase) that oxidizes C1. HbA1c is glucose's open-chain aldehyde bonded to hemoglobin, a two-to-three-month average (normal below 5.7%, prediabetes 5.7–6.4%, diabetes 6.5% or higher). Details: Sections 8.3 and 8.4.

10. Carbohydrates on cell surfaces: blood types

Short sugar chains on membrane proteins and lipids are identity tags. ABO types share a core chain (O); type A adds N-acetylgalactosamine, type B adds D-galactose, AB has both; antibodies against the missing sugar make a mismatched transfusion agglutinate. Details: Section 8.3.

Health Career Connection

Plaque bacteria ferment sucrose and other sugars to lactic acid; below the critical pH of about 5.5 enamel demineralizes, and sucrose also builds the sticky glucans of plaque biofilm (Section 8.6). Xylitol and sorbitol are sugar alcohols that taste sweet but are fermented to acid poorly or not at all (Section 8.5). "Dextrose" in D5W is D-glucose, and glucose strips use enzyme oxidation of C1 (Section 8.3). Lactose intolerance is missing lactase; galactosemia is a missing galactose enzyme and calls for lactose-free formula (Section 8.6). Heparin and alginate are polysaccharides (Section 8.7).

Worked Examples

These five are the exam-style tasks. More worked examples are on the section pages (8.2, 8.4, 8.5, 8.6).

Example 1: Classify and count chiral carbons

Problem. D-ribose is an open-chain five-carbon sugar with a CHO at C1. Classify it and count its chiral carbons.

Solution

Aldehyde + 5 carbons = aldopentose. C2, C3, and C4 each have four different groups, so there are 3 chiral carbons. C1 (CHO) and C5 (CH2OH) are not chiral.

Check. An aldose with n carbons has n − 2 chiral carbons in its open chain: 5 − 2 = 3.

Example 2: Hydrolysis

Problem. What monosaccharides form when sucrase hydrolyzes sucrose?

Solution

Sucrose + H2O → D-glucose + D-fructose (the mixture is "invert sugar").

Check. C12H22O11 + H2O gives 2 C6H12O6: one hexose from each side of the acetal link.

Example 3: Fischer to Haworth

Problem. Draw β-D-galactose as a Haworth projection, starting from its Fischer projection (OH on C2 right, C3 left, C4 left, C5 right).

Solution. Draw the pyranose ring with the oxygen at the back right and C1 at the right; number clockwise. Apply "right → down, left → up": C2 OH down, C3 OH up, C4 OH up. The sugar is D, so the C6 CH2OH on C5 points up. The anomer is β, so the new OH on C1 points up, on the same side as the CH2OH. Fill each remaining position with H.

Check. The drawing should look exactly like the β-D-glucose drawing in Section 8.4 except at C4, where the OH is up instead of down, because galactose is the C4 epimer of glucose. One flipped position, one epimer: consistent.

Example 4: Condensation and hydrolysis of a glycosidic bond

Problem. (a) Two D-glucose molecules condense to form maltose. Write the balanced equation and name the type of functional group created. (b) Starch is boiled with dilute acid until it tests negative with iodine and positive with Benedict's reagent. Explain both results.

Solution. (a) The anomeric (C1) OH of α-D-glucose, a hemiacetal, condenses with the C4 OH of a second glucose: 2 C6H12O6 → C12H22O11 + H2O. The new C1–O–C4 link is an acetal, called an α(1→4) glycosidic bond. (b) Acid hydrolyzes acetals. The long α(1→4) chains of amylose are cut into maltose and finally glucose, so the helix that traps iodine is gone (negative iodine test), and the free hemiacetal of every glucose molecule can open to an aldehyde (positive Benedict's test).

Check. Atoms balance: 12 C, 24 H, 12 O on each side of (a). In (b), intact starch would have given the opposite pair of results (iodine positive, Benedict's essentially negative), so the change in both tests confirms hydrolysis.

Example 5: Predict the oxidation or reduction product

Problem. Name the product and its class when D-galactose is (a) treated with Benedict's reagent, (b) oxidized only at C6 by an enzyme, (c) oxidized at both C1 and C6, and (d) reduced with hydrogen and a catalyst.

Solution. Use the glucose pattern and change the stem to "galact-": (a) C1 aldehyde → COOH gives galactonic acid, an aldonic acid (Cu2+ is reduced to Cu2O). (b) C6 CH2OH → COOH with C1 untouched gives galacturonic acid, an alduronic (uronic) acid. (c) Both ends oxidized gives galactaric acid, an aldaric acid. (d) C1 CHO → CH2OH gives galactitol, a sugar alcohol.

Check. The suffixes tell you the carbons: -onic (C1), -uronic (C6), -aric (both), -itol (reduced). Only (b) and the starting sugar still have an aldehyde, so only they would still be reducing sugars.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Deciding D/L from C2. Use the chiral carbon farthest from the C=O (C5 in glucose).
  • Mixing up α and β. Compare the anomeric OH with the CH2OH: opposite = α, same side = β.
  • Calling sucrose a reducing sugar.
  • Thinking cellulose is made of a different sugar than starch. Both are glucose; only the linkage differs.
  • Calling α- and β-D-glucose enantiomers. They differ at one carbon (C1) only, so they are diastereomers (anomers). Enantiomers differ at every chiral carbon (D-glucose vs. L-glucose).
  • Calling galactose and mannose epimers of each other. Each is an epimer of glucose, but they differ from each other at two carbons.
  • Thinking a sugar alcohol such as sorbitol can form a ring or give a positive Benedict's test. With no C=O there is no hemiacetal and nothing to oxidize.
  • Forgetting the water. Condensation of two hexoses releases one H2O (C12H22O11, not C12H24O12); hydrolysis consumes it.
  • Drawing the CH2OH up for an L sugar, or forgetting that the α/β call is made relative to the CH2OH. For an L sugar the CH2OH is down, so the α (trans) OH is up.
  • Calling the glycosidic bond a hemiacetal. The bond itself is an acetal (the anomeric carbon has two OR groups); the hemiacetal is the free anomeric carbon that may be left on the other unit.
  • Flipping only one OH when asked for an enantiomer. An enantiomer flips every chiral center; flipping one gives an epimer.

Clinical Update: Rosa’s Story, Part 8 of 15

Sugar, fiber and xylitol gum

At her next nutrition visit, Rosa learns to count carbohydrates. The dietitian points out that starch in tortillas and rice is digested to glucose, while the fiber in beans and vegetables, including cellulose, is not digested; it passes through without being absorbed, and soluble fiber slows the rise in blood glucose after a meal. Table sugar, sucrose, breaks down into glucose and fructose.

For her dry mouth, the hygienist suggests chewing sugar-free gum sweetened with xylitol. Chewing increases saliva, and the mouth bacteria that make acid cannot ferment xylitol.

Case Question 8.1

Xylitol is a sugar alcohol. How is a sugar alcohol made from a sugar?

Answer
By reducing the sugar’s carbonyl group (aldehyde or ketone) to an –OH group. Xylitol comes from reducing the aldehyde of xylose, so every carbon in xylitol carries an –OH.

Case Question 8.2

Starch and cellulose are both polymers of glucose. Why can Rosa digest one but not the other?

Answer
Starch is joined by α(1→4) glycosidic bonds (with α(1→6) branches), which human digestive enzymes, starting with amylase, hydrolyze. Cellulose is joined by β(1→4) bonds, and humans have no enzyme that breaks them.

Case Question 8.3

Is sucrose a reducing sugar? Explain.

Answer
No. The glycosidic bond in sucrose joins the anomeric carbons of both glucose and fructose, so neither ring can open to a free aldehyde or ketone, and sucrose gives a negative Benedict’s test.

Where this chemistry is taught: Section 8.5, Section 8.6, Section 8.7. Rosa’s story continues at the start of Chapter 9.

Practice

Exercise 1

Classify D-fructose and give its number of chiral carbons (open chain).

Answer
Ketohexose; 3 chiral carbons (C3, C4, C5)

Exercise 2

What sugar alcohol forms when D-glucose is reduced? Is it a reducing sugar?

Answer
D-sorbitol (glucitol). No. It has no C=O or hemiacetal left to oxidize.

Exercise 3

A polysaccharide has α(1→4) links with α(1→6) branches about every 8 to 12 glucose units and is stored in liver. Name it.

Answer
Glycogen

Exercise 4

Lactose is hydrolyzed by lactase. Name the products and the linkage that was broken.

Answer
D-galactose and D-glucose; a β(1→4) glycosidic bond

Exercise 5

D-Glucose and D-mannose differ only in the arrangement at C2. What term describes this pair?

Answer
Epimers (a special kind of diastereomer)

Exercise 6

The liver attaches glucuronic acid to drugs and bilirubin to make them water soluble for excretion. Which carbon of glucose was oxidized to make it?

Answer
C6 (the CH2OH becomes COOH); it is a uronic acid.

Exercise 7

A patient's fasting blood glucose is reported as 6.0 mmol/L. Convert it to mg/dL and state whether it is in the normal fasting range (70–99 mg/dL).

Answer
6.0 mmol/L × 18 mg/dL per mmol/L = 108 mg/dL. That is above the normal fasting range and in the prediabetes range (100–125 mg/dL), below the 126 mg/dL diabetes criterion.

Exercise 8

Pure β-D-glucose is dissolved in water. After an hour, what fraction of the molecules is still β, and what process explains the change?

Answer
About 64% β (36% α, a trace of open chain). Mutarotation: the ring opens to the aldehyde and re-closes with the C1 OH up or down until equilibrium is reached.
