Learning Objectives

  • Classify monosaccharides (aldose/ketose and number of carbons, e.g., aldohexose).
  • Assign D or L from a Fischer projection, and count chiral carbons.
  • Distinguish enantiomers, diastereomers, epimers, and functional group isomers among sugars.
  • Convert a Fischer projection to a Haworth ring and label the α and β anomers.
  • Identify reducing sugars and predict oxidation and reduction products (aldonic, alduronic, and aldaric acids; sugar alcohols).
  • Describe the glycosidic links and monomers in common disaccharides and polysaccharides, and write condensation and hydrolysis equations for them.

Carbohydrates are the sugars and the polymers built from them. In this chapter you will meet only a handful of molecules (glucose, galactose, fructose, three disaccharides, four polysaccharides), but the chemistry you already know shows up everywhere: aldehydes and ketones, hemiacetals and acetals, oxidation of aldehydes and alcohols, and chirality. If you can draw a hemiacetal from Chapter 5 and assign D/L from Chapter 5, you can do everything here.

Key Ideas

1. Monosaccharides

A monosaccharide (simple sugar) is a small polyhydroxy aldehyde or ketone: one C=O plus an OH on almost every other carbon. The general formula is (CH2O)n, which is where the name "carbo-hydrate" comes from.

  • If the C=O is an aldehyde (at C1), the sugar is an aldose. If it is a ketone (usually at C2), it is a ketose.
  • Combine that with the carbon count: glucose = aldohexose, fructose = ketohexose, ribose = aldopentose, glyceraldehyde = aldotriose.
  • D or L: look at the chiral carbon farthest from the C=O. OH on the right = D. Nearly all sugars in the body are D.
  • Know glucose, galactose, and fructose. Galactose differs from glucose only at C4.

The table below is the whole classification system: pick the row for the carbon count and the column for the type of carbonyl.

CarbonsAldose (CHO at C1)Ketose (C=O at C2)
3 (triose)glyceraldehyde (aldotriose)dihydroxyacetone (ketotriose; no chiral carbon)
4 (tetrose)erythrose (aldotetrose)erythrulose (ketotetrose)
5 (pentose)ribose (aldopentose; in RNA)ribulose (ketopentose)
6 (hexose)glucose, galactose, mannose (aldohexoses)fructose (ketohexose)

Fischer projections. A Fischer projection draws the carbon chain vertically with the C=O at (or nearest) the top. Each horizontal line is a bond that points toward you; the vertical bonds point away. Every crossing point is a chiral carbon. Number the carbons from the top, starting with the end closest to the C=O. To assign D or L, find the chiral carbon farthest from the C=O (the penultimate carbon, next to the bottom CH2OH): OH on the right = D, OH on the left = L. The other OH groups do not matter for D/L; they decide which sugar it is.

Professor Tip

Say the glucose pattern out loud as "right, left, right, right" for the OH groups on C2, C3, C4, C5. Galactose is "right, left, left, right" (C4 flipped). Mannose is "left, left, right, right" (C2 flipped). Fructose has the same C3–C5 pattern as glucose ("left, right, right") but with a ketone at C2 instead of an aldehyde at C1. In Carb Lab you can build each one from these patterns.

2. Rings: hemiacetals again

In water, a C5 OH attacks the C=O (C1 in glucose) and forms a cyclic hemiacetal. The old carbonyl carbon is the anomeric carbon (the "goggle eyes" carbon from Chapter 5).

This is the same reaction you learned for aldehydes and ketones, just happening inside one molecule: an alcohol adds to a carbonyl to give a carbon with both an OH and an OR (an intramolecular hemiacetal). For glucose the attacking OH is on C5, so the ring contains five carbons plus one oxygen: a six-membered pyranose ring. For fructose the ketone is at C2; when the C5 OH attacks it, the ring is five-membered (four carbons plus the oxygen), a furanose, with the anomeric carbon at C2. This is the fructose ring you will draw and the one found in sucrose. Open-chain and ring forms are in equilibrium, but in water almost all of the sugar (more than 99%) is in a ring at any moment.

  • In a Haworth projection, groups on the right in the Fischer projection point down, and groups on the left point up.
  • For D-sugars: anomeric OH down (opposite the CH2OH) = α; OH up (same side as the CH2OH) = β.
  • α and β forms interconvert in solution through the open chain (mutarotation).

Haworth projection rules (the ring drawn flat, viewed edge-on, with the ring oxygen at the back right and C1 at the right):

  1. Number clockwise starting from the anomeric carbon (C1) at the right.
  2. Right in Fischer → down in Haworth; left in Fischer → up. For D-glucose: C2 OH down, C3 OH up, C4 OH down.
  3. For every D sugar the terminal CH2OH (C6) points up on C5. (It points down for an L sugar.)
  4. The new anomeric OH on C1 can be down (α) or up (β). The two anomers are not enantiomers of each other; they differ only at the one new chiral center created when the ring closed.

Mutarotation. If you dissolve pure crystalline α-D-glucose in water, its optical rotation drifts over an hour or two and settles at a fixed value. The ring is opening to the free aldehyde and re-closing, and each time it re-closes the OH can end up down or up. The result is the equilibrium mixture above. The open chain is a tiny fraction, but it matters: it is the form that can be oxidized (Section 3) and the form that reacts with proteins (see the nursing box on HbA1c).

3. Reactions

  • Reducing sugars have a free hemiacetal (an anomeric OH) that can open to an aldehyde. They give a positive Benedict's test. All monosaccharides are reducing sugars, and so are most disaccharides except sucrose.
  • Oxidation of the aldehyde end gives a sugar acid (glucose → gluconic acid).
  • Reduction of the C=O gives a sugar alcohol (glucose → sorbitol, xylose → xylitol).

Here is why the word "reducing" is used. A sugar whose ring can open exposes an aldehyde, and aldehydes are easily oxidized to carboxylic acids (Chapter 5). When the sugar is oxidized, something else must be reduced: in Benedict's reagent, blue Cu2+ is reduced to brick-red Cu2O; in Tollens' reagent, Ag+ is reduced to a silver mirror. The sugar is the reducing agent, so we call it a reducing sugar. Ketoses count too: in the basic test solution, fructose rearranges to an aldose and then reacts. A sugar whose anomeric carbon is locked in an acetal (a glycosidic bond, Section 7) cannot open, so it is non-reducing.

SugarFree anomeric OH (hemiacetal)?Benedict's test
Glucose, galactose, fructose, any monosaccharideYesPositive (reducing)
Maltose, lactoseYes (on the right-hand glucose unit)Positive (reducing)
SucroseNo; both anomeric carbons are in the glycosidic bondNegative (non-reducing)
Sorbitol, xylitol (sugar alcohols)No C=O at allNegative
Starch, glycogen, celluloseOnly one free end on thousands of unitsEssentially negative

Reduction to sugar alcohols. Reducing the C=O of a sugar (hydrogen gas with a metal catalyst, or an enzyme in the body) turns the aldehyde or ketone into one more CH2OH or CHOH group. The product is a sugar alcohol (alditol): glucose → sorbitol (also called glucitol), mannose → mannitol, xylose → xylitol, galactose → galactitol. Sugar alcohols taste sweet, keep the OH groups and the water solubility of sugars, but they have no carbonyl to open and close: no ring, no mutarotation, no positive Benedict's test. 4. Disaccharides and polysaccharides

A disaccharide is two monosaccharides joined by a glycosidic bond, an acetal that links the anomeric carbon of one sugar to an OH of the other. You name the bond by the anomer (α or β) and the two carbon numbers it connects. The table collects everything you need for the three dietary disaccharides.

Professor Tip

All four polysaccharides we cover are made of glucose. What makes them different is the α vs. β linkage and the branching, and that is exactly what decides whether we can digest them.

5. Epimers and functional group isomers

All the hexoses share the formula C6H12O6, so they are isomers of one another. Sorting out which kind of isomer uses the vocabulary from Chapters 2 and 5. Work from the outside in: same connectivity or not? If the same, mirror images or not?

RelationshipTestSugar example
Functional group isomers (a type of constitutional isomer)Same formula, atoms connected differently; different functional groupD-glucose (an aldehyde) and D-fructose (a ketone)
EnantiomersSame connectivity; non-superimposable mirror images; every chiral center is oppositeD-glucose and L-glucose
DiastereomersSame connectivity; stereoisomers that are not mirror images; some but not all centers oppositeD-glucose and D-galactose; D-glucose and D-mannose; also α- and β-D-glucose (anomers)
Epimers (a special diastereomer)Differ at exactly one chiral carbonD-glucose and D-galactose (C4 epimers); D-glucose and D-mannose (C2 epimers)
  • Epimers are diastereomers that differ at only one chiral carbon: D-glucose and D-galactose (C4), and D-glucose and D-mannose (C2).
  • Glucose (an aldose) and fructose (a ketose) are both C6H12O6: they are functional group isomers.
  • Galactose and mannose are both epimers of glucose, but they are not epimers of each other: they differ at two carbons (C2 and C4), so they are simply diastereomers.

6. The three kinds of sugar acids

An aldose has two carbons that can be oxidized to a carboxylic acid: the aldehyde at C1 (easy, as for any aldehyde) and the primary alcohol at C6 (harder; needs a strong oxidant or, in the body, an enzyme). Which end gets oxidized decides the name.

TypeCarbon(s) oxidized to COOHFrom glucoseHow it forms; why it matters
Aldonic acidC1 (the aldehyde)gluconic acidMild oxidation: Benedict's or Tollens' reagent, or glucose oxidase in test strips. This is the reaction behind "reducing sugar."
Aldaric acidC1 and the last carbon (C6)glucaric acidStrong oxidation (for example, warm nitric acid); both ends become COOH.
Uronic (alduronic) acidOnly the last carbon (C6); C1 stays an aldehydeglucuronic acidMade by liver enzymes with C1 protected in a ring. Attached to drugs and bilirubin (glucuronidation) to make them water soluble for excretion. Still a reducing sugar because C1 is untouched.

7. Building and breaking glycosidic bonds

A disaccharide forms by condensation: the anomeric OH of one sugar (a hemiacetal) reacts with an OH of another sugar to form an acetal, the glycosidic bond, and releases H2O. Hydrolysis (by acid or by enzymes such as maltase, lactase, and amylase) adds water back and breaks the bond.

You saw both reactions in Chapter 5: hemiacetal + alcohol → acetal + water, and acetal + water (acid catalyst) → hemiacetal + alcohol. Here the "alcohol" is one of the OH groups of a second sugar. Two facts follow from acetal chemistry:

  • A glycosidic bond is stable in base and in neutral water, but it is hydrolyzed by aqueous acid or by a specific enzyme. That is why sucrose in a sealed bottle does not fall apart, but sucrose heated with dilute acid in the lab, or met by sucrase in the small intestine, does.
  • Enzymes are shape-specific. Amylase, maltase, and sucrase hydrolyze α links; lactase hydrolyzes the β(1→4) link of lactose; no human enzyme hydrolyzes the β(1→4) links of cellulose.

Mass is conserved, so you can always balance the equation: two hexoses (2 × C6H12O6) condense to one disaccharide (C12H22O11) plus one H2O, and hydrolysis runs the same equation in reverse. A polysaccharide of n glucose units has lost (n − 1) waters: (C6H10O5)n.

8. Physical properties of monosaccharides

Everything about how a sugar behaves physically comes from its many OH groups and hydrogen bonding, the same reasoning you used for alcohols in Chapter 4.

  • Very water soluble. Five or six OH groups on a six-carbon molecule hydrogen bond strongly with water. Glucose dissolves at roughly 900 g per liter of water at room temperature; sucrose dissolves even more. Sugars are insoluble in nonpolar solvents such as hexane.
  • Crystalline solids with high melting points for molecules of their size (glucose about 146 °C, sucrose about 186 °C) because every OH group hydrogen bonds to its neighbors in the crystal. Compare hexane, a liquid that boils at 69 °C.
  • They decompose instead of boiling. Heat a sugar past its melting point and it darkens and caramelizes (the OH groups and C=O react with each other) rather than distilling. Sugars have no boiling point.
  • Sweet taste. Relative to sucrose (defined as 100), fructose is about 170, glucose about 75, maltose about 30, lactose about 15. Sugar alcohols are sweet too (xylitol about 100, sorbitol about 60), which is why they replace sugar in "sugar-free" products.
  • Optically active. Every monosaccharide is chiral, so solutions rotate plane-polarized light; mutarotation is the slow change in that rotation as anomers reach equilibrium.

9. Glucose in the blood: the numbers nurses use

Blood glucose is reported in the United States in milligrams per deciliter (mg/dL); 1 dL = 100 mL. A normal fasting level is about 70–99 mg/dL. Below about 70 mg/dL is hypoglycemia (low blood sugar); a fasting value of 126 mg/dL or higher on repeated tests is one of the criteria for diabetes mellitus, and a persistently high level is hyperglycemia. (Many other countries use mmol/L; 18 mg/dL = 1 mmol/L, so 90 mg/dL is 5.0 mmol/L. Check the unit before you interpret a value.) Glucose meters and test strips use the enzyme glucose oxidase, which oxidizes C1 of glucose to gluconic acid, the aldonic acid from Section 6; the strip measures the electrons released. It is reducing-sugar chemistry made specific for glucose.

Health Career Connection

  • Dental hygiene – caries: Oral bacteria such as Streptococcus mutans ferment sucrose and other sugars into lactic acid. When plaque pH falls below about 5.5, enamel starts to demineralize. Bacteria also use sucrose to build sticky glucose polymers that help plaque attach to teeth.
  • Dental hygiene – xylitol: Xylitol is a sugar alcohol (reduced xylose). It tastes sweet, but cariogenic bacteria cannot ferment it to acid, so it is used in sugar-free gums and mints.
  • Nursing – IV fluids and glucose testing: "Dextrose" in D5W IV fluid is D-glucose. Glucose test strips use enzyme-based oxidation of glucose, an updated version of the reducing-sugar idea.
  • Nursing – lactose intolerance and galactosemia: Without the enzyme lactase, lactose is not hydrolyzed and is fermented by gut bacteria. In galactosemia, an enzyme for using galactose is missing, so infants must avoid lactose.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

D5W is 5% dextrose in water: 5 g of D-glucose per 100 mL (about 170 kcal/L). HbA1c (glycated hemoglobin) is reducing-sugar chemistry inside a red blood cell: the open-chain fraction of glucose has a free aldehyde, which reacts with an amine group of hemoglobin (the N-terminal valine of the β chain) and stays attached for the life of the cell, about 120 days. Higher average glucose means a larger glycated fraction, so HbA1c (normal below 5.7%; 6.5% or higher supports a diagnosis of diabetes) reports the average over two to three months, not today's value. Above the kidney's threshold (about 180 mg/dL) glucose spills into the urine (glucosuria). Dipsticks use glucose oxidase and detect only glucose; the older copper-reduction tablet test reacts with any reducing sugar, which is why it was used to screen infants' urine for galactose (galactosemia). The liver attaches glucuronic acid (the alduronic acid of glucose) to bilirubin and to drugs such as acetaminophen to make them water soluble (glucuronidation); newborns do this slowly, one cause of neonatal jaundice. Two sugar alcohols are drugs: IV mannitol pulls water from tissue by osmosis (an osmotic diuretic); oral sorbitol holds water in the gut (an osmotic laxative). Heparin, the IV anticoagulant, is a sulfated polysaccharide of uronic acids and amino sugars, too large and charged to be absorbed from the gut.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Dental caries (tooth decay) is carbohydrate chemistry gone wrong. Streptococcus mutans and other plaque bacteria ferment sucrose, glucose, and fructose to lactic acid. Within minutes of a sugary snack, plaque pH falls below the critical pH of about 5.5 and hydroxyapatite in enamel begins to demineralize; saliva's bicarbonate buffer needs roughly 30 to 60 minutes to restore the pH so remineralization can resume (the Stephan curve). Frequent sipping keeps the pH low. Sucrose is doubly bad: bacterial enzymes also polymerize its glucose into sticky glucans, the scaffold of plaque biofilm. Starch that sticks to teeth is hydrolyzed by salivary amylase to maltose and glucose that bacteria can use. Xylitol and sorbitol, the sugar alcohols in sugar-free gum, taste sweet but have no carbonyl and cannot be fermented to acid; xylitol also enters S. mutans and inhibits its growth, and chewing raises salivary flow. Sucrose-sweetened liquid pediatric medicines and bottles of juice or formula at bedtime cause early childhood caries ("baby-bottle caries"). Two polysaccharides are tools of the trade: alginate impression material, a polymer of uronic acids from brown algae that gels with calcium ions, and carboxymethylcellulose, which thickens toothpaste and artificial saliva for patients with xerostomia (dry mouth).

  • Iodine test: amylose (starch) traps iodine in its helix and turns blue-black.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Classify and count chiral carbons

Problem. D-ribose is an open-chain five-carbon sugar with a CHO at C1. Classify it and count its chiral carbons.

Solution

Aldehyde + 5 carbons = aldopentose. C2, C3, and C4 each have four different groups, so there are 3 chiral carbons. C1 (CHO) and C5 (CH2OH) are not chiral.

Check. An aldose with n carbons has n − 2 chiral carbons in its open chain: 5 − 2 = 3.

Example 2: α or β?

Problem. In a Haworth drawing of D-glucose, the CH2OH points up and the OH on C1 points down. Which anomer is it?

Solution

Anomeric OH opposite the CH2OH (down) = α-D-glucose.

Check. For a D sugar the CH2OH is up, so "OH down" and "opposite the CH2OH" say the same thing: α.

Example 3: Reducing sugar?

Problem. Which give a positive Benedict's test: glucose, fructose, maltose, sucrose?

Solution

Glucose, fructose, and maltose: positive. (Fructose is a ketose, but under the basic test conditions it rearranges to an aldose, so it still reacts.) Sucrose: negative. Its α1–β2 link ties up both anomeric carbons, so no ring can open.

Check. One question decides it: is there a free hemiacetal (anomeric OH)? Yes for the three monosaccharides and the right-hand unit of maltose; no for sucrose.

Example 4: Hydrolysis

Problem. What monosaccharides form when sucrase hydrolyzes sucrose?

Solution

Sucrose + H2O → D-glucose + D-fructose (the mixture is "invert sugar").

Check. C12H22O11 + H2O gives 2 C6H12O6: one hexose from each side of the acetal link.

Example 5: Read a Fischer projection

Problem. A sugar is drawn as a Fischer projection: CHO at the top, then four chiral carbons with OH on the left (C2), right (C3), right (C4), and left (C5), and CH2OH at the bottom. Classify it, assign D or L, and decide how it is related to D-glucose.

Solution. CHO at C1 makes it an aldose; six carbons make it an aldohexose. The chiral carbon farthest from the C=O is C5, and its OH is on the left, so the sugar is L. Compare it with D-glucose (OH right, left, right, right): this sugar is left, right, right, left, which is the opposite at every one of the four centers. Opposite at every center means mirror images: it is L-glucose, the enantiomer of D-glucose.

Check. Four chiral carbons were compared and all four differ. If only one had differed, the answer would have been "epimer"; if two or three, "diastereomer." The L assignment agrees, because an enantiomer of a D sugar must be L.

Example 6: Fischer to Haworth

Problem. Draw β-D-galactose as a Haworth projection, starting from its Fischer projection (OH on C2 right, C3 left, C4 left, C5 right).

Solution. Draw the pyranose ring with the oxygen at the back right and C1 at the right; number clockwise. Apply "right → down, left → up": C2 OH down, C3 OH up, C4 OH up. The sugar is D, so the C6 CH2OH on C5 points up. The anomer is β, so the new OH on C1 points up, on the same side as the CH2OH. Fill each remaining position with H.

Check. The drawing should look exactly like the β-D-glucose drawing in Section 2 except at C4, where the OH is up instead of down, because galactose is the C4 epimer of glucose. One flipped position, one epimer: consistent.

Example 7: Condensation and hydrolysis of a glycosidic bond

Problem. (a) Two D-glucose molecules condense to form maltose. Write the balanced equation and name the type of functional group created. (b) Starch is boiled with dilute acid until it tests negative with iodine and positive with Benedict's reagent. Explain both results.

Solution. (a) The anomeric (C1) OH of α-D-glucose, a hemiacetal, condenses with the C4 OH of a second glucose: 2 C6H12O6 → C12H22O11 + H2O. The new C1–O–C4 link is an acetal, called an α(1→4) glycosidic bond. (b) Acid hydrolyzes acetals. The long α(1→4) chains of amylose are cut into maltose and finally glucose, so the helix that traps iodine is gone (negative iodine test), and the free hemiacetal of every glucose molecule can open to an aldehyde (positive Benedict's test).

Check. Atoms balance: 12 C, 24 H, 12 O on each side of (a). In (b), intact starch would have given the opposite pair of results (iodine positive, Benedict's essentially negative), so the change in both tests confirms hydrolysis.

Example 8: Predict the oxidation or reduction product

Problem. Name the product and its class when D-galactose is (a) treated with Benedict's reagent, (b) oxidized only at C6 by an enzyme, (c) oxidized at both C1 and C6, and (d) reduced with hydrogen and a catalyst.

Solution. Use the glucose pattern and change the stem to "galact-": (a) C1 aldehyde → COOH gives galactonic acid, an aldonic acid (Cu2+ is reduced to Cu2O). (b) C6 CH2OH → COOH with C1 untouched gives galacturonic acid, an alduronic (uronic) acid. (c) Both ends oxidized gives galactaric acid, an aldaric acid. (d) C1 CHO → CH2OH gives galactitol, a sugar alcohol.

Check. The suffixes tell you the carbons: -onic (C1), -uronic (C6), -aric (both), -itol (reduced). Only (b) and the starting sugar still have an aldehyde, so only they would still be reducing sugars.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Deciding D/L from C2. Use the chiral carbon farthest from the C=O (C5 in glucose).
  • Mixing up α and β. Compare the anomeric OH with the CH2OH: opposite = α, same side = β.
  • Calling sucrose a reducing sugar.
  • Thinking cellulose is made of a different sugar than starch. Both are glucose; only the linkage differs.
  • Calling α- and β-D-glucose enantiomers. They differ at one carbon (C1) only, so they are diastereomers (anomers). Enantiomers differ at every chiral carbon (D-glucose vs. L-glucose).
  • Calling galactose and mannose epimers of each other. Each is an epimer of glucose, but they differ from each other at two carbons.
  • Thinking a sugar alcohol such as sorbitol can form a ring or give a positive Benedict's test. With no C=O there is no hemiacetal and nothing to oxidize.
  • Forgetting the water. Condensation of two hexoses releases one H2O (C12H22O11, not C12H24O12); hydrolysis consumes it.

What sugar alcohol forms when D-glucose is reduced? Is it a reducing sugar?

D-sorbitol (glucitol). No. It has no C=O or hemiacetal left to oxidize.

Glycogen

Lactose is hydrolyzed by lactase. Name the products and the linkage that was broken.

Answer
D-galactose and D-glucose; a β(1→4) glycosidic bond

Exercise 5

D-Glucose and D-mannose differ only in the arrangement at C2. What term describes this pair?

Answer
Epimers (a special kind of diastereomer)

Exercise 6

The liver attaches glucuronic acid to drugs and bilirubin to make them water soluble for excretion. Which carbon of glucose was oxidized to make it?

Answer
C6 (the CH2OH becomes COOH); it is a uronic acid.

Exercise 7

A patient's fasting blood glucose is reported as 6.0 mmol/L. Convert it to mg/dL and state whether it is in the normal fasting range (70–99 mg/dL).

Answer
6.0 mmol/L × 18 mg/dL per mmol/L = 108 mg/dL. That is above the normal fasting range (but below the 126 mg/dL diabetes criterion).

Exercise 8

Pure β-D-glucose is dissolved in water. After an hour, what fraction of the molecules is still β, and what process explains the change?

Answer
About 64% β (36% α, a trace of open chain). Mutarotation: the ring opens to the aldehyde and re-closes with the C1 OH up or down until equilibrium is reached.
