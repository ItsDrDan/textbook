Previously, you learned that monosaccharides can form cyclic structures by the reaction of the carbonyl group with an OH group. These cyclic molecules can in turn react with another alcohol. Disaccharides (C12H22O11) are sugars composed of two monosaccharide units that are joined by a carbon–oxygen–carbon linkage known as a glycosidic linkage. This linkage is formed from the reaction of the anomeric carbon of one cyclic monosaccharide with the OH group of a second monosaccharide.

The disaccharides differ from one another in their monosaccharide constituents and in the specific type of glycosidic linkage connecting them. There are three common disaccharides: maltose, lactose, and sucrose. All three are white crystalline solids at room temperature and are soluble in water. We’ll consider each sugar in more detail.

Maltose

Maltose occurs to a limited extent in sprouting grain. It is formed most often by the partial hydrolysis of starch and glycogen. In the manufacture of beer, maltose is liberated by the action of malt (germinating barley) on starch; for this reason, it is often referred to as malt sugar. Maltose is about 30% as sweet as sucrose. The human body is unable to metabolize maltose or any other disaccharide directly from the diet because the molecules are too large to pass through the cell membranes of the intestinal wall. Therefore, an ingested disaccharide must first be broken down by hydrolysis into its two constituent monosaccharide units.

In the body, such hydrolysis reactions are catalyzed by enzymes such as maltase. The same reactions can be carried out in the laboratory with dilute acid as a catalyst, although in that case the rate is much slower, and high temperatures are required. Whether it occurs in the body or a glass beaker, the hydrolysis of maltose produces two molecules of D-glucose.

\[\mathrm{maltose \xrightarrow{H^+\: or\: maltase} \textrm{2 D-glucose}} \nonumber \]

Maltose is a reducing sugar. Thus, its two glucose molecules must be linked in such a way as to leave one anomeric carbon that can open to form an aldehyde group. The glucose units in maltose are joined in a head-to-tail fashion through an α-linkage from the first carbon atom of one glucose molecule to the fourth carbon atom of the second glucose molecule (that is, an α-1,4-glycosidic linkage, also written α(1→4); see Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\)). The bond from the anomeric carbon of the first monosaccharide unit is directed downward, which is why this is known as an α-glycosidic linkage. The OH group on the anomeric carbon of the second glucose can be in either the α or the β position, as shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\).

Lactose

Lactose is known as milk sugar because it occurs in the milk of humans, cows, and other mammals. In fact, the natural synthesis of lactose occurs only in mammary tissue, whereas most other carbohydrates are plant products. Human milk contains about 7.5% lactose, and cow’s milk contains about 4.5%. This sugar is one of the lowest ranking in terms of sweetness, being about one-sixth as sweet as sucrose. Lactose is produced commercially from whey, a by-product in the manufacture of cheese. It is important as an infant food and in the production of penicillin.

Lactose is a reducing sugar composed of one molecule of D-galactose and one molecule of D-glucose joined by a β-1,4-glycosidic bond (the bond from the anomeric carbon of the first monosaccharide unit being directed upward). The two monosaccharides are obtained from lactose by acid hydrolysis or the catalytic action of the enzyme lactase:

Many adults and some children suffer from a deficiency of lactase. These individuals are said to be lactose intolerant because they cannot digest the lactose found in milk. A more serious problem is the genetic disease galactosemia, which results from the absence of an enzyme needed to convert galactose to glucose. Certain bacteria can metabolize lactose, forming lactic acid as one of the products. This reaction is responsible for the “souring” of milk.

To Your Health: Lactose Intolerance and Galactosemia

Lactose makes up about 40% of an infant’s diet during the first year of life. Infants and small children have one form of the enzyme lactase in their small intestines and can digest the sugar easily; however, in many people the small intestine makes much less lactase after early childhood (see the Valley Connection below for how common this is). As a result, many adults experience a reduction in the ability to hydrolyze lactose to galactose and glucose in their small intestine. For some people the inability to synthesize sufficient enzyme increases with age.

In people with lactose intolerance, some of the unhydrolyzed lactose passes into the colon, where it tends to draw water from the interstitial fluid into the intestinal lumen by osmosis. At the same time, intestinal bacteria may act on the lactose to produce organic acids and gases. The buildup of water and bacterial decay products leads to abdominal distention, cramps, and diarrhea, which are symptoms of the condition.

The symptoms disappear if milk or other sources of lactose are excluded from the diet or consumed only sparingly. Alternatively, many food stores now carry special brands of milk that have been pretreated with lactase to hydrolyze the lactose. Cooking or fermenting milk causes at least partial hydrolysis of the lactose, so some people with lactose intolerance are still able to enjoy cheese, yogurt, or cooked foods containing milk. The most common treatment for lactose intolerance, however, is the use of lactase preparations (e.g., Lactaid), which are available in liquid and tablet form at drugstores and grocery stores. These are taken orally with dairy foods—or may be added to them directly—to assist in their digestion.

Galactosemia is a condition in which one of the enzymes needed to convert galactose to glucose is missing. Consequently, the blood galactose level is markedly elevated, and galactose is found in the urine. An infant with galactosemia experiences a lack of appetite, weight loss, diarrhea, and jaundice. The disease may result in impaired liver function, cataracts, intellectual disability, and even death. If galactosemia is recognized in early infancy (U.S. newborns are screened for it), its life-threatening effects can be prevented by removing milk and all other sources of galactose from the diet. This galactose-restricted diet is continued for life. The incidence of galactosemia in the United States is 1 in every 65,000 newborn babies.

Sucrose

Sucrose, probably the largest-selling pure organic compound in the world, is known as beet sugar, cane sugar, table sugar, or simply sugar. Most of the sucrose sold commercially is obtained from sugar cane and sugar beets (whose juices are 14%–20% sucrose) by evaporation of the water and recrystallization. The dark brown liquid that remains after the recrystallization of sugar is sold as molasses.

The sucrose molecule is unique among the common disaccharides in having an α-1,β-2-glycosidic (head-to-head) linkage. Because this glycosidic linkage is formed by the OH group on the anomeric carbon of α-D-glucose and the OH group on the anomeric carbon of β-D-fructose, it ties up the anomeric carbons of both glucose and fructose.

This linkage gives sucrose certain properties that are quite different from those of maltose and lactose. As long as the sucrose molecule remains intact, neither monosaccharide “uncyclizes” to form an open-chain structure. Thus, sucrose is incapable of mutarotation and exists in only one form both in the solid state and in solution. In addition, sucrose does not undergo reactions that are typical of aldehydes and ketones. Therefore, sucrose is a nonreducing sugar.

The hydrolysis of sucrose in dilute acid or through the action of the enzyme sucrase (also known as invertase) gives an equimolar mixture of glucose and fructose. This 1:1 mixture is referred to as invert sugar because it rotates plane-polarized light in the opposite direction from sucrose. The hydrolysis reaction has several practical applications. Sucrose readily recrystallizes from a solution, but invert sugar has a much greater tendency to remain in solution. In the manufacture of jelly and candy and in the canning of fruit, the recrystallization of sugar is undesirable. Therefore, conditions leading to the hydrolysis of sucrose are employed in these processes. Moreover, because fructose is sweeter than sucrose, the hydrolysis adds to the sweetening effect. Bees carry out this reaction when they make honey.

In the United States, about 68 lb of refined cane and beet sugar (sucrose) per person was available for consumption in 2023, down from about 102 lb in 1972 (USDA Economic Research Service). The widespread use of sucrose is a contributing factor to obesity and tooth decay. Carbohydrates such as sucrose are converted to fat when the caloric intake exceeds the body’s requirements, and sucrose causes tooth decay by promoting the formation of plaque that sticks to teeth.

Valley Connection: Milk Sugar in America’s Top Dairy County

Drive south from Fresno on Highway 99 and you will pass dairy after dairy. In the 2022 Census of Agriculture, Tulare County ranked first of all U.S. counties in sales of milk from cows, about $2.8 billion.2 Dairy is also California’s most valuable farm commodity ($8.6 billion in 2024), and the state leads the nation in dairy production.3

The chemistry. The main sugar in all that milk is lactose, a disaccharide made of galactose and glucose joined by a β-1,4 glycosidic bond. Your body can’t absorb a disaccharide directly. The small intestine makes the enzyme lactase, which breaks down lactose1 by hydrolysis: water is added across the glycosidic bond, releasing the two monosaccharides.

Lactose intolerance. In many people the small intestine makes less lactase after infancy, a common condition called lactase nonpersistence. Undigested lactose then travels to the colon, where bacteria break it down and produce gas and fluid, causing symptoms within a few hours.1 NIH estimates that about 68% of people worldwide and about 36% of people in the U.S. have trouble digesting lactose. It is more common among African American, American Indian, Asian American, and Hispanic/Latino people and least common among people of European ancestry.1 So in a region famous for milk, many local families may not digest it well.

In the dental office. Another disaccharide, sucrose (glucose + fructose), feeds Streptococcus mutans, considered the major bacterial cause of tooth decay.5 Its glucosyltransferase enzymes split sucrose—their only substrate—and then link the glucose units with new glycosidic bonds into long glucan polymers that help build sticky plaque biofilm.5 Plaque bacteria turn sugars into acids that make tooth enamel lose minerals, the first step toward a cavity.4 Sticky foods such as dried fruit stay on teeth longer, so the ADA suggests eating sweets with meals, when saliva flow is higher, and rinsing with water afterward.6 Future dental hygienists will give this advice every day.

Think about it: Lactose and sucrose are both disaccharides. Which bond does lactase hydrolyze, and why can’t lactase digest sucrose?

Answer
Lactase hydrolyzes the β-1,4 glycosidic bond between galactose and glucose in lactose. Enzymes are highly specific: the active site fits the shape of its substrate. Sucrose is built from different monosaccharides (glucose and fructose) joined by a different glycosidic linkage (α-1,β-2), so it does not fit lactase’s active site. A different enzyme, sucrase, breaks down sucrose.

Summary

Maltose is composed of two molecules of glucose joined by an α-1,4-glycosidic linkage. It is a reducing sugar that is found in sprouting grain. Lactose is composed of a molecule of galactose joined to a molecule of glucose by a β-1,4-glycosidic linkage. It is a reducing sugar that is found in milk. Sucrose is composed of a molecule of glucose joined to a molecule of fructose by an α-1,β-2-glycosidic linkage. It is a nonreducing sugar that is found in sugar cane and sugar beets.

Molecule in the Clinic: Lactulose

Lactulose is a synthetic disaccharide: galactose joined by a β(1→4) glycosidic bond to fructose. Humans have no enzyme that hydrolyzes it, so it reaches the colon intact. There, bacteria ferment it into acids that pull water into the colon (a laxative effect) and trap ammonia as ammonium ion. That second effect is why lactulose is given to patients with liver failure and high blood ammonia.

Your turn: Lactose is galactose β(1→4) glucose. How does lactulose differ, and why does it not raise blood glucose?

Answer
Lactulose has fructose where lactose has glucose. Because no human enzyme can hydrolyze it, it is never broken into absorbable monosaccharides, so nothing enters the blood.

Your turn: Is lactulose a reducing sugar?

Answer
Yes. The galactose anomeric carbon is tied up in the glycosidic bond, but the anomeric carbon of the fructose unit is free, so the ring can open.

Lecture Connection

In lecture a disaccharide is "an acetal between two sugars": one monosaccharide acts as the hemiacetal and the other as the alcohol, water is lost (condensation), and the new C–O–C link is the glycosidic bond. The single most-tested idea in the chapter is why maltose and lactose are reducing sugars and sucrose is not.

Naming the glycosidic bond

You name the bond by the anomer (α or β: an α bond points trans to the CH2OH, a β bond cis) and the two carbon numbers it connects, written α(1→4) or, as on the slides, α-1,4. A disaccharide whose second unit still has a free hemiacetal is named by that anomer too (α-maltose, β-maltose), and any disaccharide with a hemiacetal is a reducing sugar. Building and breaking glycosidic bonds

A disaccharide forms by condensation: the anomeric OH of one sugar (a hemiacetal) reacts with an OH of another sugar to form an acetal, the glycosidic bond, and releases H2O. Hydrolysis (by acid or by enzymes such as maltase, lactase, and amylase) adds water back and cleaves the C–O glycosidic bond, re-forming the two monosaccharides. Remember the two definitions you will use for the rest of the course: condensation, two molecules join and a small molecule (H2O) is lost; hydrolysis, water splits a molecule. When you are asked where the bonds break and form: in condensation the new bond is C1(anomeric)–O–C4 and the hemiacetal becomes an acetal; in hydrolysis that same C–O bond breaks, the acetal goes back to a hemiacetal, and the other sugar gets its alcohol OH back. The glycosidic link looks like an ether (C–O–C), but because one of those carbons also carries the ring oxygen it is an acetal, which is what makes it hydrolyzable.

You saw both reactions in Chapter 5: hemiacetal + alcohol → acetal + water, and acetal + water (acid catalyst) → hemiacetal + alcohol. Here the "alcohol" is one of the OH groups of a second sugar. Two facts follow from acetal chemistry:

  • A glycosidic bond is stable in base and in neutral water, but it is hydrolyzed by aqueous acid or by a specific enzyme. That is why sucrose in a sealed bottle does not fall apart, but sucrose heated with dilute acid in the lab, or met by sucrase in the small intestine, does.
  • Enzymes are shape-specific. Amylase, maltase, and sucrase hydrolyze α links; lactase hydrolyzes the β(1→4) link of lactose; no human enzyme hydrolyzes the β(1→4) links of cellulose.

Mass is conserved, so you can always balance the equation: two hexoses (2 × C6H12O6) condense to one disaccharide (C12H22O11) plus one H2O, and hydrolysis runs the same equation in reverse. A polysaccharide of n glucose units has lost (n − 1) waters: (C6H10O5)n.

Example 1: Read a disaccharide drawing

Problem. For Disaccharide W below: (a) identify the two monosaccharides; (b) name the glycosidic linkage (α or β, and the carbon numbers); (c) locate the anomeric carbons and state whether W is a reducing sugar; (d) write the products of hydrolysis.

Solution. (a) Read each ring back to a Fischer pattern (down = right, up = left). Left ring: C2 down, C3 up, C4 down (it is bonded to the bridging O through C1, and its C4 OH is down): right, left, right, and the CH2OH is up, so it is D-glucose. Right ring: the same pattern at C2 and C3, CH2OH up, so it is also D-glucose. (b) The bridge runs from C1 of the left ring to the oxygen on C4 of the right ring. The bond leaving C1 points up, cis to the CH2OH, so the link is β(1→4). (c) C1 of the left ring is an acetal (bonded to two oxygens, no OH): locked. C1 of the right ring still carries an OH and the ring O: a free hemiacetal, so the ring can open to an aldehyde and W is a reducing sugar (it is β-cellobiose, the repeating unit of cellulose). (d) W + H2O → 2 D-glucose, by acid or by a β-glucosidase; human maltase cannot do it.

Check. Compare with maltose (figure above): same two sugars, same carbons joined; the only difference is that the bond at C1 points down (α) in maltose and up (β) here. That one change is the difference between starch and cellulose.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Lactose intolerance is the loss of lactase, from a genetic difference, the normal decline with age, or injury to the intestinal lining. Undigested lactose cannot be absorbed; gut bacteria ferment it, causing cramps and diarrhea, so patients use lactose-free milk or lactase drops. Galactosemia is the more serious problem: an enzyme for using galactose is missing, galactose builds up, and infants must be fed a lactose-free formula, because every lactose molecule hydrolyzed in the gut releases a galactose. Both conditions are, chemically, a missing hydrolysis step.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Dental caries (tooth decay) is carbohydrate chemistry gone wrong. Streptococcus mutans and other plaque bacteria ferment sucrose, glucose, and fructose to lactic acid. Within minutes of a sugary snack, plaque pH falls below the critical pH of about 5.5 and hydroxyapatite in enamel begins to demineralize; saliva's bicarbonate buffer needs roughly 30 to 60 minutes to restore the pH so remineralization can resume (the Stephan curve). Frequent sipping keeps the pH low. Sucrose is doubly bad: bacterial enzymes also polymerize its glucose into sticky glucans, the scaffold of plaque biofilm, which is how plaque attaches to teeth. Starch that sticks to teeth is hydrolyzed by salivary amylase to maltose and glucose that bacteria can use. Sucrose-sweetened liquid pediatric medicines and bottles of juice or formula at bedtime cause early childhood caries ("baby-bottle caries").
