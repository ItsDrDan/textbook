Learning Objectives

  • Define catabolic and anabolic reactions, name the three stages of catabolism, and distinguish a linear from a cyclic pathway.
  • Define oxidation and reduction in terms of loss or gain of electrons, oxygen, or hydrogen, and identify each coenzyme (NAD+/NADH, FAD/FADH2, coenzyme A, ATP/ADP, NADPH) as an oxidizing agent, reducing agent, acyl carrier, or phosphate carrier; name the B vitamin each is built from.
  • Rank ATP, ADP, and AMP by energy, tell whether ATP → ADP releases or absorbs energy, and explain why it is coupled to other reactions.
  • Name the enzymes of carbohydrate digestion in order and state where each works.
  • Know the initial reactant and final product of glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, glycogenesis, glycogenolysis, and the pentose phosphate pathway.
  • Given the reactants and products of any step (in words or as structures), answer the four questions: What type of reaction is this? How can I tell? What class of enzyme is needed? What coenzyme (or ATP) is needed?
  • Predict the fate of pyruvate under aerobic and anaerobic conditions.
  • Describe how insulin and glucagon control glycogen synthesis and breakdown, and what activates or inhibits the control enzymes of glycolysis.

Key Ideas

1. The big picture

Metabolism is the sum of all the chemical reactions that take place in an organism; its job is to provide energy for the body. Sorting those reactions into two piles makes the whole chapter easier:

  • Catabolism is the breakdown of large molecules into smaller ones. Energy is generally released (exothermic: energy is a product). The electrons removed from the fuel are caught by NAD+ and FAD, and some of the energy is stored as ATP.
  • Anabolism is the synthesis of large molecules from smaller ones. Energy is generally absorbed (endothermic: energy is a reactant), usually as ATP, plus reducing power (NADPH).

A series of consecutive reactions is a metabolic pathway. A linear pathway (glycolysis) ends in a product different from any reactant; a cyclic pathway (the citric acid cycle, Chapter 14) regenerates its first reactant. Metabolic reactions run at body temperature and physiological pH, so each one needs an enzyme and often a coenzyme.

Catabolism of food happens in three stages. You will spend this chapter in Stages 1 and 2 for carbohydrates and Chapter 14 in Stage 3.

  • Stage 1, digestion and hydrolysis: large molecules are broken down to smaller ones that enter the bloodstream (polysaccharides → monosaccharides; fats → fatty acids + glycerol; proteins → amino acids). No useful energy is captured.
  • Stage 2, degradation: the small molecules are broken down to two- and three-carbon compounds. For glucose this is glycolysis (to three-carbon pyruvate) plus pyruvate oxidation to two-carbon acetyl CoA, the common product of carbohydrate, fat, and protein breakdown. A little ATP and NADH are made.
  • Stage 3, oxidation: the citric acid cycle oxidizes acetyl CoA to CO2, loading NADH and FADH2, and electron transport uses O2 to turn those carriers into most of the cell's ATP (Chapter 14).

Where it happens matters, and it is the only biology you need: the cytosol (the fluid inside the cell) is the site of glycolysis, glycogen metabolism, and the pentose phosphate pathway; the mitochondrion is where pyruvate becomes acetyl CoA and where Stage 3 runs; ribosomes are where proteins are made (Chapter 12).

Lactose-free milk is simply milk pre-treated with lactase.

2. The coenzymes: know their jobs

Many metabolic reactions are redox reactions, so start with the definitions you will use all chapter:

  • Oxidation is a loss of electrons, a gain of oxygen atoms, or a loss of hydrogen atoms.
  • Reduction is a gain of electrons, a loss of oxygen atoms, or a gain of hydrogen atoms.

Coenzymes carry the hydrogen ions and electrons to or from the substrate. A coenzyme acting as an oxidizing agent makes the substrate get oxidized, so the coenzyme itself is reduced (it gains H+ and e−). A coenzyme acting as a reducing agent is itself oxidized (it loses H+ and e−). Five carriers do most of the work in metabolism. Each carries something specific (electrons, phosphate, or an acyl group), and four of them are built from a B vitamin, which is why vitamin deficiencies show up as energy problems.

NADH and NADPH are chemically almost twins, but the cell keeps them separate: NADH hands electrons to the electron transport chain to make ATP; NADPH hands electrons to biosynthesis. NAD+ and FAD are each a dinucleotide (two nucleotides joined through their phosphates), which is where the “D” in each acronym comes from.

ATP, ADP, and AMP. Adenosine carries one, two, or three phosphates: adenosine monophosphate (AMP), diphosphate (ADP), and triphosphate (ATP). The phosphate–ribose bond is a phosphoester bond; the phosphate–phosphate bonds are phosphoanhydride bonds, and these are the high-energy ones. Rank by energy: ATP > ADP > AMP. Hydrolyzing the last phosphate releases energy, and that release fuels walking, running, and breathing:

ATP + H2O → ADP + Pi + energy (about 7.3 kcal/mol)

Running it backward, phosphorylation of ADP to ATP absorbs and stores energy. Coupled reactions are why ATP → ADP shows up next to so many steps: an energetically unfavorable reaction is paired with a favorable one that releases more energy than the first one needs, so the pair runs together and the overall change is favorable. Step 1 of glycolysis is the classic case: phosphorylating glucose needs energy, hydrolyzing ATP releases more, and hexokinase runs the two as one reaction. A resting adult hydrolyzes and remakes roughly their own body weight in ATP every day, which tells you how fast this currency circulates.

Professor Tip

Use the Latin roots: -lysis = loosening, splitting apart; -neo- = new; -gen- = birth, origin; -esis = action, process; glyco- = glucose or glycogen. So glycolysis is breaking apart glucose, gluconeogenesis is creating new glucose, glycogenesis is the process of making glycogen, and glycogenolysis is breaking apart glycogen. Then, for every step of every pathway, turn your paper sideways and set up a table with four columns: What type of reaction is this? How can I tell? What class of enzyme is needed? What coenzyme is needed? The “how can I tell” column is the chemistry: ATP became ADP (phosphorylation, energy in); same formula in and out (isomerization); NAD+ became NADH and a C=O appeared (oxidation); water left and a C=C appeared (dehydration). The enzyme names give the class away: kinase = transferase, dehydrogenase = oxidoreductase, isomerase or mutase = isomerase, aldolase and enolase = lyase, and in digestion every -ase that uses water is a hydrolase.

3. Glycolysis (cytoplasm, 10 steps)

  • Glycolysis is a 10-step linear pathway that converts glucose (6 carbons) to 2 pyruvate (3 carbons each). It is an oxidation in which no molecular oxygen is used, an anaerobic pathway, and it takes place in the cytosol of every cell.
  • Steps 1–5 require energy: 2 ATP are spent to phosphorylate the sugar twice, then the six-carbon chain is split into two three-carbon pieces.
  • Steps 6–10 generate energy: each three-carbon piece is oxidized once (NAD+ → NADH) and gives up two phosphates to ADP. Because there are two pieces, the payoff counts twice: 4 ATP and 2 NADH.
  • Net: 2 ATP + 2 NADH per glucose. No O2 is needed and no CO2 is released.

Nobody expects you to memorize the intermediates, but if you want them, the lecture mnemonic is “Goodness Gracious, Father Franklin Did Go By Picking Pumpkins (to) Prepare Pies”: Glucose, Glucose-6-phosphate, Fructose-6-phosphate, Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate, Dihydroxyacetone phosphate, Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, 1,3-Bisphosphoglycerate, 3-Phosphoglycerate, 2-Phosphoglycerate, Phosphoenolpyruvate, Pyruvate. What you are expected to do is look at any step and ask: How many carbons are in my molecule? What type of reaction is occurring? Does it take or release energy, and how can I tell? What type of enzyme is needed? Is a coenzyme needed?

Count the carriers: steps 1 and 3 spend one ATP each (2 spent); steps 7 and 10 each make one ATP, twice (4 made); step 6 makes one NADH, twice. Net 2 ATP and 2 NADH. Substrate-level phosphorylation means ATP is produced from ADP by transferring a phosphate from a high-energy compound (the substrate), no oxygen or electron transport chain required. In step 6, the oxidizing agent is NAD+: it takes the hydrogen the aldehyde loses. Only three steps (1, 3, and 10) are irreversible under cell conditions, and all three are kinase steps; they are the ones the cell regulates (Key Idea 7) and the ones gluconeogenesis must bypass (Key Idea 9).

Other hexoses. Digestion also delivers galactose and fructose. Both are converted, in the liver, to intermediates that enter the glycolysis pathway, so all the common hexoses end up as pyruvate (Key Idea 6).

4. Fates of pyruvate

Glycolysis leaves the cell with two problems: pyruvate still holds most of the energy of glucose, and the NADH made in step 6 must be turned back into NAD+ or step 6 stops and glycolysis stalls. There are three pathways for pyruvate, and which one runs depends on the availability of oxygen.

  • Aerobic conditions (oxygen present, cell has mitochondria): three-carbon pyruvate is oxidized and decarboxylated to two-carbon acetyl CoA + CO2 by the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex in the mitochondrion. One carbon leaves as CO2, NAD+ is reduced to NADH, and the acetyl group is attached to coenzyme A as a thioester. (The enzyme is an oxidoreductase that also needs a cofactor made from thiamine, vitamin B1.) Acetyl CoA then enters the citric acid cycle, and the NADH from glycolysis is re-oxidized by the electron transport chain (Chapter 14).
  • Anaerobic conditions in humans (strenuous exercise, when not enough O2 is inhaled to re-oxidize NADH; red blood cells, which have no mitochondria): lactate dehydrogenase (an oxidoreductase) reduces pyruvate to lactate, using NADH + H+ and regenerating NAD+. The ketone C=O of pyruvate becomes the 2° alcohol C–OH of lactate. Net gain is still only 2 ATP per glucose, but it is fast and needs no oxygen. Lactate that builds up in muscle contributes to soreness and cramping; deep breaths replenish O2 so lactate can be re-oxidized to pyruvate, and lactate also travels in the blood to the liver, which rebuilds it into glucose (the Cori cycle, Key Idea 9 and Section 13.10).
  • Anaerobic fermentation in yeast and other microorganisms: pyruvate is decarboxylated to acetaldehyde, and acetaldehyde is reduced to ethanol, regenerating NAD+. The CO2 raises bread and carbonates beer; humans do not have the decarboxylase, so in humans anaerobic conditions lead to lactate, not ethanol.

Keep the NAD+ bookkeeping straight: in a red blood cell, glycolysis makes 2 NADH per glucose and lactate dehydrogenase uses 2 NADH per glucose, so the cell's net products are 2 ATP and 2 lactate, with no NADH left over. In a muscle cell with enough oxygen, the same 2 NADH are instead cashed in for ATP in the mitochondria.

5. Blood glucose control

  • Insulin (released when blood glucose is high, e.g., after a meal): increases glucose uptake and glycogenesis.
  • Glucagon (released when glucose is low; acts on the liver) and epinephrine (released in stress and exercise; acts on liver and muscle) trigger glycogenolysis. The liver also runs gluconeogenesis.
  • Cori cycle: lactate from muscle goes to the liver, is rebuilt into glucose, and is sent back.
  • Glycogen, a branched polymer of glucose with α(1→4) bonds in the chains and α(1→6) bonds at the branches, is stored mainly in liver and muscle. Glycogenesis operates when the first step of glycolysis is making glucose-6-phosphate faster than the cell needs it; once the glycogen stores are full, additional glucose is converted to triacylglycerols and stored as body fat (Chapter 15).
  • The full hormone table, the fed–fasting timeline, stress hyperglycemia, and diabetes are in Section 13.8. The steps of glycogen synthesis and breakdown are in Key Idea 10 and Section 13.9; gluconeogenesis and the Cori cycle in Key Idea 9 and Section 13.10.

6. Other sugars enter glycolysis

The breakdown products of carbohydrate digestion are glucose, galactose, and fructose, but glycolysis is written for glucose. Both galactose and fructose are converted, in the liver, to intermediates that enter the glycolysis pathway, using the same kinds of reactions you have just classified: phosphorylation by a kinase (transferase), isomerization (isomerase), and cleavage (lyase). Galactose ends up as glucose-6-phosphate and enters at step 2; fructose is split into three-carbon pieces that enter at steps 5–6. So all the common hexoses are metabolized to pyruvate. The individual enzymes, and the inherited disease galactosemia (a missing galactose enzyme, so galactose-1-phosphate piles up and milk must be avoided), are in Section 13.7.

7. Regulation of glycolysis

The amount of glucose broken down depends on the need for pyruvate and ATP. Glycolysis is regulated at its three irreversible kinase steps, by three enzymes:

  • Hexokinase (step 1) is inhibited by high levels of its own product, glucose-6-phosphate. If G6P is not being used, glucose is not trapped in the cell.
  • Phosphofructokinase (step 3) is the main control point and is under allosteric control (Chapter 11): a regulator binds at a site other than the active site. It is inhibited by high levels of ATP and activated by high levels of ADP or AMP. The logic: glycolysis exists to make ATP, so the ATP/AMP ratio is the sensible switch.
  • Pyruvate kinase (step 10) is inhibited by high levels of ATP or acetyl CoA. Plenty of acetyl CoA means Stage 3 already has fuel, so the cell stops making more pyruvate.

In one sentence: when the cell has plenty of energy (ATP, acetyl CoA, unused G6P), glycolysis slows; when it is energy-poor (ADP, AMP), glycolysis speeds up. Hormonal control of the same enzymes is in Section 13.8.

8. Pentose phosphate pathway

Glucose can be degraded by a pathway different from glycolysis: the pentose phosphate pathway, a two-stage route for glucose-6-phosphate whose products are NADPH (the coenzyme needed in lipid biosynthesis, Chapter 15) and ribose-5-phosphate (the pentose needed to build nucleic acids and many coenzymes: ATP, GTP, UTP, CoA, NAD+, FAD, and RNA all contain ribose). NADPH is the reduced form of NADP+, the phosphorylated version of NADH, and it is essential to biosynthetic pathways.

  • Stage 1, oxidative stage: glucose-6-phosphate (6 C) is oxidized and decarboxylated to ribulose-5-phosphate (5 C) + CO2. Dehydrogenases (oxidoreductases) do the oxidizing, and the coenzyme is NADP+ → NADPH (2 NADPH per glucose-6-phosphate).
  • Stage 2, non-oxidative stage: ribulose-5-phosphate is isomerized to ribose-5-phosphate (an isomerase; same formula, no coenzyme).

Glucose-6-phosphate enters this pathway when the cell needs NADPH and/or ribose-5-phosphate for biosynthesis. When ribose is not needed, ribose-5-phosphate is converted to fructose-6-phosphate and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, both glycolysis intermediates, and returns to glycolysis. People with G6PD deficiency (glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, the first oxidative enzyme) cannot make enough NADPH in red blood cells, and certain drugs or foods can trigger red-cell breakdown (hemolytic anemia). The pathway is laid out in the second half of Section 13.7.

9. Gluconeogenesis vs. glycolysis

  • Gluconeogenesis is the synthesis of glucose from non-carbohydrate sources: pyruvate, lactate, glycerol, and some amino acids, after they are converted to pyruvate or another intermediate. It is an anabolic pathway (initial reactant pyruvate, final product glucose), and about 90% of it takes place in the liver.
  • Because it consumes ATP, it runs only when needed: to replenish depleted liver glycogen, to convert the lactate from strenuous exercise back to glucose (completing the Cori cycle), and to maintain blood glucose when glycogen stores are gone. Without it, the glucose-dependent brain would be in trouble after a single day without food.
  • The pathway is similar, but not identical, to glycolysis run backward. Seven glycolysis reactions are simply reversed, using the same enzymes. The three that cannot be reversed are the kinase steps, 1, 3, and 10. Kinases use or make ATP, and if you need ATP one way, you don’t need it the other way, so each kinase step is bypassed by a different enzyme:
    • Glycolysis step 10 (pyruvate kinase) is bypassed in two steps: pyruvate is carboxylated to oxaloacetate (4 C) using ATP, and oxaloacetate is converted to phosphoenolpyruvate using the high-energy compound GTP. The large energy difference between pyruvate and PEP is why one step is not enough.
    • Glycolysis step 3 (phosphofructokinase) is bypassed by fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase, a hydrolase that simply removes the phosphate with water. No ATP is needed, and none is recovered.
    • Glycolysis step 1 (hexokinase) is bypassed by glucose-6-phosphatase, another hydrolase, which releases free glucose to the blood.
  • Step 7 of glycolysis (phosphoglycerate kinase) runs backward with the same enzyme, but in this direction it costs ATP. Add it up: ATP or GTP is used at three places, each twice (two pyruvates per glucose), so gluconeogenesis spends 6 ATP equivalents to make one glucose, while glycolysis produces a net 2 ATP from one glucose.
  • Glucagon stimulates gluconeogenesis; insulin inhibits it. Glycolysis and gluconeogenesis are regulated in opposite directions so they do not run at full speed at the same time. Details, the Cori cycle, and the step-by-step contrast with glycolysis are in Section 13.10.

Health Career Connection

  • Dental hygiene – the acid attack: Plaque bacteria run glycolysis and then ferment pyruvate to lactic acid, just as your muscles make lactate. Within minutes of a sugary snack, plaque pH can drop below the point where enamel demineralizes (about 5.5). Frequent snacking means more acid attacks.
  • Nursing – diabetes: In type 1 diabetes the pancreas makes little or no insulin. In type 2 the cells respond poorly to it. Either way, glucose stays high in the blood instead of entering cells. Recognizing hypoglycemia (shaky, sweaty, confused) and hyperglycemia is a core nursing and dental-office skill.
  • Nursing – lactic acidosis: In shock or severe hypoxia, cells depend on anaerobic glycolysis and lactate builds up. Serum lactate is a common lab marker of poor tissue perfusion.
  • Dental hygiene – saliva fights back: Saliva is supersaturated with calcium and phosphate. Between acid attacks it redeposits mineral onto softened enamel (remineralization), and fluoride speeds this up. Sugar-free gum helps by increasing saliva flow. Medications that cause dry mouth, such as some antidepressants, antihistamines, and beta blockers, raise caries risk.
  • Both – vitamins are coenzymes: Niacin, riboflavin, pantothenic acid, and thiamine become NAD+, FAD, coenzyme A, and the thiamine cofactor of pyruvate dehydrogenase. A patient with poor intake (alcohol use disorder, malabsorption, restrictive diets) can have a metabolic problem that is really a vitamin problem.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Lactate as a vital sign. In shock or sepsis (a body-wide response to infection), tissues do not get enough oxygen, pyruvate is shunted to lactate, and the H+ that comes with it causes lactic acidosis. A serum lactate above about 2 mmol/L is a red flag; watching it fall shows perfusion is improving. Lactated Ringer’s, a common IV fluid, contains sodium lactate; a working liver converts that lactate to bicarbonate, so the fluid does not add acid. Red blood cells have no mitochondria and live on glycolysis (2 ATP per glucose) plus the pentose phosphate pathway. Thiamine is the cofactor of pyruvate dehydrogenase: without it pyruvate cannot become acetyl CoA, lactate rises, and the heavily aerobic brain and heart suffer first. That is beriberi (heart failure, neuropathy) and Wernicke encephalopathy (confusion, unsteady gait) in alcohol use disorder, which is why thiamine is given with IV dextrose. The brain depends on glucose (about 120 g/day) and cannot burn fatty acids, so hypoglycemia (below 70 mg/dL) shows up as sweating, shakiness, and a racing heart, then confusion and seizures; the remedy is glucose itself, by mouth or as IV dextrose, following protocol. HbA1c, glucose permanently attached to hemoglobin, reports the average glucose of the past 2–3 months. In healthy exercise, lactate rises and clears within an hour; it is fuel, not a poison.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Caries is glycolysis you can see. Streptococcus mutans and its neighbors in the plaque biofilm take up sucrose, glucose, and fructose, run glycolysis, and, being bacteria with no mitochondria and little oxygen under the biofilm, reduce pyruvate to lactic acid. The Stephan curve plots what happens next: plaque pH falls within 2–5 minutes of a sugar rinse, dips below the critical pH of about 5.5 at which hydroxyapatite begins to dissolve (demineralization), and takes 20–40 minutes of salivary buffering to recover. Frequent sipping of soda keeps the curve below 5.5 for hours. Sucrose is doubly bad: besides being fermented, it is the raw material bacteria use to build sticky glucans that glue the biofilm to enamel. Fluoride does more than build fluorapatite. At low pH it enters bacteria as HF, and inside the cell it inhibits enolase, the step-9 lyase of glycolysis; less phosphoenolpyruvate means less acid and less sugar uptake, so fluoride is also an antibacterial agent. Xylitol, a five-carbon sugar alcohol, is taken up but cannot be fermented, so it feeds no acid; chewing it in sugar-free gum also stimulates saliva, whose bicarbonate buffers the plaque and whose calcium and phosphate drive remineralization. Patients with xerostomia (dry mouth) lose that protection and need a tighter recall schedule.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Identify the coenzyme

In one step, the C–OH group of malate is converted to a C=O. Which coenzyme is used?

Solution

An alcohol is oxidized to a carbonyl, so the coenzyme is NAD+ (reduced to NADH).

Check: The substrate lost two hydrogens (one from O–H, one from C–H) and gained no oxygen atoms. That is the NAD+ signature; FAD is for C–C → C=C.

Example 2: Counting glycolysis products

How much net ATP and NADH are made when 3 glucose molecules go through glycolysis?

Solution

Per glucose: 2 ATP and 2 NADH. For 3 glucose: 6 ATP, 6 NADH, and 6 pyruvate.

Check: Gross ATP would be 12 made minus 6 spent = 6 net. Same answer.

Example 3: Fate of pyruvate

During an all-out 400 m sprint, what happens to most pyruvate in leg muscle? Why?

Solution

O2 supply cannot keep up, so pyruvate is reduced to lactate. This step regenerates NAD+, which glycolysis needs to keep producing ATP.

Check: Lactate dehydrogenase is an oxidoreductase running in the reduction direction: pyruvate’s C=O becomes lactate’s C–OH while NADH becomes NAD+.

Example 4: Anabolic or catabolic?

Classify: (a) glycogen → glucose-1-phosphate; (b) lactate → glucose (in liver).

Solution

(a) Breaking down: catabolic (glycogenolysis). (b) Building glucose: anabolic (gluconeogenesis).

Check: (a) goes polymer → monomer; (b) goes 3-carbon → 6-carbon and costs ATP. Size and energy direction agree with the labels.

Example 5: Classify a glycolysis step

Problem. Step 6 of glycolysis converts glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (an aldehyde) into 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate. Identify (a) the reaction type, (b) the enzyme class, and (c) the coenzyme, and explain how you can tell from the structures.

Solution. (a) The aldehyde carbon (CHO) ends up as a carbon bonded to two oxygens (an acyl phosphate, C(=O)–OPO32−), so the carbon gained oxygen and lost hydrogen: an oxidation, with a phosphate added at the same time. (b) An enzyme that oxidizes a substrate while reducing a coenzyme is a dehydrogenase, which belongs to the oxidoreductase class. (c) The electrons must go somewhere; they go to NAD+, giving NADH. The new phosphate comes from free inorganic phosphate, not ATP, so no ATP is spent.

Check: The enzyme’s name, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase, confirms the class. And the pathway needs this NADH: it is the only oxidation in glycolysis, which is why NAD+ regeneration (Key Idea 4) is such a big deal.

Example 6: Reading enzyme names for enzyme class

Problem. Classify each glycolysis enzyme into one of the six enzyme classes from Chapter 11: hexokinase, aldolase, phosphoglycerate mutase, enolase, pyruvate kinase.

Solution. Hexokinase and pyruvate kinase move a phosphate between ATP/ADP and a substrate: transferases. Aldolase splits a C–C bond without water or oxidation: a lyase. Phosphoglycerate mutase moves a phosphate from one carbon to another within the same molecule: an isomerase. Enolase removes H2O to form a C=C (a dehydration): a lyase.

Check: No hydrolases (no water is used to break a bond; digestion is where the hydrolases live) and no ligases (nothing is joined at the cost of ATP). Glycolysis uses only four of the six classes: transferase, isomerase, lyase, oxidoreductase.

Example 7: Coupling to ATP

Problem. Attaching a phosphate to glucose using free phosphate has ΔG°′ = +3.3 kcal/mol. Show that hexokinase can make the reaction go by using ATP instead, and state the overall ΔG°′.

Solution. Add the two reactions. Glucose + Pi → glucose-6-phosphate + H2O (+3.3 kcal/mol) and ATP + H2O → ADP + Pi (−7.3 kcal/mol) sum to glucose + ATP → glucose-6-phosphate + ADP, ΔG°′ = +3.3 + (−7.3) = −4.0 kcal/mol. The water and free phosphate cancel. The negative sign means the coupled reaction is favorable.

Check: The coupled reaction is favorable but by less than 7.3 kcal/mol, because part of ATP’s energy was “spent” pushing the unfavorable half uphill. This is the pattern for every kinase step.

Example 8: A lactate result

Problem. A patient arriving with a severe infection has a serum lactate of 4.8 mmol/L (reference < 2). Using Key Idea 4, explain in chemical terms where the lactate came from and why the blood pH is likely low.

Solution. Poor perfusion means the tissues are short of O2, so the pyruvate dehydrogenase route and the mitochondria are starved. To keep making ATP, cells keep glycolysis running and use lactate dehydrogenase to reduce pyruvate to lactate, regenerating NAD+. Lactic acid (pKa about 3.9) is fully ionized at blood pH, so each lactate released into blood comes with an H+; when production outruns the liver’s ability to clear it, bicarbonate is used up and pH falls: lactic acidosis.

Check: The same chemistry occurs in a sprinting athlete, but there the liver and heart clear the lactate within an hour; in sepsis, production is widespread and clearance is impaired, so the number stays high.

Example 9: Set up the table from a word description

Problem. A step is described in words: “Ribulose-5-phosphate is converted to ribose-5-phosphate.” A second step: “Glucose-6-phosphate is converted to a five-carbon sugar phosphate, releasing CO2, while NADP+ becomes NADPH.” For each, fill in the four columns: type of reaction, how you can tell, enzyme class, coenzyme.

Solution. First step: ribulose-5-phosphate and ribose-5-phosphate are both C5 sugar phosphates with the same formula (one is a ketose, the other an aldose), so this is an isomerization; the tell is “same formula in and out, no coenzyme, no energy change”; the enzyme is an isomerase; coenzyme none. Second step: a carbon left as CO2 (6 C → 5 C) and a coenzyme gained hydrogen, so this is an oxidation plus decarboxylation; the tell is “NADP+ → NADPH means the substrate lost H and was oxidized; CO2 out means decarboxylation”; the enzyme is a dehydrogenase, an oxidoreductase; coenzyme NADP+, which is the oxidizing agent and is itself reduced.

Check. Count carbons: 5 → 5 for the isomerization, 6 → 5 + 1 for the decarboxylation. Both steps belong to the pentose phosphate pathway (Key Idea 8), and the coenzyme is NADP+ rather than NAD+ because the product NADPH is destined for biosynthesis, not for ATP.

Example 10: Glycolysis forward, gluconeogenesis backward

Problem. In step 3 of glycolysis, fructose-6-phosphate is converted to fructose-1,6-bisphosphate using ATP. (a) Does the reverse process in gluconeogenesis require ATP, release ATP, or neither? (b) Name the type of reaction and the enzyme class used in gluconeogenesis for this step. (c) Fill in the blanks: gluconeogenesis spends ATP or GTP at ____ places (each twice), for a net expenditure of ____ ATP equivalents, while glycolysis produces a net ____ ATP.

Solution. (a) Neither. The forward step spent ATP to add a phosphate; the reverse step cannot run the kinase backward to remake ATP. Instead it hydrolyzes the phosphate off with water, so no ATP is required and none is recovered. (b) Hydrolysis, by fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase, a hydrolase. (c) Three places (pyruvate → oxaloacetate with ATP, oxaloacetate → PEP with GTP, and 3-phosphoglycerate → 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate with ATP), each done twice because two pyruvates make one glucose: 6 ATP equivalents spent, versus a net 2 ATP produced by glycolysis.

Check. If you need ATP one way, you don’t need it the other way. A phosphatase is the opposite of a kinase: kinase adds phosphate from ATP (transferase), phosphatase removes it with water (hydrolase). And gluconeogenesis must cost more than glycolysis makes, or the cell could cycle glucose → pyruvate → glucose and create ATP from nothing.

Example 11: Match the coenzyme to its description

Problem. Match each coenzyme to the one description that fits best. Coenzymes: NAD+; FAD; NADH + H+; FADH2; coenzyme A. Descriptions: (i) the reduced form of the coenzyme built from riboflavin; (ii) carries acetyl groups as a thioester; (iii) the oxidizing agent used when a 2° alcohol becomes a ketone; (iv) the oxidizing agent used when –CH2–CH2– becomes –CH=CH–; (v) the coenzyme left over after a C=O bond has been formed.

Solution. (i) FADH2: FAD is built from riboflavin (B2), and the reduced form carries the two added hydrogens. (ii) Coenzyme A, made from pantothenic acid (B5); its –SH group forms the thioester. (iii) NAD+: more C–O bonds means NAD+. (iv) FAD: a C–C single bond to a C=C is a dehydrogenation handled by FAD. (v) NADH + H+: once the alcohol has been oxidized to the carbonyl, the NAD+ has accepted the hydrogen and is now NADH.

Check. Two of the five are oxidizing agents (NAD+, FAD), two are what those become after doing their job (NADH, FADH2), and one carries carbon rather than electrons (CoA). Every description used exactly once.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Writing 4 ATP for glycolysis. That is the gross amount; the net is 2.
  • Thinking glycolysis needs oxygen. It does not; what happens to pyruvate afterward does.
  • Mixing up glycogenesis, glycogenolysis, and gluconeogenesis. Break the words apart.
  • Treating NADH and NADPH as interchangeable. NADH is cashed in for ATP; NADPH is spent on biosynthesis and antioxidant defense. The pentose phosphate pathway makes NADPH, glycolysis makes NADH.
  • Saying pyruvate → acetyl CoA is “part of glycolysis.” Glycolysis ends at pyruvate in the cytoplasm; pyruvate dehydrogenase works in the mitochondrion and is the first step that releases CO2.
  • Calling a kinase a “phosphorylase.” Kinases are transferases that move a phosphate from ATP. (Glycogen phosphorylase, Section 13.9, is also a transferase, but it uses free phosphate, not ATP, to split a glycosidic bond.)
  • Forgetting that steps 6–10 run twice per glucose. Count each payoff-phase ATP and NADH twice.
  • Saying humans make ethanol under anaerobic conditions. Humans make lactate; yeast makes ethanol. Both routes exist only to regenerate NAD+.
  • Calling glycogenolysis “glycogenesis in reverse.” Glycogenesis needs UTP and goes through UDP-glucose; glycogenolysis uses free phosphate and never touches UDP. Only the phosphoglucomutase step is shared.
  • Assuming gluconeogenesis gets the glycolysis ATP back. It does not: the two kinase steps are bypassed by phosphatases (hydrolases) that throw the phosphate away, and the pyruvate → PEP detour costs ATP and GTP. Net: 6 ATP spent to make a glucose, versus 2 made by breaking one down.
  • Answering “what type of reaction” with an enzyme name. The reaction type is the chemistry (phosphorylation, oxidation, dehydration, isomerization, cleavage); the enzyme class follows from it. Fill in the “how can I tell” column first.

A red blood cell consumes one glucose. How many ATP, NADH, and lactate does it end up with, and why is the NADH count what it is?

Answer
2 ATP, 0 NADH, 2 lactate. Glycolysis makes 2 NADH, but with no mitochondria the cell must use both to reduce 2 pyruvate to 2 lactate, regenerating the NAD+ that step 6 needs.

Exercise 8

A muscle cell has a high ATP concentration and a low AMP concentration. Predict what PFK-1 is doing and what that means for the rate of glycolysis.

Answer
ATP binds PFK-1’s regulatory site and slows it; there is little AMP to speed it up. Glycolysis slows, since the cell already has the ATP it needs.

Exercise 9

True or false, and correct any false statement: “Glycogenolysis is the reverse of glycogenesis.”

Answer
False. Glycogenesis activates glucose-1-phosphate with UTP (making UDP-glucose) before glycogen synthase adds it to the chain; glycogenolysis removes a glucose unit with free phosphate using glycogen phosphorylase and never involves UTP or UDP. Only the phosphoglucomutase step (glucose-1-phosphate ↔ glucose-6-phosphate) is shared.

Exercise 10

Give the initial reactant and final product of each pathway: glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, glycogenesis, glycogenolysis, pentose phosphate pathway.

Answer
Glycolysis: glucose → pyruvate. Gluconeogenesis: pyruvate (or lactate, glycerol, amino acids) → glucose. Glycogenesis: glucose → glycogen. Glycogenolysis: glycogen → glucose-6-phosphate (free glucose in the liver). Pentose phosphate pathway: glucose-6-phosphate → ribose-5-phosphate + NADPH (+ CO2).
