Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish catabolic and anabolic pathways and describe the stages of catabolism.
  • Identify the job of the coenzymes ATP, NAD+, FAD, and coenzyme A.
  • Summarize glycolysis: starting material, products, and net ATP and NADH.
  • Predict the fate of pyruvate under aerobic and anaerobic conditions.
  • Describe how insulin and glucagon control glycogen synthesis and breakdown.

Key Ideas

1. The big picture

  • Catabolism breaks large molecules into smaller ones and releases energy. Anabolism builds large molecules and uses energy.
  • Stage 1: digestion (polysaccharides → monosaccharides). Stage 2: break those down to acetyl CoA (glycolysis + pyruvate oxidation). Stage 3: citric acid cycle and electron transport make most of the ATP (Chapter 14).
  • Carbohydrate digestion: salivary amylase (mouth), pancreatic amylase (small intestine), then maltase, sucrase, and lactase on the intestinal wall.

Professor Tip

Use the Latin: -lysis = splitting, -genesis = making, glyco- = glucose or glycogen, neo = new. Glycolysis splits glucose. Glycogenesis makes glycogen. Glycogenolysis splits glycogen. Gluconeogenesis makes "new" glucose from non-carbohydrates. And remember the name of the game in catabolism: oxidize the fuel and capture the electrons on NAD+ and FAD.

3. Glycolysis (cytoplasm, 10 steps)

  • Glucose (6 C) → 2 pyruvate (3 C each).
  • Energy-investment steps use 2 ATP. Energy-payoff steps make 4 ATP and 2 NADH.
  • Net: 2 ATP + 2 NADH per glucose. No O2 is needed.

4. Fates of pyruvate

  • Aerobic (O2 present): pyruvate → acetyl CoA + CO2 + NADH (in mitochondria), then on to the citric acid cycle.
  • Anaerobic, in humans (hard-working muscle, red blood cells): pyruvate → lactate. This regenerates NAD+ so glycolysis can keep going.
  • Anaerobic, in yeast: pyruvate → ethanol + CO2 (fermentation).

5. Blood glucose control

  • Insulin (released when blood glucose is high, e.g., after a meal): increases glucose uptake and glycogenesis.
  • Glucagon (liver) and epinephrine (muscle) when glucose is low: trigger glycogenolysis. The liver also runs gluconeogenesis.
  • Cori cycle: lactate from muscle goes to the liver, is rebuilt into glucose, and is sent back.

6. Other sugars enter glycolysis

Fructose and galactose are converted into glycolysis intermediates (galactose → glucose-6-phosphate; fructose → glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate and DHAP in the liver). In galactosemia, one of the enzymes for this conversion is missing.

7. Regulation of glycolysis

Three steps are essentially irreversible and serve as control points: hexokinase (inhibited by its own product, glucose-6-phosphate), phosphofructokinase (the main control point: slowed by high ATP and citrate, sped up by AMP/ADP), and pyruvate kinase. When the cell has plenty of energy, glycolysis slows.

8. Pentose phosphate pathway

An alternate route for glucose-6-phosphate that produces ribose-5-phosphate (for building nucleotides) and NADPH (for fatty acid synthesis and for protecting red blood cells from oxidative damage). People with G6PD deficiency cannot make enough NADPH, and certain drugs or foods can trigger red-cell breakdown (hemolytic anemia).

9. Gluconeogenesis vs. glycolysis

  • Gluconeogenesis builds glucose (mostly in the liver) from lactate, glycerol, and some amino acids.
  • It reverses most glycolysis steps but must bypass the three irreversible steps with different enzymes, and it costs ATP and GTP.
  • Glucagon stimulates it; insulin inhibits it. Glycolysis and gluconeogenesis are regulated in opposite directions so they do not run at full speed at the same time.

Health Career Connection

  • Dental hygiene – the acid attack: Plaque bacteria run glycolysis and then ferment pyruvate to lactic acid, just as your muscles make lactate. Within minutes of a sugary snack, plaque pH can drop below the point where enamel demineralizes (about 5.5). Frequent snacking means more acid attacks.
  • Nursing – diabetes: In type 1 diabetes the pancreas makes little or no insulin. In type 2 the cells respond poorly to it. Either way, glucose stays high in the blood instead of entering cells. Recognizing hypoglycemia (shaky, sweaty, confused) and hyperglycemia is a core nursing and dental-office skill.
  • Nursing – lactic acidosis: In shock or severe hypoxia, cells depend on anaerobic glycolysis and lactate builds up. Serum lactate is a common lab marker of poor tissue perfusion.
  • Dental hygiene – saliva fights back: Saliva is supersaturated with calcium and phosphate. Between acid attacks it redeposits mineral onto softened enamel (remineralization), and fluoride speeds this up. Sugar-free gum helps by increasing saliva flow. Medications that cause dry mouth, such as some antidepressants, antihistamines, and beta blockers, raise caries risk.

Further reading: M. Epple and J. Enax, "The Chemistry of Dental Care," ChemistryViews (Part 1, Part 2, Part 3).

Worked Examples

Example 1: Identify the coenzyme

In one step, the C–OH group of malate is converted to a C=O. Which coenzyme is used?

Solution

An alcohol is oxidized to a carbonyl, so the coenzyme is NAD+ (reduced to NADH).

Example 2: Counting glycolysis products

How much net ATP and NADH are made when 3 glucose molecules go through glycolysis?

Solution

Per glucose: 2 ATP and 2 NADH. For 3 glucose: 6 ATP, 6 NADH, and 6 pyruvate.

Example 3: Fate of pyruvate

During an all-out 400 m sprint, what happens to most pyruvate in leg muscle? Why?

Solution

O2 supply cannot keep up, so pyruvate is reduced to lactate. This step regenerates NAD+, which glycolysis needs to keep producing ATP.

Example 4: Anabolic or catabolic?

Classify: (a) glycogen → glucose-1-phosphate; (b) lactate → glucose (in liver).

Solution

(a) Breaking down: catabolic (glycogenolysis). (b) Building glucose: anabolic (gluconeogenesis).

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Writing 4 ATP for glycolysis. That is the gross amount; the net is 2.
  • Thinking glycolysis needs oxygen. It does not; what happens to pyruvate afterward does.
  • Mixing up glycogenesis, glycogenolysis, and gluconeogenesis. Break the words apart.

Which hormone stimulates glycogenesis, and when is it released?

Answer
Insulin, when blood glucose is high (after a meal)

What are the products when pyruvate is oxidized under aerobic conditions?

Answer
Acetyl CoA, CO2, and NADH

Which enzyme starts carbohydrate digestion, and where?

Answer
Salivary amylase, in the mouth

Which pathway supplies both NADPH and ribose-5-phosphate?

The pentose phosphate pathway
