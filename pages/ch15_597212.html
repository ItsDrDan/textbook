Learning Objectives

  • Explain why triacylglycerol in adipose tissue is the body's main long-term energy store and compare it with glycogen.
  • Trace a dietary fatty acid from a chylomicron into a stored triacylglycerol, naming lipoprotein lipase and the source of glycerol 3-phosphate.
  • Describe how glucagon and epinephrine switch on hormone-sensitive lipase through cAMP and protein kinase A, and how insulin switches it off.
  • State where the fatty acids and glycerol released by lipolysis go and what each is used for.
  • Predict what adipose tissue does after a meal, during a fast, and when insulin is missing.

A nurse weighing a patient in diabetic ketoacidosis, a dietitian planning calories for a burn patient, and a dental hygienist who notices a patient's weight loss and acetone breath are all looking at the same tissue at work: adipose tissue, the body's fat store. This section follows triacylglycerol into the fat cell after a meal and back out again during fasting or stress, and shows how two hormones you already know, insulin and glucagon, decide which direction the traffic runs.

Fat is the body's long-term energy store

You store energy in two chemical forms: glycogen (Chapter 13) and triacylglycerol (TAG), the triester of glycerol with three fatty acids (Chapter 9). Glycogen is quick to draw on but small. A well-fed adult holds roughly 400 to 500 g of glycogen in muscle and liver, about 1,600 to 2,000 kcal, less than one day's energy. The same adult carries about 11 to 12 kg of TAG, close to 100,000 kcal, enough to survive weeks of fasting.

Two chemical facts explain why fat is the better long-term store:

  • Fat is more reduced. The carbons in a fatty acid are mostly CH2 groups with almost no oxygen, so there is more to oxidize. Fat yields about 9 kcal/g, compared with 4 kcal/g for carbohydrate or protein.
  • Fat is stored dry. TAG is nonpolar, so it packs into a droplet with no water of hydration. Glycogen is polar and carries about 2 to 3 g of water for every gram of glycogen. Gram for gram of tissue, fat stores roughly six to seven times more energy. If your 100,000 kcal were stored as hydrated glycogen, you would carry about 75 kg of glycogen-plus-water in place of 12 kg of fat.

White and brown adipose tissue. Almost all of your fat is white adipose tissue: large cells, each nearly filled by a single lipid droplet, whose job is storage. Infants, and adults in small amounts around the neck and shoulders, also have brown adipose tissue, packed with mitochondria and small droplets. Its mitochondria contain the uncoupling protein thermogenin (UCP1), which lets protons re-enter the matrix without making ATP (Chapter 14), so the energy of fatty acid oxidation is released as heat. Brown fat burns fat to keep a newborn warm; white fat stores it.

Storage after a meal

Dietary fat reaches the fat cell in the lipoprotein particles you met in Section 15.5: chylomicrons from the intestine and VLDL (very-low-density lipoprotein) carrying fat the liver has made from excess carbohydrate. The steps are:

  1. Hydrolysis at the capillary. TAG cannot cross a cell membrane. Lipoprotein lipase (LPL), a hydrolase anchored to the inside of the capillary wall in adipose tissue and muscle, hydrolyzes the TAG in passing lipoproteins to fatty acids and glycerol. Insulin raises the amount of LPL in adipose tissue, so fat delivery to storage is highest after a meal.
  2. Fatty acids enter the adipocyte. Inside, acyl CoA synthetase (a ligase) activates them to fatty acyl CoA, exactly as in β-oxidation.
  3. Glycerol 3-phosphate comes from glucose. To rebuild a TAG, the cell needs a glycerol backbone carrying a phosphate, glycerol 3-phosphate. Adipocytes lack glycerol kinase, so they cannot reuse free glycerol; they must make glycerol 3-phosphate by reducing dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP) from glycolysis. That means a fat cell can store fat only when it is also taking in glucose, and glucose enters fat cells through GLUT4 transporters that insulin moves to the membrane. Fat storage therefore requires insulin twice: once for LPL and once for glucose uptake.
  4. Esterification. Three fatty acyl CoA groups are transferred onto glycerol 3-phosphate (transferases), the phosphate is removed, and the finished TAG joins the lipid droplet.

Professor Tip

Students often ask how eating sugar makes you gain fat. Follow the carbons: extra glucose → pyruvate → acetyl CoA → fatty acids in the liver (lipogenesis, Section 15.8) → VLDL → lipoprotein lipase → adipose TAG. And follow the signal: the same insulin that is released by the sugar also turns on LPL and GLUT4 in the fat cell and turns off the lipase that would release fat. Carbohydrate supplies both the material and the permission.

Mobilization during fasting and stress

Between meals, overnight, during exercise, or under stress, the body needs the stored fat back. The release of fatty acids and glycerol from TAG is called lipolysis (lipid hydrolysis) or fat mobilization. It is controlled by covalent modification (Chapter 11), by the same cAMP cascade that drives glycogen breakdown:

  1. Hormone. Falling blood glucose releases glucagon from the pancreas; stress, exercise, or cold releases epinephrine from the adrenal medulla. Both bind receptors on the adipocyte surface.
  2. Second messenger. The receptor activates adenylate cyclase, which makes cyclic AMP (cAMP).
  3. Kinase. cAMP activates protein kinase A, which transfers phosphate from ATP to hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL). The phosphorylated enzyme is the active form (covalent modification, Chapter 11).
  4. Complete hydrolysis. The activated lipase hydrolyzes all three ester bonds, releasing three fatty acids and one glycerol per TAG. Compare the incomplete hydrolysis by pancreatic lipase during digestion, which stops at a monoacylglycerol.

The two products take different roads, because one is water-soluble and the other is not:

  • Fatty acids are nearly insoluble in blood, so they bind to the plasma protein albumin, which carries up to several fatty acids at a time. Muscle, heart, kidney, and liver take them up for β-oxidation. In a long fast the liver turns much of this acetyl CoA into ketone bodies for the brain (Section 15.7).
  • Glycerol dissolves in plasma and goes to the liver, the main organ with glycerol kinase. There it becomes DHAP and is used for gluconeogenesis, supplying some of the glucose that red blood cells and the brain still need.

Insulin is the brake. After a meal, insulin activates a phosphatase and lowers cAMP, so HSL is dephosphorylated and switched off. Even a small amount of insulin is enough to restrain lipolysis, which is why, in a healthy person, mild fasting gives mild ketosis but never ketoacidosis.

Worked examples

Example 1: Predict the state of adipose tissue

Problem. Describe what is happening in a fat cell (a) two hours after a large pasta dinner and (b) at the end of a 24-hour fast. Name the hormone, the state of hormone-sensitive lipase, and the direction fatty acids are moving.

Solution. (a) After the meal, blood glucose and insulin are high. Insulin keeps cAMP low, so hormone-sensitive lipase is dephosphorylated and inactive; lipoprotein lipase is active, and GLUT4 brings in glucose for glycerol 3-phosphate. The liver is also converting extra carbohydrate into VLDL fat. Fatty acids move from blood into the cell and the TAG droplet grows. (b) After 24 hours, glucose and insulin are low and glucagon is high. cAMP rises, protein kinase A phosphorylates hormone-sensitive lipase, and lipolysis runs. Fatty acids leave on albumin for muscle and liver; glycerol leaves for liver gluconeogenesis; the liver is beginning to make ketone bodies. The droplet shrinks.

Check. Insulin and glucagon are opposites in every row of the regulation table. If your answer has both lipoprotein lipase and hormone-sensitive lipase active at the same time, one of them is wrong.

Example 2: How much energy is in 1 kg of body fat?

Problem. Adipose tissue is about 87% triacylglycerol by mass (the rest is water, protein, and cell structure). Using 9 kcal/g for fat, estimate the energy in 1.0 kg of adipose tissue, and the number of days it would cover a 2,000 kcal/day requirement.

Solution. TAG mass = 1,000 g × 0.87 = 870 g. Energy = 870 g × 9 kcal/g ≈ 7,800 kcal (commonly rounded to 7,700 kcal, the figure dietitians use for "one kilogram of body fat"). At 2,000 kcal/day that is about 3.9 days of total energy, or, put the other way, a sustained deficit of 500 kcal/day removes roughly 1 kg of fat every two weeks.

Check. Pure fat would be 9,000 kcal/kg; the answer must be a little less because tissue is not pure fat. 7,800 is reasonable.

Example 3: Why does an insulin-deficient patient lose weight and make ketones?

Problem. A newly diagnosed patient with type 1 diabetes has lost 6 kg in a month despite eating well, and has ketones in the urine. Explain both findings using this section.

Solution. With no insulin, the adipocyte behaves as if it were starving even though blood glucose is high. Hormone-sensitive lipase stays phosphorylated and active because nothing opposes glucagon, so lipolysis runs continuously and the fat stores shrink: weight loss. GLUT4 is not moved to the membrane, so the cell cannot make glycerol 3-phosphate and cannot re-store the fatty acids, and lipoprotein lipase is low, so dietary fat is not stored either. The flood of fatty acids reaches the liver, where β-oxidation produces acetyl CoA faster than the citric acid cycle can use it (oxaloacetate is being pulled into gluconeogenesis), and the excess becomes ketone bodies: ketonuria, and if unchecked, diabetic ketoacidosis.

Check. Starting insulin should reverse both: it switches HSL off, restores glucose uptake, and the ketones clear, which is exactly what happens clinically.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Energy balance. Body fat is the running total of calories in minus calories out; a persistent surplus is stored as TAG (obesity is this store grown large enough to harm health), and a persistent deficit is drawn from it at roughly 7,700 kcal per kilogram. Rapid weight loss in a patient with hyperglycemia and thirst points to uncontrolled diabetes: unopposed lipolysis plus loss of glucose (and its water) in the urine. Stress lipolysis. In sepsis, burns, and major trauma, epinephrine and cortisol keep hormone-sensitive lipase switched on for weeks; serum triglycerides and free fatty acids rise and fat stores waste even when the patient is fed, which is one reason critically ill patients need careful calorie and protein support. Long-term corticosteroid therapy redistributes fat toward the trunk and face while limbs thin. IV lipid. The lipid emulsion in parenteral nutrition, and the lipid vehicle of the anesthetic propofol (about 1.1 kcal/mL), are real calories: lipoprotein lipase handles them like chylomicrons, and they must be counted in the patient's intake. A high triglyceride value on a lipid panel is TAG in VLDL and chylomicrons; it is lowest after an overnight fast, which is why the test is drawn fasting.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

The epinephrine in a local anesthetic cartridge is there to constrict blood vessels, but it is the same hormone that raises cAMP in fat cells, so a nervous patient's own adrenaline, plus the cartridge, produces a small, brief burst of lipolysis and a rise in blood glucose; this is negligible for most patients but one more reason to minimize epinephrine in a poorly controlled diabetic patient. Chronic stress raises cortisol, which keeps lipolysis and protein breakdown running and, over time, impairs healing of periodontal tissues. And a patient who reports losing weight without trying, especially with a fruity breath odor or dry mouth (xerostomia), may be showing the unopposed lipolysis of undiagnosed diabetes; a referral for a blood glucose test is appropriate.

Summary

  • Adipose triacylglycerol is the main energy store: about 100,000 kcal in an average adult versus about 2,000 kcal of glycogen, because fat gives 9 kcal/g and is stored without water.
  • Brown adipose tissue uses thermogenin to burn fat for heat; white adipose tissue stores it.
  • After a meal, lipoprotein lipase (a hydrolase, increased by insulin) releases fatty acids from chylomicrons and VLDL; adipocytes re-esterify them with glycerol 3-phosphate made from glucose, because they lack glycerol kinase.
  • In fasting or stress, glucagon or epinephrine → cAMP → protein kinase A → phosphorylated (active) hormone-sensitive lipase, which completely hydrolyzes TAG to three fatty acids and glycerol.
  • Fatty acids travel on albumin to muscle and liver for β-oxidation; glycerol goes to the liver for gluconeogenesis.
  • Insulin switches lipolysis off; without insulin, lipolysis is unopposed, causing weight loss and ketone production.

Exercises

Exercise 1

A 70-kg adult stores about 400 g of glycogen and 12 kg of fat. Calculate the energy in each store and the ratio between them.

Answer
Glycogen: 400 g × 4 kcal/g = 1,600 kcal. Fat: 12,000 g × 9 kcal/g = 108,000 kcal. Ratio about 68 : 1. The fat store is larger both because there is more of it and because each gram holds more than twice the energy.

Exercise 2

Why can an adipocyte not simply reuse the glycerol released by lipolysis to rebuild triacylglycerol?

Answer
Adipocytes lack glycerol kinase, so they cannot phosphorylate glycerol to glycerol 3-phosphate. They must make glycerol 3-phosphate from DHAP produced by glycolysis, which requires glucose uptake (insulin and GLUT4). The free glycerol leaves for the liver.

Exercise 3

Name, in order, the five molecules or enzymes in the signal chain from epinephrine to active hormone-sensitive lipase, and state which enzyme class protein kinase A belongs to.

Answer
Epinephrine → receptor → adenylate cyclase → cAMP → protein kinase A → phosphorylated hormone-sensitive lipase. Protein kinase A is a transferase (it transfers a phosphate from ATP to the enzyme).

Exercise 4

Compare and contrast lipoprotein lipase and hormone-sensitive lipase: reaction catalyzed, location, and the hormone that increases each.

Answer
Both are hydrolases that hydrolyze the ester bonds of triacylglycerol. Lipoprotein lipase works on the capillary wall on TAG inside chylomicrons and VLDL, and insulin increases it in adipose tissue (storage). Hormone-sensitive lipase works inside the adipocyte on stored TAG, and glucagon or epinephrine activates it by phosphorylation (mobilization); insulin inhibits it.

Exercise 5

A marathon runner's blood is sampled at mile 20. Predict the levels (high or low) of insulin, glucagon, epinephrine, free fatty acids, and glycerol, and explain the fatty acid and glycerol results.

Answer
Insulin low; glucagon and epinephrine high; free fatty acids and glycerol high. Epinephrine and glucagon have activated hormone-sensitive lipase, so TAG is being hydrolyzed; the fatty acids (on albumin) supply muscle β-oxidation, and the glycerol is going to the liver for gluconeogenesis to help maintain blood glucose.

Exercise 6

A patient's parenteral nutrition includes 250 mL of a 20% lipid emulsion (20 g of fat per 100 mL). How many kilocalories does the fat alone supply?

Answer
250 mL × 20 g/100 mL = 50 g of fat; 50 g × 9 kcal/g = 450 kcal (commercial emulsions list about 2 kcal/mL because of the glycerol and phospholipid also present, giving about 500 kcal).

Exercise 7

Explain why a diet high in carbohydrate but very low in fat can still increase body fat.

Answer
Extra glucose becomes acetyl CoA in the liver and is converted to fatty acids (lipogenesis), which are exported as VLDL; lipoprotein lipase in adipose tissue releases the fatty acids for storage. The insulin released by the carbohydrate also raises lipoprotein lipase and GLUT4 and switches hormone-sensitive lipase off, so fat is stored and not released.

Exercise 8

In brown adipose tissue, fatty acid oxidation produces little ATP. Where does the energy go, and which protein is responsible?

Answer
It is released as heat. Thermogenin (UCP1) in the inner mitochondrial membrane lets protons flow back into the matrix without passing through ATP synthase, uncoupling electron transport from ATP synthesis.
