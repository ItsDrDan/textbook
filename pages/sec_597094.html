Learning Objectives
  1. Give two major types of reactions of alcohols.
  2. Describe the result of the oxidation of a primary alcohol.
  3. Describe the result of the oxidation of a secondary alcohol.

Chemical reactions in alcohols occur mainly at the functional group, but some involve hydrogen atoms attached to the OH-bearing carbon atom or to an adjacent carbon atom. Of the three major kinds of alcohol reactions, which are summarized in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\), two—dehydration and oxidation—are considered here. The third reaction type—esterification—is covered elsewhere.

Dehydration

As noted in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\), an alcohol undergoes dehydration in the presence of a catalyst to form an alkene and water. The reaction removes the OH group from the alcohol carbon atom and a hydrogen atom from an adjacent carbon atom in the same molecule:

 

Under the proper conditions, it is possible for the dehydration to occur between two alcohol molecules. The entire OH group of one molecule and only the hydrogen atom of the OH group of the second molecule are removed. The two ethyl groups attached to an oxygen atom form an ether molecule.

 

(Ethers are discussed in elsewhere) Thus, depending on conditions, one can prepare either alkenes or ethers by the dehydration of alcohols.

Both dehydration and hydration reactions occur continuously in cellular metabolism, with enzymes serving as catalysts and at a temperature of about 37°C. The following reaction occurs in the "Embden–Meyerhof" pathway

 

Although the participating compounds are complex, the reaction is the same: elimination of water from the starting material. The idea is that if you know the chemistry of a particular functional group, you know the chemistry of hundreds of different compounds.

Oxidation

Primary and secondary alcohols are readily oxidized. We saw earlier how methanol and ethanol are oxidized by liver enzymes to form aldehydes. Because a variety of oxidizing agents can bring about oxidation, we can indicate an oxidizing agent without specifying a particular one by writing an equation with the symbol [O] above the arrow. For example, we write the oxidation of ethanol—a primary alcohol—to form acetaldehyde—an aldehyde—as follows:

 

We shall see that aldehydes are even more easily oxidized than alcohols and yield carboxylic acids. Secondary alcohols are oxidized to ketones. The oxidation of isopropyl alcohol by potassium dichromate (\(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\)) gives acetone, the simplest ketone:

Unlike aldehydes, ketones are relatively resistant to further oxidation, so no special precautions are required to isolate them as they form. Note that in oxidation of both primary (RCH2OH) and secondary (R2CHOH) alcohols, two hydrogen atoms are removed from the alcohol molecule, one from the OH group and other from the carbon atom that bears the OH group.

These reactions can also be carried out in the laboratory with chemical oxidizing agents. One such oxidizing agent is potassium dichromate. The balanced equation (showing only the species involved in the reaction) in this case is as follows:

\[\ce{8H^{=} + Cr2O7^{2-} + 3CH3CH2OH -> 3CH3CHO + 2Cr^{3+} + 7H2O} \nonumber \]

Alcohol oxidation is important in living organisms. Enzyme-controlled oxidation reactions provide the energy cells need to do useful work. One step in the metabolism of carbohydrates involves the oxidation of the secondary alcohol group in isocitric acid to a ketone group:

The overall type of reaction is the same as that in the conversion of isopropyl alcohol to acetone.

Tertiary alcohols (R3COH) are resistant to oxidation because the carbon atom that carries the OH group does not have a hydrogen atom attached but is instead bonded to other carbon atoms. The oxidation reactions we have described involve the formation of a carbon-to-oxygen double bond. Thus, the carbon atom bearing the OH group must be able to release one of its attached atoms to form the double bond. The carbon-to-hydrogen bonding is easily broken under oxidative conditions, but carbon-to-carbon bonds are not. Therefore tertiary alcohols are not easily oxidized.

Write an equation for the oxidation of each alcohol. Use [O] above the arrow to indicate an oxidizing agent. If no reaction occurs, write “no reaction” after the arrow.

  1. CH3CH2CH2CH2CH2OH
Solution

The first step is to recognize the class of each alcohol as primary, secondary, or tertiary.

  1. This alcohol has the OH group on a carbon atom that is attached to only one other carbon atom, so it is a primary alcohol. Oxidation forms first an aldehyde and further oxidation forms a carboxylic acid.

  2. This alcohol has the OH group on a carbon atom that is attached to three other carbon atoms, so it is a tertiary alcohol. No reaction occurs.
  3. This alcohol has the OH group on a carbon atom that is attached to two other carbon atoms, so it is a secondary alcohol; oxidation gives a ketone.

Summary

Alcohols can be dehydrated to form either alkenes (higher temperature, excess acid) or ethers (lower temperature, excess alcohol). Primary alcohols are oxidized to form aldehydes. Secondary alcohols are oxidized to form ketones. Tertiary alcohols are not readily oxidized.

Lecture Connection

Alcohols do three things in this course: they burn (combustion), they lose water (dehydration) and they lose hydrogen (oxidation). For dehydration you need Zaitsev's rule; for oxidation you need the 1°/2°/3° classification from Section 4.2. Classify first, predict second.

Combustion

Like hydrocarbons, alcohols burn in oxygen to give carbon dioxide and water: C2H5OH + 3 O2 → 2 CO2 + 3 H2O. Balance it the same way you balanced hydrocarbon combustion in Chapter 2, remembering that the alcohol already contributes one oxygen atom. Ethanol burns with a clean, nearly invisible flame, which is why it is used in fondue burners and lab alcohol lamps.

Example: Combustion of an alcohol

Problem. Write and balance the equation for the complete combustion of 2-propanol, C3H7OH.

Solution. Products are always CO2 and H2O. Balance C first (3 CO2), then H (8 H → 4 H2O), then O. The right side has 6 + 4 = 10 O atoms; the alcohol supplies 1, so O2 must supply 9, which is 4½ O2. Double everything to clear the fraction:

2 C3H7OH + 9 O2 → 6 CO2 + 8 H2O

Check. C: 6 = 6. H: 16 = 16. O: 2 + 18 = 20 on the left; 12 + 8 = 20 on the right. The one difference from hydrocarbon combustion is the oxygen atom already in the alcohol; forgetting it is the most common error.

Dehydration: an elimination, with Zaitsev's rule

With a strong acid catalyst and heat, water is eliminated from an alcohol to form an alkene. The OH leaves from one carbon and an H leaves from a neighboring carbon; the double bond forms between them. You already know the reverse reaction: in hydration (Section 4.4 and the lab), water adds to an alkene to form an alcohol. Dehydration is an elimination, hydration is an addition, and the two are related the same way oxidation and reduction are.

Symmetric vs. asymmetric alcohols. If the two carbons next to the OH carbon are identical (3-pentanol, cyclohexanol), only one alkene is possible. If they are different, two alkenes can form, and you need Zaitsev's rule (also spelled Saytzeff): the alkene formed in the greatest amount is the one made by removing the hydrogen from the neighboring carbon that has the fewest hydrogens. Put another way, the major product has the most alkyl groups bonded to the C=C: the more substituted alkene wins. Notice that this is the opposite of Markovnikov's rule for addition, where the H goes to the carbon that already has more hydrogens. Count the alkyl groups on each possible C=C to decide.

Example: Zaitsev's rule with two possible alkenes

Problem. Predict the major and minor products when 2-butanol, CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3, is dehydrated with H2SO4 and heat.

Solution. The OH is on C2. Its neighbors are C1 (a CH3, three H atoms) and C3 (a CH2, two H atoms). Removing an H from C3 gives CH3CH=CHCH3, 2-butene, whose double bond carries two alkyl groups. Removing an H from C1 gives CH2=CHCH2CH3, 1-butene, whose double bond carries only one alkyl group. Zaitsev's rule: 2-butene is major, 1-butene is minor.

Check. Both products are C4H8, and each is 2-butanol (C4H10O) minus H2O. Notice that C3 had fewer hydrogens to give up yet still produced the major product; it is the substitution of the alkene, not the number of available H atoms, that decides.

Oxidation: the class of the alcohol decides

In organic chemistry an oxidation is an increase in the number of C–O bonds and/or a decrease in the number of C–H bonds. The symbol [O] over the arrow stands for any oxidizing agent, such as potassium dichromate, K2Cr2O7. The alcohol loses the H from its O–H and an H from the OH carbon, and a C=O forms, so the class of the alcohol decides the outcome:

  • Primary alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid. The aldehyde still has an H on its C=O carbon, so with excess [O] it is oxidized a second time.
  • Secondary alcohol → ketone. The ketone carbon has no H left, so the reaction stops there.
  • Tertiary alcohol → no reaction. There is no H bonded to the C–OH carbon to remove.

Reduction, written [H], is the reverse: an aldehyde goes back to a 1° alcohol and a ketone back to a 2° alcohol (Chapter 5). Oxidation/reduction and elimination/addition are the two reversible pairs you should be able to run in either direction.

In the Lab

Chromate oxidation test (alcohols and phenols experiment): orange chromium(VI) (dichromate, Cr2O72−, in the acidic reagent) turns green (Cr3+) when it oxidizes a 1° or 2° alcohol. A 3° alcohol cannot be oxidized, so the solution stays orange. The balanced equation in the section text is this same color change written out: dichromate is the orange reactant, Cr3+ the green product.

Professor Tip

Read the product first, then pick the reagent. A new C=O in place of a C–OH means [O]. A new C=C and a lost water means H+ and heat. CO2 and H2O mean O2 and a flame. The lecture's "what reactants lead to each product?" practice is exactly this matching exercise.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

One enzyme, three poisons. Ethanol is absorbed in the stomach and small intestine, and in the liver the enzymes alcohol dehydrogenase and aldehyde dehydrogenase act as the oxidizing agents. (A breathalyzer runs the same oxidation chemically, using the color change of an acidic dichromate reagent, orange to green, to measure the ethanol in exhaled breath.) Alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) treats ethanol, methanol and ethylene glycol as the same kind of molecule: a 1° alcohol to be oxidized to an aldehyde and then an acid. Ethanol's products (acetaldehyde, acetic acid) are handled routinely. Methanol's (formaldehyde, formic acid) damage the optic nerve, which is why the section text warns that as little as 15 mL can cause blindness. Ethylene glycol's (ending in oxalic acid) cause metabolic acidosis (blood pH below normal) and calcium oxalate crystals in the kidneys (Section 4.6). The antidote concept follows from the chemistry: give ADH something else to work on. Fomepizole blocks the enzyme, and ethanol itself is a competitive substrate that keeps ADH busy while the unchanged methanol or glycol is cleared. (Concept only; dosing belongs to pharmacology.)
