There is free rotation about the carbon-to-carbon single bonds (C–C) in alkanes. In contrast, the structure of alkenes requires that the carbon atoms of a double bond and the two atoms bonded to each carbon atom all lie in a single plane, and that each doubly bonded carbon atom lies in the center of a triangle. This part of the molecule’s structure is rigid; rotation about doubly bonded carbon atoms is not possible without rupturing the bond. Look at the two chlorinated hydrocarbons in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\).

In 1,2-dichloroethane (part (a) of Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\)), there is free rotation about the C–C bond. The two models shown represent exactly the same molecule; they are not isomers. You can draw structural formulas that look different, but if you bear in mind the possibility of this free rotation about single bonds, you should recognize that these two structures represent the same molecule:

In 1,2-dichloroethene (Figure \(\PageIndex{1b}\)), however, restricted rotation about the double bond means that the relative positions of substituent groups above or below the double bond become significant. This leads to a special kind of isomerism. The isomer in which the two chlorine (Cl) atoms lie on the same side of the molecule is called the cis isomer (Latin cis, meaning “on this side”) and is named cis-1,2-dichloroethene. The isomer with the two Cl atoms on opposite sides of the molecule is the trans isomer (Latin trans, meaning “across”) and is named trans-1,2-dichloroethene. These two compounds are cis-trans isomers (or geometric isomers), compounds that have different configurations (groups permanently in different places in space) because of the presence of a rigid structure in their molecule.

Consider the alkene with the condensed structural formula CH3CH=CHCH3. We could name it 2-butene, but there are actually two such compounds; the double bond results in cis-trans isomerism (Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\)).

Cis-2-butene has both methyl groups on the same side of the molecule. Trans-2-butene has the methyl groups on opposite sides of the molecule. Their structural formulas are as follows:

Figure \(\PageIndex{3}\): Models of (left) Cis-2-Butene and (right) Trans-2-Butene.

Note, however, that the presence of a double bond does not necessarily lead to cis-trans isomerism (Figure \(\PageIndex{4}\)). We can draw two seemingly different propenes:

Figure \(\PageIndex{4}\): Different views of the propene molecule (flip vertically). These are not isomers.

However, these two structures are not really different from each other. If you could pick up either molecule from the page and flip it over top to bottom, you would see that the two formulas are identical. Thus there are two requirements for cis-trans isomerism:

  1. Rotation must be restricted in the molecule.
  2. There must be two nonidentical groups on each doubly bonded carbon atom.

In these propene structures, the second requirement for cis-trans isomerism is not fulfilled. One of the doubly bonded carbon atoms does have two different groups attached, but the rules require that both carbon atoms have two different groups. In general, the following statements hold true in cis-trans isomerism:

  • Alkenes with a C=CH2 unit do not exist as cis-trans isomers.
  • Alkenes with a C=CR2 unit, where the two R groups are the same, do not exist as cis-trans isomers.
  • Alkenes of the type R–CH=CH–R can exist as cis and trans isomers; cis if the two R groups are on the same side of the carbon-to-carbon double bond, and trans if the two R groups are on opposite sides of the carbon-to-carbon double bond.

Cis-trans isomerism also occurs in cyclic compounds. In ring structures, groups are unable to rotate about any of the ring carbon–carbon bonds. Therefore, groups can be either on the same side of the ring (cis) or on opposite sides of the ring (trans). Cis/trans isomers of rings are optional in this course (the exam questions are about alkenes), but they are worth recognizing. For our purposes here, we represent all cycloalkanes as planar structures, and we indicate the positions of the groups, either above or below the plane of the ring.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Which compounds can exist as cis-trans isomers? Draw them.

  1. CH2=CHCH2CH2CH3
  2. CH3CH=CHCH2CH3
  3. CH3CH2CH=CHCH2CH3
Answer
  1. No: C1 carries two H atoms.
  2. Yes: cis- and trans-2-pentene (CH3 and CH2CH3 on the same side or on opposite sides of the C=C).
  3. Yes: cis- and trans-3-hexene (the two CH2CH3 groups on the same side or on opposite sides).
  4. No: C1 carries two H atoms.
  5. No: C2 carries two identical CH3 groups.

Key Takeaway

  • Cis-trans (geometric) isomerism exists when there is restricted rotation in a molecule and there are two nonidentical groups on each doubly bonded carbon atom.

Lecture Connection

Lecture's test for cis/trans is one question asked twice: does this double-bond carbon carry two different groups? Both carbons must pass. Then the labels: cis means the matching groups are on the same side of the double bond, trans means across.

Locked in place

Single bonds rotate; double and triple bonds cannot. An alkene is rigid and planar, so it has edges and sides, and the groups on its two carbons are locked where they are drawn. If each double-bond carbon has two different groups, two isomers exist:

  • cis: the matching groups (usually the main chain) are on the same side of the double bond (not bonded to the same carbon).
  • trans: they are on opposite sides.

If either double-bond carbon carries two identical groups (such as =CH2 or =C(CH3)2), cis/trans isomers are not possible. Alkynes are linear, so they never have cis/trans isomers.

Cis and trans isomers are stereoisomers: the atoms are connected in the same order, but they are arranged differently in space. They are different compounds with different melting and boiling points, and in living systems the difference matters: the double bonds in natural unsaturated fatty acids are almost all cis, which puts a kink in the chain (Chapter 9).

Professor Tip

Every cis/trans question you will see has a matching pair of groups (usually the two ends of the main chain, or two identical atoms) that can be called same side or opposite side. Look for that pair first, then decide: same side = cis, opposite sides = trans.

Example 1: Which alkenes have cis/trans isomers?

Problem. For each alkene, decide whether cis and trans isomers exist, and if so draw the trans isomer in condensed form: (a) 1-chloropropene, ClCH=CHCH3; (b) 2-methyl-2-butene, (CH3)2C=CHCH3; (c) 1-pentene, CH2=CHCH2CH2CH3.

Solution. Look at each double-bond carbon separately and ask, "Are its two groups different?"

  • (a) C1 has Cl and H (different). C2 has CH3 and H (different). Yes. Trans isomer: Cl and CH3 on opposite sides of the C=C; cis: same side.
  • (b) C2 has CH3 and CH3: two identical groups. No. Swapping the two methyls changes nothing, so there is only one 2-methyl-2-butene. You do not even need to look at C3.
  • (c) C1 has H and H. No. Any 1-alkene fails the test at C1.

Check. The test fails as soon as either carbon carries a matched pair. Both carbons must pass for cis/trans to exist, which is why 2-butene, 2-pentene, and 3-hexene have isomers and 1-butene, 2-methylpropene, and 2-methyl-2-pentene do not.

Example 2: Reading a real-world molecule

Problem. Sorbic acid, below, is a food preservative that stops mold growth in cheese and baked goods. (a) Which functional group makes it an acid? (b) What makes it unsaturated? (c) Is each double bond cis or trans in the drawing? (d) Give the shape and bond angle around C2.

Solution. (a) The carboxylic acid group, the C=O with an O–H on the same carbon at the right-hand end of the chain. (b) The two carbon–carbon double bonds, between C2–C3 and C4–C5: the chain is not saturated with hydrogen. (The C=O does not count; "unsaturated" refers to C=C and C≡C.) (c) Both are trans: at each C=C the chain continues on opposite sides of the double bond, as the zigzag shows. (d) C2 is a double-bond carbon with three electron groups: trigonal planar, 120°.

Check. Count hydrogens: CH3 (3), four =CH– carbons (4), COOH (1) = C6H8O2. A six-carbon chain with two double bonds and the acid group is exactly what the name 2,4-hexadienoic acid describes. ✓

Clinical Connection: Nursing

The hydrogenation reaction of Section 3.4 is run on an industrial scale on vegetable oils. An oil is a triacylglycerol whose fatty-acid chains contain several cis C=C bonds; the kinks keep the chains from packing, so the oil is liquid. Bubbling H2 over a nickel catalyst adds H to some of those double bonds, straightens the chains, and turns the oil into a spreadable solid, which is how margarine and shortening were made. The problem is that the catalyst also lets some of the remaining double bonds flip from cis to trans. These trans fatty acids are even worse for the heart than saturated fat: they raise LDL ("bad") cholesterol and lower HDL ("good") cholesterol on a lipid panel, worsening hyperlipidemia (excess blood lipids) and the risk of atherosclerosis, the fatty plaque that narrows arteries. Partially hydrogenated oils were removed from the U.S. food supply for this reason. When you read a patient's lipid panel, you are looking at the downstream result of cis/trans isomerism. One more polyene to recognize: beta-carotene in carrots is a long chain of alternating C=C bonds that the body splits into two molecules of vitamin A.
