Learning Objectives
  • To recognize amino acids and classify them based on the characteristics of their side chains.

The proteins in all living species, from bacteria to humans, are constructed from the same set of 20 amino acids, so called because each contains an amino group attached to a carboxylic acid. The amino acids in proteins are α-amino acids, which means the amino group is attached to the α-carbon of the carboxylic acid. Humans can synthesize only about half of the needed amino acids; the remainder must be obtained from the diet and are known as essential amino acids. However, two additional amino acids have been found in limited quantities in proteins: Selenocysteine was discovered in 1986, while pyrrolysine was discovered in 2002.

The amino acids are colorless, nonvolatile, crystalline solids, melting and decomposing at temperatures above 200°C. These melting temperatures are more like those of inorganic salts than those of amines or organic acids and indicate that the structures of the amino acids in the solid state and in neutral solution are best represented as having both a negatively charged group and a positively charged group. Such a species is known as a zwitterion.

Classification

In addition to the amino and carboxyl groups, amino acids have a side chain or R group attached to the α-carbon. Each amino acid has unique characteristics arising from the size, shape, solubility, and ionization properties of its R group. As a result, the side chains of amino acids exert a profound effect on the structure and biological activity of proteins. Although amino acids can be classified in various ways, one common approach is to classify them according to whether the functional group on the side chain at neutral pH is nonpolar, polar but uncharged, negatively charged, or positively charged. The first amino acid to be isolated was asparagine in 1806. It was obtained from protein found in asparagus juice (hence the name). Glycine, the major amino acid found in gelatin, was named for its sweet taste (Greek glykys, meaning “sweet”). In some cases an amino acid found in a protein is actually a derivative of one of the common 20 amino acids (one such derivative is hydroxyproline). The modification occurs after the amino acid has been assembled into a protein.

Configuration

Notice in Table \(\PageIndex{1}\) that glycine is the only amino acid whose α-carbon is not chiral. Therefore, with the exception of glycine, the amino acids could theoretically exist in either the D- or the L-enantiomeric form and rotate plane-polarized light. As with sugars, chemists used L-glyceraldehyde as the reference compound for the assignment of absolute configuration to amino acids. Its structure closely resembles an amino acid structure except that in the latter, an amino group takes the place of the OH group on the chiral carbon of the L-glyceraldehyde and a carboxylic acid replaces the aldehyde. Modern stereochemistry assignments using the Cahn-Ingold-Prelog priority rules used ubiquitously in chemistry show that all of the naturally occurring chiral amino acids are S except Cys which is R.

We learned that all naturally occurring sugars belong to the D series. It is interesting, therefore, that nearly all known plant and animal proteins are composed entirely of L-amino acids. However, certain bacteria contain D-amino acids in their cell walls, and several antibiotics (e.g., actinomycin D and the gramicidins) contain varying amounts of D-leucine, D-phenylalanine, and D-valine.

Summary

Amino acids can be classified based on the characteristics of their distinctive side chains as nonpolar, polar but uncharged, negatively charged, or positively charged. The amino acids found in proteins are L-amino acids.

Lecture Connection

In lecture we spend most of our time on one skill: look at a side chain and say what kind of amino acid it is. The backbone (N–Cα–C) is identical in all 20; only the R group changes, and the R group decides the amino acid's personality: whether it likes water, whether it carries a charge, and what interactions it can form inside a folded protein.

The parts of an amino acid

Every one of the 20 common amino acids is built the same way: a central carbon, the α-carbon (alpha carbon), carries four groups: an amino group (–NH2, written –NH3+ at body pH), a carboxyl group (–COOH, written –COO− at body pH), a hydrogen, and a side chain called the R group. Because the amine sits on the carbon right next to the carboxyl carbon, these are α-amino acids.

Proteins make up about half of the dry weight of the body and do most of its work: transport (hemoglobin), structure (collagen), hormones (insulin), antibodies, and enzymes. Unlike fat and glycogen, protein is not stored, so it must be eaten every day (about 0.8 g per kilogram of body weight for adults). Of the 20 amino acids, 11 can be made by the body and 9, the essential amino acids, must come from food; meat, eggs and milk supply all nine, while grains, beans and nuts are each short of one or more. Chapter 15 returns to this.

In lecture the side chains are sorted in two steps. First the big split: is the R group hydrophobic (nonpolar: only C and H, or C, H and a thioether S) or hydrophilic (polar: it has an O–H, S–H, amide, carboxylate or extra nitrogen that can hydrogen-bond or carry a charge)? Then sort the hydrophilic ones by what they can do:

  • Basic: contains an N (or N+) on the side chain with an available lone pair. His, Lys, Arg.
  • Acidic: contains a COOH (COO−) on the side chain. Asp (aspartate), Glu (glutamate).
  • Neutral polar: capable of hydrogen bonding but uncharged at pH 7.4. Ser, Thr, Tyr, Cys, Asn, Gln.

Everything that is not hydrophobic is hydrophilic, so the hydrophobic list is the one to learn: Gly, Ala, Val, Leu, Ile, Phe, Met, Pro, Trp. The other eleven are hydrophilic. An exam question may use either level of the sort, "polar or nonpolar" (the same split as hydrophilic or hydrophobic) or "acidic, basic, or neutral." Tyrosine is the one that straddles classes: its ring is aromatic, but its phenol O–H hydrogen-bonds, so lecture and this book file it under hydrophilic (polar neutral).

In this course

The section text above sorts side chains as nonpolar, "polar but neutral," "negatively charged," and "positively charged." On exams use the lecture names for the same four groups: nonpolar, polar neutral, acidic (Asp, Glu) and basic (His, Lys, Arg). Histidine counts as basic even though only part of it is protonated at pH 7.4. The text also mentions R and S labels from the Cahn–Ingold–Prelog rules; we do not use R/S in this course. Stereochemistry of amino acids is D or L from a Fischer projection, exactly as it was for sugars.

Professor Tip

Three-letter codes are used when you write a peptide; one-letter codes when you write a whole protein. Of the 20, eleven one-letter codes are simply the first letter of the name (G, A, V, L, I, P, M, S, T, C, H). Learn the other nine as a set: arginine R and tyrosine Y use the second letter of the name; phenylalanine is F (say "Fenylalanine"); lysine is K, which sits next to L in the alphabet; tryptophan is W (two rings, "double-you"); and the two acids and their amides pair up, D (Asp) with N (Asn) and E (Glu) with Q (Gln). The Peptide Builder game drills these.

Stereochemistry: every amino acid but glycine is L

  • Every amino acid except glycine has a chiral α-carbon: it carries four different groups (–NH3+, –COO−, –H and R). Glycine's R group is a second H, so it is achiral and has no D or L form.
  • Draw the Fischer projection the same way you did for sugars in Chapter 8: the carbon chain runs vertically with the most oxidized carbon (the carboxylate) on top, so the R group ends up at the bottom and the horizontal bonds (H and NH3+) point toward you. Then read the amino group: on the left means L; on the right means D. The amino acids in human proteins are L, and the ribosome only uses L amino acids. D-amino acids occur mainly in bacterial cell walls.
  • "L" is not short for "levorotatory" or "left-handed." L and D come from the Fischer projection and say nothing about which way the compound rotates light.

Worked examples

Example 1: Classify from the structure

Problem. Three amino acids have the side chains (a) –CH2CH2COO−, (b) –CH2OH and (c) –CH2CH(CH3)2. Name each, give its three- and one-letter codes, classify its R group, and state its net charge at pH 7.4.

Solution.

  • (a) A second carboxylate two carbons from the α-carbon: glutamate, Glu, E; acidic. At pH 7.4: –NH3+ (+1) and two –COO− (−2), net −1.
  • (b) A hydroxyl group: serine, Ser, S; polar neutral. Net 0 (zwitterion; the OH is uncharged).
  • (c) A branched hydrocarbon: leucine, Leu, L; nonpolar (and essential). Net 0.

Check. Only acidic and basic side chains change the net charge at pH 7.4. If (a) had been one carbon shorter (–CH2COO−) it would be aspartate, Asp, D, with the same classification and charge.

Example 2: Take an amino acid apart

Problem. The amino acid below is drawn in its zwitterion form. (a) Point out the carboxyl group, the amine group, the α-carbon and the R group. (b) Name it and give both abbreviations. (c) Classify the R group (polar, nonpolar, acidic or basic) and explain why. (d) Draw the structure at a pH well below its pI and well above its pI.

Solution.

(a) The carboxyl group is the C(=O)O− on the right; the amine group is the –NH3+ above the central carbon; the α-carbon is that central carbon, the one bonded to both; the R group is everything hanging off it to the left, –CH(OH)CH3.

(b) A side chain with an O–H on a carbon that also carries a methyl group is threonine: Thr, T. (Serine would be –CH2OH, one carbon shorter and with no methyl.)

(c) Polar (hydrophilic, neutral). The hydroxyl group hydrogen-bonds with water but has no charge at pH 7.4, and there is no second carboxyl group (acidic) or second nitrogen (basic) on the side chain.

(d) Well below the pI (about 5.6), everything that can hold an H+ does: +H3N–CH[CH(OH)CH3]–COOH, net +1. Well above the pI, everything that can give up an H+ has: H2N–CH[CH(OH)CH3]–COO−, net −1. The side-chain O–H is an alcohol, far too weak an acid to lose its H+ in either case, so it is drawn the same way in all three forms.

Check. The charge should step down +1 → 0 → −1 as the pH climbs, and only the two backbone groups changed. A side chain changes form with pH only when it is acidic (Asp, Glu) or basic (Lys, Arg, His).
