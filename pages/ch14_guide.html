Learning Objectives

  • Describe how pyruvate becomes acetyl CoA (oxidation and decarboxylation) and name the coenzymes involved.
  • Know the initial reactant and final product of the citric acid cycle (oxaloacetate → oxaloacetate) and what enters and leaves one turn.
  • Given the reactants and products of a step, in words or as structures, say what type of reaction is occurring, which enzyme (kinase, isomerase, synthase, dehydrogenase, and so on) and which coenzyme (NAD+, NADH, CoA, FAD, FADH2, CoQ) is likely involved.
  • Count and track carbons through glycolysis and the citric acid cycle: turns per glucose, decarboxylations per turn, CO2 per glucose.
  • Explain how electron transport (complexes I–IV, coenzyme Q, cytochrome c) and ATP synthase use NADH and FADH2 to make ATP, and predict what an inhibitor or uncoupler does.
  • Know the ATP output from NADH (2.5) and FADH2 (1.5) and calculate the ATP from acetyl CoA and from the complete oxidation of glucose (32).
  • Describe the factors that activate and inhibit the citric acid cycle and electron transport.
  • Calculate the energy content of food from grams of carbohydrate, protein, and fat.

Chapter Summary

The big ideas of the chapter, one paragraph each. Each paragraph ends with the section where it is taught in full.

1. The citric acid cycle (Krebs cycle)

Chapter 13 covered Stages 1 and 2; this chapter is Stage 3, oxidation. The cycle runs under aerobic conditions in the mitochondrial matrix and is a cyclic pathway: initial reactant and final product are both oxaloacetate. Acetyl CoA (2 C) + oxaloacetate (4 C) → citrate (6 C); eight steps later the acetyl carbons are gone as CO2 and oxaloacetate is back. Per turn: 2 CO2, 3 NADH, 1 FADH2, 1 GTP (= 1 ATP); two turns per glucose. Pyruvate gets in through the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (oxidation + decarboxylation: pyruvate + CoA + NAD+ → acetyl CoA + CO2 + NADH; needs thiamine), not as a cycle intermediate. For every step, set up the four-question table; the three oxidations that make a C=O (steps 3, 4 and 8) use NAD+, the one that makes a C=C (succinate → fumarate) uses FAD, and step 5 is the only direct GTP. Citrate synthase is treated as a ligase, aconitase and fumarase are lyases, the dehydrogenases are oxidoreductases. Carbon count first: 2 decarboxylations per turn, 2 turns per glucose, the other 2 CO2 from pyruvate dehydrogenase. Details, the figure, and the eight-step table: Sections 14.1 and 14.3.

2. Electron transport and oxidative phosphorylation

Four enzyme complexes (I, II, III, IV) in the inner mitochondrial membrane pass electrons from NADH and FADH2 to O2, the final electron acceptor, which becomes H2O. Two mobile carriers: coenzyme Q (fat-soluble quinone; I and II → III) and cytochrome c (heme protein, iron cycles Fe2+/Fe3+; III → IV). Order: NADH or FADH2 → (I or II) → CoQ → III → cyt c → IV → O2. Pumped H+ returns through ATP synthase (oxidative phosphorylation). Yields: NADH → 2.5 ATP; FADH2 → 1.5 ATP. LEO says GER: NADH → NAD+ is oxidation, O2 → H2O is reduction; without O2 the coenzymes are not regenerated and the cycle stops. Redox bookkeeping and examples: Section 14.2.

3. ATP from one glucose

Glycolysis 2 ATP + 2 NADH (= 7), pyruvate → acetyl CoA 2 NADH (= 5), two turns of the cycle 2 GTP + 6 NADH + 2 FADH2 (= 20): 32 ATP for C6H12O6 + 6 O2 → 6 CO2 + 6 H2O. The total fluctuates with the convention: this book and the lecture use 2.5 and 1.5 (32, or 30 with the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle); older books use 3 and 2 (36–38). Long story short, the range is 30–38 and 32 is the number to use here. Our cells are about 35% efficient; the rest is heat. Line-by-line tally: Section 14.5.

4. Energy in food

Carbohydrate and protein: 4 kcal/g. Fat: 9 kcal/g. (1 food Calorie = 1 kcal.) Fat yields more because its carbons are more reduced, so there is more to oxidize. The 4-4-9 rule is the basis of nutrition labels and of diet counseling for patients with diabetes, obesity, or malnutrition.

5. A closer look at electron transport

Four stations, two shuttles: Complex I (NADH in; pumps H+), Complex II (= succinate dehydrogenase; FADH2 in; does not pump), CoQ (to III), Complex III (pumps), cytochrome c (to IV), Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase; reduces O2 to water; pumps). The chemiosmotic model: pumping builds an H+ gradient; H+ returns through the F0 channel of ATP synthase and ATP is made at F1. NADH passes three pumps (2.5 ATP); FADH2 enters at II and passes two (1.5 ATP). Blockers (cyanide, CO at Complex IV) stop electron flow, so O2 use falls; uncouplers (DNP; thermogenin in brown fat) let H+ leak back, so O2 use and heat rise while ATP falls. Details, the figure, and the inhibitor table: Section 14.4.

6. Regulation and carbon balance

The cycle slows when ATP and NADH are high and speeds up when ADP is high: ATP inhibits citrate synthase (step 1) and ADP activates it; NADH inhibits isocitrate dehydrogenase (step 3) and ADP activates it. Electron transport depends on ADP, Pi, O2, and NADH, most strongly on ADP (respiratory control): a working cell breathes fast. Carbon balance: 2 C in as acetyl, 2 C out as CO2, oxaloacetate regenerated as a catalyst; per glucose, 2 CO2 from pyruvate dehydrogenase + 4 from two turns = 6. Details: Sections 14.3 and 14.4.

7. Getting glycolysis NADH into the mitochondria

The 2 NADH from glycolysis are made in the cytosol, and mitochondria are impermeable to NADH and NAD+, so a shuttle moves the electrons, not the NADH. Malate–aspartate shuttle (liver, heart, kidney): cytosolic NADH reduces oxaloacetate to malate (malate dehydrogenase); malate crosses; in the matrix it is oxidized back to oxaloacetate, making a matrix NADH worth 2.5 ATP; oxaloacetate returns as aspartate by transamination. Muscle and brain use the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle, which delivers the electrons as FADH2 (1.5 ATP each), giving about 30 ATP per glucose. Both shuttles and the full accounting: Section 14.5.

Health Career Connection

  • Respiratory therapy and nursing – why oxygen matters: O2 is the final electron acceptor; without it, electron transport stops and cells fall back to glycolysis alone (2 ATP instead of 32). This is the biochemistry behind hypoxia (Section 14.4).
  • Nursing – carbon monoxide and cyanide: CO binds hemoglobin so oxygen is not delivered; CO and cyanide also block cytochrome oxidase. Oxygen can displace CO from hemoglobin, but a blocked Complex IV needs an antidote that removes the cyanide (Section 14.4).
  • Nursing – thiamine: pyruvate dehydrogenase needs its thiamine cofactor; give thiamine with or before IV dextrose in at-risk patients (Section 14.1).
  • Nursing – newborn thermoregulation: brown fat uncouples electron transport from ATP synthesis and releases the energy as heat (Section 14.4).

Worked Examples

Example 1: ATP per acetyl CoA

Problem. How many ATP are made from one acetyl CoA going through the citric acid cycle and electron transport?

Solution. 3 NADH × 2.5 = 7.5; 1 FADH2 × 1.5 = 1.5; 1 GTP = 1. Total = 10 ATP.

Check: Two acetyl CoA per glucose give 20, which is the citric-acid-cycle line of the 32-ATP tally in Key Idea 3.

Example 2: Food energy

Problem. A snack bar has 20 g carbohydrate, 5 g protein, and 10 g fat. How many kcal does it contain?

Solution. 20(4) + 5(4) + 10(9) = 80 + 20 + 90 = 190 kcal. About half the energy comes from the fat.

Check: 35 g of food at 4–9 kcal/g must land between 140 and 315 kcal; 190 does.

Example 3: Classify a cycle step

Problem. For each conversion, give the reaction type, the enzyme class, and the coenzyme (if any): (a) succinate → fumarate; (b) fumarate → malate; (c) α-ketoglutarate → succinyl CoA; (d) succinyl CoA → succinate.

Solution. Look at what changed in the structure. (a) A C–C single bond became a C=C: an oxidation (2 H removed), so the enzyme is an oxidoreductase (succinate dehydrogenase) and the coenzyme is FAD → FADH2. (b) H and OH were added across the C=C: a hydration, no oxidation state change, so a lyase (fumarase) with no coenzyme. (c) A carbon was lost as CO2 and the remaining acid became a thioester: an oxidative decarboxylation by an oxidoreductase (α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase) using NAD+ → NADH and CoA–SH. (d) The thioester is broken and the energy is used to make GTP from GDP + Pi: a ligase (succinyl CoA synthetase), substrate-level phosphorylation.

Check. Count the carbons: (a), (b) and (d) keep 4 C; (c) goes 5 C → 4 C, matching the one CO2 released. Only (a) and (c) make a reduced coenzyme, and only (d) makes a nucleoside triphosphate directly.

Example 4: Predict the effect of cyanide

Problem. Cyanide binds Complex IV. Predict what happens to (a) the ratio NADH/NAD+ in the matrix, (b) the citric acid cycle, (c) ATP production, and (d) oxygen consumption, and (e) whether giving more oxygen alone would fix the problem.

Solution. (a) Electrons can no longer reach O2, so every carrier upstream stays reduced: NADH rises and NAD+ falls. (b) The three NAD+-dependent dehydrogenases run out of NAD+ (and NADH inhibits them allosterically), so the cycle stops. (c) No electron flow means no proton pumping, no gradient, and no ATP from ATP synthase; only glycolysis (2 ATP per glucose) remains. (d) Oxygen consumption falls to nearly zero even though blood oxygen is normal; venous blood stays bright red. (e) No. The problem is not oxygen delivery but the enzyme that uses it, so the antidote must remove the cyanide.

Check. Compare with an uncoupler, which has the opposite effect on oxygen use: oxygen consumption goes up while ATP goes down. A blocker stops flow; an uncoupler wastes it.

Example 5: Follow the electrons

Problem. (a) Arrange in the order they appear in electron transport: cytochrome c, FAD, coenzyme Q. (b) In the half-reaction FADH2 → FAD + 2 H+ + 2 e−, how many electrons are transferred, and is FADH2 oxidized or reduced? (c) Which complex receives those electrons, and why does this make FADH2 worth less ATP than NADH?

Solution. (a) FAD, coenzyme Q, cytochrome c. FADH2 hands its electrons to Complex II, Complex II passes them to CoQ, CoQ carries them to Complex III, and cytochrome c carries them from III to IV. (b) Two electrons; FADH2 loses hydrogen and electrons, so it is oxidized (LEO). (c) Complex II (succinate–coenzyme Q reductase), which does not pump protons. The electrons then pass only two pumps (III and IV) instead of three, so FADH2 yields 1.5 ATP rather than 2.5.

Check. The same order works for NADH if you replace FAD with NAD+ and Complex II with Complex I. In both cases the last stop is Complex IV, cytochrome c oxidase, where the electrons meet O2 and form water.

Example 6: Read the energy state

Problem. A muscle cell at rest has high ATP and high NADH. (a) Predict the activity of citrate synthase and isocitrate dehydrogenase, and the rate of electron transport. (b) The person starts sprinting. Which small molecule rises first, and what does it do to each of those three?

Solution. (a) High ATP inhibits citrate synthase; high NADH inhibits isocitrate dehydrogenase; with little ADP available, ATP synthase has nothing to phosphorylate, protons cannot return through F0, and electron transport slows. The cycle idles because the cell does not need ATP. (b) ADP rises as ATP is hydrolyzed for contraction. ADP activates both citrate synthase and isocitrate dehydrogenase, and the availability of ADP lets ATP synthase and the electron transport chain speed up; NADH is consumed by Complex I, relieving its inhibition of step 3. Oxygen consumption rises, which is why the sprinter breathes hard.

Check. Every regulator points the same way: signals of “energy spent” (ADP) turn the pathway on; signals of “energy stored” (ATP, NADH) turn it off. Compare glycolysis in Chapter 13, where ATP inhibits and ADP/AMP activate phosphofructokinase for the same reason.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Counting the cycle once per glucose. It turns twice (2 acetyl CoA).
  • Giving FADH2 the same value as NADH. It is 1.5, not 2.5.
  • Thinking the citric acid cycle uses O2 directly. It does not, but it stops without O2 because NAD+ and FAD are not regenerated.
  • Thinking CO2 is made in electron transport. All 6 CO2 come from pyruvate dehydrogenase and the cycle; electron transport makes H2O.
  • Calling succinate dehydrogenase a lyase because it makes a C=C. It removes two hydrogens, so it is an oxidoreductase (and it is Complex II). The lyase is fumarase, which adds water.
  • Mixing up a blocker and an uncoupler. Cyanide stops electron flow (O2 use falls); DNP lets electrons flow without making ATP (O2 use rises, heat is released).
  • Forgetting that oxaloacetate is regenerated. It is a catalyst, not a reactant that is used up, so the cycle needs only a trace of it to keep turning.
  • Putting the carriers in the wrong order. Electrons go NADH → I (or FADH2 → II) → CoQ → III → cytochrome c → IV → O2. Coenzyme Q comes before cytochrome c, and O2 is the acceptor at the very end, not NAD+ or FAD.
  • Choosing the coenzyme by habit instead of by structure. Ask what changed: 2° alcohol → ketone means NAD+; –CH2–CH2– → –CH=CH– means FAD; a thioester appearing or disappearing means coenzyme A.

Clinical Update: Rosa’s Story, Part 14 of 15

An evening walk

Rosa’s story for this chapter is on the Chapter 14 opening page. Quick recap for the questions below: Rosa walks after dinner, and her working muscles break down glucose through glycolysis, the citric acid cycle and the electron transport chain to make ATP; and metformin can rarely cause lactic acidosis, a build-up of lactate that is more likely when tissues get too little oxygen.

Case Question 14.1

In aerobic metabolism, what is the final electron acceptor of the electron transport chain, and what does it form?

Answer
Oxygen, O2. It accepts electrons and H+ to form water.

Case Question 14.2

When oxygen is low, why do cells convert pyruvate to lactate instead of sending it to the citric acid cycle?

Answer
Without enough O2, the electron transport chain cannot reoxidize NADH. Reducing pyruvate to lactate regenerates NAD+, which glycolysis needs in order to keep making a small amount of ATP. Lactate (and acid) build up as a result.

Case Question 14.3

About how many ATP does one glucose yield with oxygen compared with glycolysis alone?

Answer
Roughly 30–32 ATP per glucose with complete aerobic oxidation, compared with a net of 2 ATP from glycolysis alone.

In step 2 of the cycle, the 3° alcohol of citrate becomes the 2° alcohol of isocitrate. Name the overall reaction type, the two reactions it is “deconstructed” into, the enzyme class, and the coenzyme (if any).

When ATP levels are high, which cycle enzyme is inhibited, and when ADP is high, which two enzymes are activated?
