Recall that ammonia (NH3) acts as a base because the nitrogen atom has a lone pair of electrons that can accept a proton. Amines also have a lone electron pair on their nitrogen atoms and can accept a proton from water to form substituted ammonium (NH4+) ions and hydroxide (OH−) ions:

As a specific example, methylamine reacts with water to form the methylammonium ion and the OH− ion.

Nearly all amines, including those that are not very soluble in water, will react with strong acids to form salts soluble in water.

Amine salts are named like other salts: the name of the cation is followed by the name of the anion.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\)

What are the formulas of the acid and base that react to form [CH3NH2CH2CH3]+CH3COO−?

Solution

The cation has two groups—methyl and ethyl—attached to the nitrogen atom. It comes from ethylmethylamine (CH3NHCH2CH3). The anion is the acetate ion. It comes from acetic acid (CH3COOH).

To Your Health: Amine Salts as Drugs

Salts of aniline are properly named as anilinium compounds, but an older system, still in use for naming drugs, identifies the salt of aniline and hydrochloric acid as “aniline hydrochloride.” These compounds are ionic—they are salts—and the properties of the compounds (solubility, for example) are those characteristic of salts. Many drugs that are amines are converted to hydrochloride salts to increase their solubility in aqueous solution.

Heterocyclic Amines

Looking back at the various cyclic hydrocarbons discussed previously, we see that all the atoms in the rings of these compounds are carbon atoms. In other cyclic compounds, called heterocyclic compounds (Greek heteros, meaning “other”), nitrogen, oxygen, sulfur, or some other atom is incorporated in the ring. Many heterocyclic compounds are important in medicine and biochemistry. Some compose part of the structure of the nucleic acids, which in turn compose the genetic material of cells and direct protein synthesis.

Many heterocyclic amines occur naturally in plants. Like other amines, these compounds are basic. Such a compound is an alkaloid, a name that means “like alkalis.” Many alkaloids are physiologically active, including the familiar drugs caffeine, nicotine, and cocaine.

To Your Health: Three Well-Known Alkaloids

Caffeine is a stimulant found in coffee, tea, and some soft drinks. It works mainly by blocking the receptors for adenosine, a heterocyclic base that acts as a neurotransmitter, a substance that carries messages across a tiny gap (synapse) from one nerve cell (neuron) to another cell. The effective dose of caffeine is about 200 mg, corresponding to about two cups of strong coffee or tea.

Nicotine acts as a stimulant by a different mechanism; it probably mimics the action of the neurotransmitter acetylcholine. People ingest this drug by smoking or chewing tobacco. Its stimulant effect seems transient, as this initial response is followed by depression. Nicotine is toxic. The lethal dose for an adult was long quoted as about 50 mg, but current estimates are much higher, roughly 0.5 to 1 g. Young children are at real risk: swallowing liquid nicotine from e-cigarette refills, or nicotine pouches or gum, can cause serious poisoning, so these products must be stored out of reach. Nicotine has also been used in agriculture as a contact insecticide.

Cocaine acts as a stimulant by preventing nerve cells from taking up dopamine, another neurotransmitter, from the synapse. High levels of dopamine are therefore available to stimulate the pleasure centers of the brain. The enhancement of dopamine action is thought to be responsible for cocaine’s “high” and its addictive properties. After the binge, dopamine is depleted in less than an hour. This leaves the user in a pleasureless state and (often) craving more cocaine.

Cocaine is used as the salt cocaine hydrochloride and in the form of broken lumps of the free (unneutralized) base, which is called crack cocaine.

\[\underbrace{\ce{C17H21O4N}}_{\text{cocaine (freebase)}} + \ce{HCl ->} \underbrace{\ce{C17H21O4NH^{+}Cl^{-}}}_{\text{cocaine hydrochloride}} \nonumber \]

Because it is soluble in water, cocaine hydrochloride is readily absorbed through the watery mucous membranes of the nose when it is snorted. Crack cocaine is more volatile than cocaine hydrochloride. It vaporizes at the temperature of a burning cigarette. When smoked, cocaine reaches the brain in 15 s.

Valley Connection: An Acid–Base Reaction in the Sky

Amines are weak bases because the lone pair on nitrogen can accept a proton (H+). Their parent compound, ammonia (NH3), does the same thing, and in the San Joaquin Valley that simple reaction happens on a huge scale in the air we breathe.

Where the base comes from. The Valley releases about 325 tons of ammonia per day. Confined animal facilities are the biggest source (about 187 tons per day), and dairy cattle account for about two-thirds of that; farm fertilizers add about 111 tons per day.1

Where the acid comes from. Engines and other combustion sources release nitrogen oxides (NOx).2 In the air, NO2 reacts with oxidizing species to form nitric acid (HNO3).1

Base + acid → ammonium salt. Just as an amine reacts with an acid to form an ammonium salt, ammonia reacts with nitric acid:

NH3(g) + HNO3(g) → NH4NO3(s)

The product, ammonium nitrate, is an ionic solid that forms as tiny particles. It is the largest single part of the Valley’s fine-particle pollution (PM2.5), about 40% of PM2.5 mass over a year and 55–60% on peak days.3 Ammonium nitrate is an especially large part of the Valley’s wintertime PM2.5.4 Winter measurements show Valley air usually has more ammonia than the reaction needs,1 so nitric acid is the limiting reactant, and air regulators have concluded that cutting NOx is the more effective way to reduce ammonium nitrate.1

In the clinic. PM2.5 particles are about 30 times smaller than the width of a human hair and can travel deep into the lungs; some can even reach the bloodstream.5 Particle pollution is linked to aggravated asthma, decreased lung function, irregular heartbeat, heart attacks, and early death in people with heart or lung disease.6 Children, older adults, and people with heart or lung disease are most at risk.6

Think about it: Methylamine (CH3NH2) is a simple amine. Predict the product if methylamine reacts with nitric acid, and name the type of compound formed.

Answer
CH3NH2 + HNO3 → CH3NH3+ NO3−, methylammonium nitrate. The lone pair on the amine nitrogen accepts H+ from the acid, forming an ammonium salt, exactly like ammonia forming ammonium nitrate.

Summary

Amines are bases; they react with acids to form salts. Salts of aniline are properly named as anilinium compounds, but an older system is used to name drugs: the salts of amine drugs and hydrochloric acid are called “hydrochlorides.” Heterocyclic amines are cyclic compounds with one or more nitrogen atoms in the ring.

Lecture Connection

In lecture this is the reaction of the chapter: the lone pair on nitrogen picks up H+. Write it with water, with HCl, and with a carboxylic acid, name the ammonium salt, and then explain why nearly every amine drug on a hospital shelf is sold as a "hydrochloride."

Writing the reactions

Amines are proton (H+) acceptors, which makes them Brønsted–Lowry bases; they accept the proton by donating the lone pair on N, which also makes them Lewis bases. Either way, the lone pair on N picks up an H+:

  • With water: RNH2 + H2O \(\rightleftharpoons\) RNH3+ + OH− (a weak base; the solution is basic)
  • With an acid: RNH2 + HCl → RNH3+Cl− (an ammonium salt; this is a neutralization, and no water is formed, unlike NaOH + HCl)
  • With a carboxylic acid: RNH2 + R′COOH → RNH3+ R′COO− (an ammonium carboxylate salt; at room temperature this salt is all that forms, see Section 7.6)

Amines are weak bases compared with inorganic bases such as NaOH: in water only a small fraction of the molecules are protonated at any moment, so the equilibrium arrow points both ways. Among organic compounds, though, amines are the strongest bases you will meet. At blood pH 7.4 most molecules of a typical amine drug exist as the ammonium ion. Aromatic amines such as aniline are much weaker bases because the ring shares the lone pair.

Naming the ions and salts. Replace -amine with -ammonium for the cation, then combine the ion names for the salt: methylamine gives the methylammonium ion and methylammonium chloride; aniline gives anilinium chloride; trimethylamine gives trimethylammonium acetate with acetic acid.

Why drugs are sold as ammonium salts

Ammonium salts are ionic, odorless solids that dissolve well in water and body fluids. A water-insoluble amine is converted to a water-soluble ammonium salt simply by treating it with acid. That is why many drug labels say "hydrochloride" (HCl): lidocaine hydrochloride in a dental cartridge and diphenhydramine hydrochloride (Benadryl) in an allergy capsule are the amine drugs converted to their ammonium chloride salts, which dissolve for injection or for absorption in the gut and are carried in the bloodstream. Quaternary ammonium salts are used as antiseptics: benzalkonium chloride is a disinfectant in surgical scrubs, instrument-storage solutions, and some mouthwashes (its bitter taste is also used to discourage nail biting). The reaction runs backward just as easily: adding a strong base to an ammonium salt removes the H+ and frees the amine.

RNH3+Cl− + NaOH → RNH2 + H2O + NaCl

Practice: which is more soluble in water, phenylephrine (a decongestant with a 2° amine) or phenylephrine hydrochloride? The salt, every time.

Example 1: Amine + acid

Problem. Write the reaction of trimethylamine (the compound responsible for the smell of fish) with HCl. Why does lemon juice reduce fish odor?

Solution

(CH3)3N + HCl → (CH3)3NH+Cl− (trimethylammonium chloride)

The acid in lemon juice turns the volatile amine into a nonvolatile ionic salt, so it no longer evaporates into your nose.

Check. The nitrogen gained one H and one + charge; the Cl− balances it. No N–H was needed, so a 3° amine works fine here.

Example 2: A drug as its hydrochloride salt

Problem. Diphenhydramine is a 3° amine, (C6H5)2CHOCH2CH2N(CH3)2. Write the equation that forms diphenhydramine hydrochloride, explain why the salt is used in capsules, and show how to get the free amine back.

Solution. Abbreviate the drug as RN(CH3)2, where R = (C6H5)2CHOCH2CH2–. The lone pair on N accepts H+ from HCl:

RN(CH3)2 + HCl → RNH+(CH3)2 Cl− (diphenhydramine hydrochloride)

The free amine, with two phenyl rings, is an oily, poorly water-soluble liquid. The ammonium chloride is an ionic solid that dissolves in water and gastric fluid, so it can be weighed into a capsule and absorbed reliably. A strong base reverses the reaction: RNH+(CH3)2 Cl− + NaOH → RN(CH3)2 + H2O + NaCl.

Check. A 3° amine can be protonated (it has a lone pair) but cannot form an amide (no N–H). Only the nitrogen picks up H+; the ether oxygen is far too weak a base.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Most amine drugs are supplied as hydrochloride salts (lidocaine HCl, diphenhydramine HCl, dopamine HCl): the ammonium salt dissolves in water for IV infusion and in gastric fluid for oral absorption, while the free amine often does not. The catecholamines epinephrine, norepinephrine, and dopamine, phenol-containing amines, are IV drugs for shock and cardiac arrest (their structures are drawn in Section 7.11). Antihistamines are amines shaped to block histamine. When you reconstitute or dilute one of these drugs, you are relying on the ionic salt's solubility; mixing it with an alkaline solution can push the equilibrium back toward the poorly soluble free amine.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Local anesthetics and infection. Lidocaine and mepivacaine carry a 3° amine group (articaine a 2° amine). The cartridge solution is acidic (about pH 3.5–6.5, lowest in cartridges that contain epinephrine) so the drug stays as its water-soluble hydrochloride salt, R3NH+Cl−. After injection, tissue buffers raise the pH toward 7.4 and the equilibrium R3NH+ \(\rightleftharpoons\) R3N + H+ shifts toward the neutral amine, the only form nonpolar enough to cross the nerve membrane. In an abscess or inflamed tissue the pH is lower, more drug stays as the charged ion, and less reaches the nerve, so anesthesia is slower and weaker. Cartridges with epinephrine (a catecholamine vasoconstrictor) are the most acidic, to protect its phenol groups from oxidation.

Ammonium ions in oral care. Chlorhexidine, the antiseptic in prescription mouth rinse, is a bis-biguanide, a molecule rich in amine-like nitrogens that are protonated at mouth pH; the cation binds the negatively charged surfaces of bacteria, plaque biofilm, and enamel and keeps working for hours (substantivity). Amine fluorides (olaflur) are ammonium salts with a long nonpolar tail, so they act as surfactants that spread across the tooth surface while delivering F−. Cetylpyridinium chloride (CPC) in many mouthwashes is a quaternary ammonium salt. In each case the antibacterial effect comes from the positively charged ammonium ion, not from the fluoride or chloride.
