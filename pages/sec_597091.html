Learning Objectives
  • Identify the general structure for an alcohol.
  • Identify the structural feature that classifies alcohols as primary, secondary, or tertiary.
  • Name alcohols with both common names and IUPAC names

An alcohol is an organic compound with a hydroxyl (OH) functional group on an aliphatic carbon atom. Because OH is the functional group of all alcohols, we often represent alcohols by the general formula ROH, where R is an alkyl group. Alcohols are common in nature. Most people are familiar with ethyl alcohol (ethanol), the active ingredient in alcoholic beverages, but this compound is only one of a family of organic compounds known as alcohols. The family also includes such familiar substances as cholesterol and the carbohydrates. Methanol (CH3OH) and ethanol (CH3CH2OH) are the first two members of the homologous series of alcohols.

Nomenclature of Alcohols

Alcohols with one to four carbon atoms are frequently called by common names, in which the name of the alkyl group is followed by the word alcohol:

 

According to the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC), alcohols are named by changing the ending of the parent alkane name to -ol. Here are some basic IUPAC rules for naming alcohols:

  1. The longest continuous chain (LCC) of carbon atoms containing the OH group is taken as the parent compound—an alkane with the same number of carbon atoms. The chain is numbered from the end nearest the OH group.
  2. The number that indicates the position of the OH group is prefixed to the name of the parent hydrocarbon, and the -e ending of the parent alkane is replaced by the suffix -ol. (In cyclic alcohols, the carbon atom bearing the OH group is designated C1, but the 1 is not used in the name.) Substituents are named and numbered as in alkanes.
  3. If more than one OH group appears in the same molecule (polyhydroxy alcohols), suffixes such as -diol and -triol are used. In these cases, the -e ending of the parent alkane is retained.

Example \(\PageIndex{1}\)

Give the IUPAC name for each compound.

  • HOCH2CH2CH2CH2CH2OH
Solution
  1. Ten carbon atoms in the LCC makes the compound a derivative of decane (rule 1), and the OH on the third carbon atom makes it a 3-decanol (rule 2).

    The carbon atoms are numbered from the end closest to the OH group. That fixes the two methyl (CH3) groups at the sixth and eighth positions. The name is 6,8-dimethyl-3-decanol (not 3,5-dimethyl-8-decanol).

  2. Five carbon atoms in the LCC make the compound a derivative of pentane. Two OH groups on the first and fifth carbon atoms make the compound a diol and give the name 1,5-pentanediol (rule 3).

Classification of Alcohols

Some of the properties of alcohols depend on the number of carbon atoms attached to the specific carbon atom that is attached to the OH group. Alcohols can be grouped into three classes on this basis.

  • A primary (1°) alcohol is one in which the carbon atom (in red) with the OH group is attached to one other carbon atom (in blue). Its general formula is RCH2OH.

  • A secondary (2°) alcohol is one in which the carbon atom (in red) with the OH group is attached to two other carbon atoms (in blue). Its general formula is R2CHOH.

  • A tertiary (3°) alcohol is one in which the carbon atom (in red) with the OH group is attached to three other carbon atoms (in blue). Its general formula is R3COH.

Table \(\PageIndex{1}\) names and classifies some of the simpler alcohols. Some of the common names reflect a compound’s classification as secondary (sec-) or tertiary (tert-). These designations are not used in the IUPAC nomenclature system for alcohols. Summary

In the IUPAC system, alcohols are named by changing the ending of the parent alkane name to -ol. Alcohols are classified according to the number of carbon atoms attached to the carbon atom that is attached to the OH group.

Lecture Connection

Two skills from this section show up on every exam: naming an alcohol from its structure (and drawing it from its name), and classifying the alcohol as 1°, 2° or 3°. Classify first; the class decides what the alcohol does in the oxidation reaction of Section 4.5.

The naming checklist

Use the same checklist every time:

  1. Find the longest carbon chain that contains the carbon bonded to the OH. A longer chain that does not include the OH carbon is not the parent.
  2. Decide whether that chain is an alkane, alkene or alkyne, then remove the final -e and add -ol.
  3. Number the chain to give the OH carbon the lower number.
  4. Name and number the other substituents (alkyl groups and halogens) as prefixes, in alphabetical order.

Examples: CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3 is 2-butanol (butan-2-ol); CH3CH(OH)CH2CHClCH3 is 4-chloro-2-pentanol. Two OH groups give a -diol, and here the -e stays: 1,2-ethanediol (ethylene glycol). For a cycloalkanol (a cyclic alcohol) the OH carbon is automatically C1 and the "1-" is usually omitted; number around the ring to give the next substituent the lower number: 3-methylcyclohexanol, 4-propylcyclohexanol.

Practice (lecture style): CH3CH(OH)CH2CH(CH3)CH3. Longest chain containing the OH carbon: five carbons, so the parent is pentanol; the remaining CH3 is a methyl substituent. Number from the end nearer the OH: OH on C2, methyl on C4. Name: 4-methyl-2-pentanol. Check that numbering from the other end would give 2-methyl-4-pentanol; the OH wins the lower number.

Branched alkyl substituents: n-, iso-, sec-, tert-

When a substituent is attached through a middle carbon, you will see common prefixes instead of a systematic name: n- (normal, unbranched), iso- (a Y-shaped end, two CH3 groups on the same carbon), sec- (attached through a 2° carbon) and tert- (attached through a 3° carbon). Isopropyl alcohol (2-propanol) and tert-butyl alcohol (2-methyl-2-propanol) are the two you will hear most. As the source table shows, these prefixes belong to common names only; an IUPAC name uses numbers instead.

Classifying alcohols: count the carbons on the OH carbon

Professor Tip

Go to the carbon the alcohol is connected to, then count the C–C bonds on that carbon. 1 carbon = 1°, 2 carbons = 2°, 3 carbons = 3°. Do not count the oxygen, and do not count carbons anywhere else in the molecule.

Example: Name from structure, structure from name

Problem. (a) Name a cyclopentane ring with an OH on one carbon and an ethyl group two carbons away. (b) Draw the condensed structural formula of 2,2-dimethyl-1-propanol and classify it.

Solution. (a) A cyclic alcohol is a cycloalkanol; the OH carbon is C1 and the "1-" is omitted. Count around the ring the short way to the ethyl group: C3. 3-ethylcyclopentanol. (b) "Propanol" means a three-carbon chain with the OH on C1; "2,2-dimethyl" puts two CH3 groups on C2: HOCH2C(CH3)3. The OH carbon is bonded to only one other carbon, so it is a 1° alcohol (its common name is neopentyl alcohol).

Check. In (a), going the long way around the ring would give 4-ethyl; 3 is lower. In (b), the alcohol is 1° even though the neighboring carbon is quaternary; only the OH carbon's own C–C bonds count.

Keep an eye out for isomers

Alcohols with three or more carbons have constitutional isomers, and the lecture sorts them into three kinds:

  • Positional: same chain, OH in a different place. 1-Butanol and 2-butanol; 1-pentanol and 2-pentanol.
  • Skeletal: different carbon-chain connectivity. 2-Methyl-1-propanol is a skeletal isomer of both butanols; 1-pentanol and 3-methyl-1-butanol are another pair.
  • Functional group: same formula, different functional group. An alcohol and an ether with the same formula: ethanol and dimethyl ether are both C2H6O. The ethers have no O–H, so they boil far lower than the alcohols and have different chemistry (no oxidation, no dehydration). Section 4.8 covers the ethers themselves.
