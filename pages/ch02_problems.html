Problem 2.57

How many secondary (2°) carbons are in 3-ethyl-2,2-dimethylpentane?

  1. 0
  2. 1
  3. 2
  4. 3
Answer
C. Two. C4 of the chain is bonded to C3 and C5, and the CH2 of the ethyl group is bonded to C3 and to its own CH3. The five CH3 carbons are 1°, C3 is 3°, and C2 is 4°.

Problem 2.58

Which equation shows an elimination reaction?

  1. CH3CH3 + Br2 → CH3CH2Br + HBr
  2. CH3CH2OH → CH2=CH2 + H2O
  3. CH2=CH2 + HBr → CH3CH2Br
  4. C3H8 + 5 O2 → 3 CO2 + 4 H2O
Answer
B. One reactant becomes two products and a double bond is created, the signature of elimination. (A) is a substitution (Br trades places with H), (C) is an addition (two reactants become one and the double bond is used up), and (D) is combustion.

Problem 2.59

Which pair are positional isomers?

  1. hexane and 2-methylpentane
  2. 1-bromopentane and 2-bromopentane
  3. butane and cyclobutane
  4. CH3CH(CH3)CH2CH3 and CH3CH2CH(CH3)CH3
Answer
B. Both are C5H11Br with the same pentane skeleton; only the position of the bromine differs. (A) are skeletal isomers (different carbon framework), (C) are not isomers at all (C4H10 vs C4H8), and (D) are the same compound, 2-methylbutane, written in two directions.

Problem 2.60

Which statement about cyclohexane is correct?

  1. It is a flat hexagon with 120° bond angles, just as it is drawn.
  2. It is puckered (not flat) so that every bond angle is close to 109.5°.
  3. Its C–C bonds cannot rotate at all, so it has only one possible shape.
  4. It is unsaturated because it has fewer hydrogens than hexane.
Answer
B. Each ring carbon has four single bonds and wants tetrahedral angles, which a flat hexagon cannot provide; puckering the ring gives angles near 109.5°. (C) overstates it: the ring bonds cannot rotate freely, but the ring can still flex. (D) is wrong because the two "missing" hydrogens are due to the ring, not a double bond; cyclohexane is saturated.

Problem 2.61

The table lists boiling points of the three C5H12 isomers.

Boiling points of the three C5H12 isomers
IsomerNumber of branchesBoiling point (°C)
pentane036
2-methylbutane128
2,2-dimethylpropane210

(a) What kind of isomers are these? (b) All three have the same number of electrons. What is the trend in the data, and why does it occur? (c) Predict whether 2,2-dimethylbutane boils above or below hexane (69 °C).

Answer
(a) Skeletal (constitutional) isomers: same formula, different carbon framework. (b) Boiling point falls as branching increases. With equal electron counts the dispersion forces depend on how much surface area neighboring molecules can press together; a branched, nearly spherical molecule touches its neighbors over less area, so less energy is needed to pull the molecules apart. (c) Below hexane: 2,2-dimethylbutane is a branched C6H14 isomer, so its boiling point (about 50 °C) is lower than that of the straight chain.

Problem 2.62

Analyze the data: butane (C4H10) boils at −1 °C, 1-chlorobutane (C4H9Cl) at 78 °C, and 1-bromobutane (C4H9Br) at 102 °C. (a) Which intermolecular force is present in all three? (b) Which force do the two haloalkanes have that butane lacks? (c) Why does the bromo compound boil higher than the chloro compound even though C–Cl is the more polar bond?

Answer
(a) Dispersion forces: every molecule has them. (b) Dipole–dipole attraction, because the C–Cl and C–Br bonds are polar and the molecules have a permanent dipole; butane is nonpolar. (c) Bromine has far more electrons than chlorine (35 vs 17), so 1-bromobutane has much stronger dispersion forces. For haloalkanes the extra electrons outweigh the small difference in bond polarity, which is why boiling points rise down the halogen group. None of the three can hydrogen bond.

Problem 2.63

Compare and predict: ethane gives only one monochloro product (chloroethane), while propane gives two (1-chloropropane and 2-chloropropane). (a) Explain the difference in terms of kinds of hydrogen. (b) Predict how many monochloro products 2,2-dimethylpropane, C(CH3)4, can give. (c) Predict how many butane can give, and name them.

Answer
(a) All six hydrogens of ethane are equivalent (every one is on a 1° carbon in the same environment), so replacing any of them gives the same product. Propane has two kinds: six 1° hydrogens on the end carbons and two 2° hydrogens on the middle carbon, so there are two positional isomers. (b) One. All twelve hydrogens of 2,2-dimethylpropane are on identical CH3 groups, so the only product is 1-chloro-2,2-dimethylpropane. (c) Two: the end CH3 hydrogens give 1-chlorobutane and the inner CH2 hydrogens give 2-chlorobutane. (Replacing a hydrogen on C3 gives 2-chlorobutane again, numbered from the other end.)

Problem 2.64

Compare and contrast: a student draws butane twice, once as a straight zig-zag and once with the chain bent into a U-shape, and also draws 2-methylpropane. (a) Which drawings represent the same compound, and what is the relationship between them? (b) Which represent constitutional isomers? (c) What would the student have to do to the molecule to convert one of the butane drawings into 2-methylpropane?

Answer
(a) The two butane drawings are the same compound in two conformations (shapes reached by rotation about a single bond); they differ only by rotation about the C2–C3 single bond, and both are named butane. (b) Butane and 2-methylpropane are skeletal isomers: both are C4H10, but one is a four-carbon chain and the other a three-carbon chain with a branch. (c) Rotation is not enough; a C–C bond would have to be broken and re-formed in a new place. That is the test: conformations interconvert by rotation, isomers only by breaking bonds.

More Practice: Clinical and Integrative

Problem 2.65

Nursing: isoflurane, an inhaled anesthetic, has the formula C3H2ClF5O and the structure CF3CHCl–O–CHF2. It boils at 49 °C. (a) Is the carbon skeleton saturated or unsaturated? (b) Which intermolecular forces does isoflurane have, and which one is it missing that water and alcohols have? (c) Use (b) to explain why a liquid anesthetic can be delivered as a gas from a vaporizer at room temperature.

Answer
(a) Saturated: every carbon has four single bonds (to C, H, F, Cl, or O); there is no C=C. (b) Dispersion forces, plus dipole–dipole attraction from its polar C–F, C–Cl, and C–O bonds. It has no O–H or N–H, so it cannot hydrogen bond. (c) Without hydrogen bonding and with a small molecule, the attractions between isoflurane molecules are weak, so it boils at only 49 °C and evaporates readily even below that temperature. The vaporizer simply passes the carrier gas over the liquid, picking up anesthetic vapor that the patient inhales.

Problem 2.66

Dental hygiene: before in-office tooth bleaching, the hygienist coats the patient's lips and gingiva (gum tissue) with petroleum jelly, a semisolid mixture of long-chain alkanes. The bleaching gel is hydrogen peroxide dissolved in water. (a) Classify petroleum jelly as polar or nonpolar and name its only intermolecular force. (b) Explain, using intermolecular forces, why the water-based gel cannot soak through the petroleum jelly to reach the tissue. (c) Why is petroleum jelly a semisolid at room temperature while pentane, also an alkane, is a thin liquid?

Answer
(a) Nonpolar; dispersion forces only. (b) Water molecules hydrogen bond strongly to one another and to hydrogen peroxide. To push into the alkane layer they would have to give up those hydrogen bonds in exchange for weak dispersion contacts with alkane chains, which is unfavorable, so the aqueous gel stays on top of the barrier ("like dissolves like"). (c) Petroleum jelly's chains are roughly C25 and longer, so each molecule has many more electrons and far more surface contact with its neighbors than pentane (C5). The stronger dispersion forces make it thick and semisolid instead of a free-flowing liquid.

Problem 2.67

Nursing: a patient receiving oxygen by nasal cannula asks for a petroleum-based lip balm. Hospital policy calls for a water-based product instead. (a) Which reaction type does the policy guard against? (b) Write a balanced equation for the complete combustion of hexadecane, C16H34, a representative component of petroleum jelly. (c) Why does a higher oxygen concentration make this a greater concern than in ordinary air?

Answer
(a) Combustion, the reaction of an alkane with oxygen to give carbon dioxide and water with a large release of heat. (b) 2 C16H34 + 49 O2 → 32 CO2 + 34 H2O. Check: 32 C, 68 H, and 98 O on each side. (c) Oxygen is a reactant in the equation. Raising its concentration near the face makes any flammable material, including a thin film of alkane, ignite more easily and burn faster, so the fuel is removed by switching to a water-based product that cannot burn.

Problem 2.68

Integrative (Chapters 1 and 2): consider 2-chloro-3-methylbutane. (a) Write its condensed structural formula and molecular formula. (b) Classify the carbon bearing the chlorine and the carbon bearing the methyl group as 1°, 2°, 3°, or 4°. (c) What is the shape around the carbon bearing Cl, and what is its approximate bond angle? (d) Is the C–Cl bond polar? Is the molecule polar? Which intermolecular forces does it have?

Answer
(a) CH3CHClCH(CH3)CH3; C5H11Cl (the C5H12 skeleton with one H replaced by Cl). (b) C2 (the one with Cl) is bonded to C1 and C3, so it is 2°; C3 is bonded to C2, C4, and the methyl carbon, so it is 3°. Remember to count carbons, not hydrogens. (c) Tetrahedral, about 109.5°: four single bonds and no lone pairs. (d) Yes, C–Cl is polar because chlorine is much more electronegative than carbon. The molecule is polar overall, since the single C–Cl dipole is not canceled by any other polar bond. It has dispersion forces and dipole–dipole attraction but no hydrogen bonding.

Problem 2.69

Integrative (looking ahead to Chapter 4): the carbon-classification rule and the three reaction types carry over to alcohols. (a) In 2-methyl-2-propanol, (CH3)3COH, classify the carbon that carries the OH group. (b) Heating an alcohol with acid removes H and OH from adjacent carbons to form an alkene and water. Which of the three reaction types is this? (c) Compare it with the halogenation of an alkane: in which reaction does the number of bonds to carbon stay the same, and in which does a multiple bond form?

Answer
(a) Tertiary (3°): the carbon with the OH is bonded to three other carbons, exactly as a 3° carbon in an alkane. The rule counts carbon neighbors, so replacing one of its hydrogens with OH does not change the classification. (b) Elimination: one reactant splits into two products and a C=C double bond forms. (c) In halogenation (a substitution) the carbon simply trades an H for a Cl, so the count of bonds to carbon is unchanged and no multiple bond appears. In the alcohol reaction (an elimination) a double bond forms between the two carbons that lost H and OH.

Problem 2.70

Dissect the name 5-chloro-3-ethyl-2,2-dimethyloctane. How many carbon atoms does the molecule contain in total?

  1. 8
  2. 10
  3. 11
  4. 12
Answer

Problem 2.71

Draw (condensed) and name every constitutional isomer of C3H6Cl2. How many are there?

Answer
Four. Put both chlorines on the same carbon or on different carbons of the three-carbon chain: CHCl2CH2CH3 (1,1-dichloropropane); CH3CCl2CH3 (2,2-dichloropropane); CH2ClCHClCH3 (1,2-dichloropropane); CH2ClCH2CH2Cl (1,3-dichloropropane). "2,3-dichloropropane" is 1,2-dichloropropane numbered from the wrong end, and a three-carbon chain cannot be branched, so there are no skeletal isomers; all four are positional isomers of one another.

Problem 2.72

Use the line structure below to answer the questions.

(a) How many hydrogen atoms does the molecule contain? (b) Give its molecular formula and IUPAC name. (c) Classify as 1°, 2°, 3°, or 4° the carbon bearing the Cl, the carbon bearing the methyl branch, the CH2 carbon, and the methyl branch carbon itself.

Answer
(a) Count carbon by carbon along the chain: CH3 (3), CHCl (1), CH (1), CH2 (2), CH3 (3), plus the branch CH3 (3) = 13 hydrogens. (b) Six carbons: C6H13Cl, which is C6H14 with one H replaced by Cl. Name: the longest chain is five carbons, numbered from the end nearer the chlorine (first substituent on C2 rather than C3): 2-chloro-3-methylpentane. (c) The carbon bearing Cl is bonded to two carbons (C1 and C3): 2°. The carbon bearing the methyl is bonded to three carbons (C2, C4, and the branch): 3°. The CH2 (C4) is bonded to C3 and C5: 2°. The branch methyl is bonded to only one carbon: 1°. Remember to count carbon neighbors, not hydrogens: the Cl does not change the classification.

Problem 2.73

Match each description of an alkyl group to its common name: (1) three carbons attached through the middle carbon (a Y or triangle shape); (2) four carbons attached through a carbon that carries three methyl groups; (3) four carbons attached through a CH that has a methyl on one side and an ethyl on the other; (4) four carbons attached through the stem of a Y, with the branch one carbon away from the attachment point; (5) three carbons in a straight line attached at the end. Problem 2.74

Which molecular formula could belong to an open-chain (acyclic) saturated hydrocarbon?

  1. C7H12
  2. C7H14
  3. C7H16
  4. C7H18
Answer
C. An acyclic alkane fits CnH2n+2; for n = 7 that is C7H16. C7H14 is two hydrogens short, so it must contain a ring (cycloheptane or methylcyclohexane) or a double bond (a heptene); C7H12 is four short (an alkyne, a diene, or a ring plus a double bond). C7H18 has more hydrogens than seven carbons can hold.

Problem 2.75

True or false? (If false, correct it.) "2-Ethylpentane" is an acceptable IUPAC name.

Answer
False. An ethyl group on C2 extends the chain: counting through the ethyl gives a six-carbon chain with a methyl on C3, so the compound is 3-methylhexane. The longest branch that can ever sit on C2 is a methyl.

Problem 2.76

(a) Can an alkyl branch be located on carbon 1 of a parent chain? Explain. (b) What is the longest alkyl branch that can be located on carbon 2? (c) What is the longest alkyl branch that can be located on carbon 3?

Answer
(a) No. A carbon group attached to the end carbon simply continues the chain, so the "branch" becomes part of a longer parent; "1-methylbutane" is really pentane. (b) A methyl. An ethyl on C2 would give a path through the branch one carbon longer than the chain on that side, so the chain would be re-drawn through it (see the previous problem). (c) An ethyl. On C3 there are two chain carbons to one side, so a two-carbon branch ties but does not beat the chain, while a propyl would win and become the parent.

Problem 2.77

Three alkanes: propane (C3H8), decane (C10H22), and triacontane (C30H62). (a) Predict the physical state of each at room temperature and give one typical use that follows from that state. (b) Which property do all three share that makes them useful as fuels but hazardous to store? (c) Which of the three has the highest melting point, and why?

Answer
(a) Propane is a gas (fuel for grills and heating, stored under pressure); decane is a liquid (a component of gasoline and kerosene, also a solvent); triacontane is a waxy solid (paraffin wax for candles, coatings, and dental wax). (b) Flammability: all alkanes burn in oxygen to CO2 and water, releasing a great deal of heat. (c) Triacontane. Melting point rises with chain length because a larger molecule has more electrons and more surface contact, so stronger dispersion forces hold the solid together.

Problem 2.78

Convert the condensed formula (CH3)2CHCH2CH(CH3)CH2CH3 into a line structure (describe the zigzag and where its branches are), then name the compound.

Answer

Cumulative Problem 2.1 (Chapters 1 and 2)

Pentane, 2-methylbutane and 2,2-dimethylpropane all have the formula C5H12. Which boils at the highest temperature, and which intermolecular force decides it?

  1. pentane, because its straight chain gives the largest area for dispersion forces
  2. 2,2-dimethylpropane, because branching makes it more polar
  3. 2-methylbutane, because it can hydrogen bond
  4. they boil at the same temperature because they have the same mass
Answer
A. Alkanes are nonpolar, so the only attraction between molecules is the London dispersion force (Chapter 1), and it grows with the area of contact between molecules. The unbranched chain of pentane (bp 36 °C) has more contact than the compact, nearly spherical 2,2-dimethylpropane (bp 10 °C). Same mass, same formula, different shape: that is what the Chapter 2 isomers do to a Chapter 1 property.

Cumulative Problem 2.2 (Chapters 1 and 2)

Draw the Lewis structure of propane, CH3CH2CH3. (a) What is the shape around each carbon? (b) Each C–H bond has a small dipole; is the molecule polar? (c) Will propane dissolve in water?

Answer
(a) Every carbon has four single bonds and no lone pairs, so each one is tetrahedral with bond angles near 109.5° (Chapter 1, VSEPR). (b) No. C and H differ by only 0.4 in electronegativity, and the tetrahedral C–H dipoles point in all directions and cancel, so propane is nonpolar. (c) No. A nonpolar molecule cannot hydrogen bond with water, so it does not dissolve (“like dissolves like”). The same reasoning will tell you which part of every later molecule, from fatty acids to proteins, avoids water.

Cumulative Problem 2.3 (Chapter 2)

How many constitutional isomers have the formula C4H10? Draw them, name them, and predict which has the lower boiling point.

Answer
Two: butane, CH3CH2CH2CH3, and 2-methylpropane, (CH3)3CH (isobutane). The branched isomer has less surface contact, weaker dispersion forces and the lower boiling point (−12 °C versus −0.5 °C for butane). Isobutane is the propellant in many aerosol cans; butane is the fuel in lighters.

Cumulative Problem 2.4 (Chapters 1 and 2)

Write the balanced equation for the complete combustion of butane, C4H10. Then explain why a poorly ventilated gas heater is dangerous even though butane and propane themselves are not poisonous.

Answer
2 C4H10 + 13 O2 → 8 CO2 + 10 H2O (balance C, then H, then O; doubling the alkane avoids a fractional coefficient for O2). With limited oxygen the combustion is incomplete and produces carbon monoxide, CO. CO is a small, slightly polar molecule (a C≡O triple bond with a lone pair on each atom, Chapter 1) that binds hemoglobin about 200 times more tightly than O2 does, so a few hundred parts per million in the air can be fatal. Carbon-monoxide poisoning is the reason gas appliances need ventilation and homes need CO detectors.

Cumulative Problem 2.5 (Chapter 2)

Name the alkane CH3CH(CH3)CH2CH(CH3)CH2CH3 and classify every carbon in it as primary (1°), secondary (2°) or tertiary (3°).

Carbon classes: C2 and C4 are each bonded to three other carbons, so they are 3°; C3 and C5 are bonded to two carbons, 2°; C1, C6 and both methyl carbons are bonded to one carbon, 1°. Total: four 1°, two 2°, two 3°. (Keep this skill: in Chapter 4 the same count on the carbon that carries an –OH group classifies an alcohol and predicts whether it can be oxidized.)

Cumulative Problem 2.6 (Chapters 1 and 2)

In the clinic. Halothane, CF3CHClBr, is an inhaled general anesthetic, and chloroform, CHCl3, was one of the first. Carbon tetrachloride, CCl4, is not used in medicine. (a) What family do all three belong to? (b) Draw CHCl3 and CCl4, give the shape around carbon, and decide whether each molecule is polar. (c) Which of the two is more likely to have a measurable solubility in water?

Answer
