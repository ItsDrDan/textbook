Viruses are visible only under an electron microscope. They come in a variety of shapes, ranging from spherical to rod shaped. The fact that they contain either deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) or ribonucleic acid (RNA)—but never both—allows them to be divided into two major classes: DNA viruses and RNA viruses (Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\)).

Most RNA viruses use their nucleic acids in much the same way as the DNA viruses, penetrating a host cell and inducing it to replicate the viral RNA and synthesize viral proteins. The new RNA strands and viral proteins are then assembled into new viruses. Some RNA viruses, however, called retroviruses (Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\)), synthesize DNA in the host cell, in a process that is the reverse of the DNA-to-RNA transcription that normally occurs in cells. The synthesis of DNA from an RNA template is catalyzed by the enzyme reverse transcriptase.

In 1987, azidothymidine (AZT, also known as zidovudine or the brand name Retrovir) became the first drug approved for the treatment of AIDS. It works by binding to reverse transcriptase in place of deoxythymidine triphosphate, after which, because AZT does not have a 3′OH group, further replication is blocked. In the past 10 years, several other drugs have been approved that also act by inhibiting the viral reverse transcriptase.

Raltegravir (Isentress) is a newer anti-AIDS drug that was approved by the FDA in October 2007. This drug inhibits the integrase enzyme that is needed to integrate the HIV DNA into cellular DNA, an essential step in the production of more HIV particles.

A major problem in treating HIV infections is that the virus can become resistant to any of these drugs. One way to combat the problem has been to administer a “cocktail” of drugs, typically a combination of two reverse transcriptase inhibitors along with a protease inhibitor. These treatments can significantly reduce the amount of HIV in an infected person.

Career Focus: Genetics Counselor

A genetics counselor works with individuals and families who have birth defects or genetic disorders or a family history of a disease, such as cancer, with a genetic link. A genetics counselor may work in a variety of health-care settings (such as a hospital) to obtain family medical and reproductive histories; explain how genetic conditions are inherited; explain the causes, diagnosis, and care of these conditions; interpret the results of genetic tests; and aid the individual or family in making decisions regarding genetic diseases or conditions. A certified genetics counselor must obtain a master’s degree from an accredited program. Applicants to these graduate programs usually have an undergraduate degree in biology, psychology, or genetics.

Summary

Viruses are very small infectious agents that contain either DNA or RNA as their genetic material. The human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) causes acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS).

Lecture Connection

A virus is nucleic acid in a protein coat with no machinery of its own; it borrows the host's. The lecture asks for an overview only: the difference between DNA viruses, RNA viruses and retroviruses, the steps of infection, why reverse transcriptase breaks the central dogma, and how antivirals and vaccines use that chemistry.

Three kinds of virus

A virus is a package of nucleic acid, DNA or RNA, inside a protein coat (and often a lipid envelope borrowed from the last host cell). It has no ribosomes, no metabolism, and no way to make ATP, so it is not a cell: it must get inside a host cell and borrow the host's polymerases, ribosomes, tRNA, amino acids, and energy to make copies of itself.

  • DNA viruses (herpes simplex, hepatitis B, HPV, chickenpox) deliver DNA that the host transcribes and translates like its own genes.
  • RNA viruses (influenza, SARS-CoV-2, measles, hepatitis C) deliver RNA that is copied by a viral RNA polymerase and translated directly. RNA polymerases make many errors, so RNA viruses mutate quickly (Section 12.9).
  • Retroviruses (HIV) carry RNA plus the enzyme reverse transcriptase, which copies the RNA into DNA, reversing the usual flow of information. A second viral enzyme, integrase, splices that DNA into a host chromosome, where it stays for the life of the cell. HIV infects helper T cells and gradually destroys the immune system (AIDS).

How a virus infects a cell, in outline

The virus attaches to the host cell and its DNA or RNA enters; the host's enzymes, nucleotides, ribosomes and amino acids then copy the viral nucleic acid and translate the viral proteins; new virus particles assemble and leave to infect other cells. For a retrovirus the extra first step is reverse transcription: reverse transcriptase makes a viral DNA strand from the viral RNA, that strand forms its complement, and the double-stranded viral DNA is then handled by the host like one of its own genes. Antiviral drugs work by blocking the steps that only the virus performs (for example, reverse transcriptase: the AZT in the section text is a thymidine look-alike with no 3′-OH, so the chain cannot grow once it is added), and vaccines show the immune system a harmless piece of the virus ahead of time: a killed or weakened virus, a recombinant viral protein (hepatitis B, HPV), or, in mRNA vaccines, an mRNA that your own ribosomes translate into one viral protein for a few days.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Three things on the ward come straight from this chapter. Rapidly dividing cells need nucleotides. Many chemotherapy drugs starve cells of the building blocks for DNA replication, so they hit the fastest-dividing tissues hardest: the tumor, but also the gut lining, bone marrow, and hair follicles, which explains mucositis (mouth sores), low blood counts, and hair loss. The same chemistry is why folic acid (the vitamin behind the coenzyme THF, Chapter 11) in early pregnancy lowers the risk of neural tube defects such as spina bifida: the neural tube closes in the first month, when cells divide fastest and need folate to make nucleotides. Retroviruses carry their own enzymes. A patient with HIV takes a combination of drugs, each blocking a viral enzyme human cells do not have (reverse transcriptase among them); the combination matters because a virus with such a high mutation rate quickly evolves resistance to any single drug. An mRNA vaccine is a fragile message. It needs cold storage because RNA hydrolyzes easily, it never enters the nucleus, and it is degraded within days, after ribosomes have made the viral protein that trains the immune response.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Viruses are part of every day in the operatory. Human papillomavirus type 16 is a DNA virus whose proteins disable two of the cell's growth-control proteins, and it now causes most oropharyngeal cancers (base of tongue, tonsils); oral cancer screening at each recall visit and the HPV vaccine, a recombinant viral coat protein, are the defenses. Hepatitis B is a bloodborne DNA virus that survives for days on surfaces, so clinicians are vaccinated (again a recombinant protein made in yeast) and follow standard precautions. Herpes simplex virus 1 hides in the trigeminal nerve and reactivates as herpes labialis (cold sores); during an active lesion elective treatment is deferred because the virus spreads by contact and aerosol, and antiviral nucleoside look-alikes, which the viral DNA polymerase builds into DNA and which then stop chain growth, shorten outbreaks. Saliva carries DNA from shed epithelial cells and microbes, so a saliva sample serves PCR tests for oral HPV or SARS-CoV-2 (Section 12.8).
