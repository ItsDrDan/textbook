Learning Objectives

  • Explain how the 2′-OH and uracil make RNA chemically different from DNA, and why RNA is single-stranded but still folds into base-paired stems and loops.
  • Describe the structure and job of messenger RNA, transfer RNA, and ribosomal RNA, and identify each from a description.
  • Given a tRNA anticodon, write the codon it pairs with and name the amino acid.
  • Explain how the three RNAs cooperate during translation and what snRNA and microRNA add.
  • Connect RNA chemistry to mRNA vaccines, RNA viruses, and antibiotics that act on bacterial ribosomes.

Most of the nucleic acid in a cell is not DNA. By mass, a working cell holds several times more RNA than DNA, and nearly all of it is busy: carrying messages, hauling amino acids, or building proteins. RNA is also the material of mRNA vaccines, the genome of influenza, SARS-CoV-2, and HIV, and the target of several of the antibiotics you will administer or see prescribed. This section looks at what makes RNA different from DNA and at what each kind of RNA does.

RNA chemistry: one extra OH changes everything

RNA is a polymer of ribonucleotides joined by the same 3′→5′ phosphodiester backbone as DNA (Section 12.2). Two small changes in the monomers account for every difference in behavior.

  • The sugar is ribose. Each sugar carries an OH on C2′. That OH sits right next to the 3′ phosphate ester and can attack it, cutting the backbone (the phosphate ester is hydrolyzed with help from the neighboring OH). The practical result is that RNA falls apart in mildly basic solution and at warm temperatures, while DNA, with no 2′-OH, lasts for years. DNA is the archive; RNA is the sticky note.
  • Uracil replaces thymine. Uracil pairs with adenine exactly as thymine does (two hydrogen bonds); it simply lacks the methyl group. Cells use the "expensive" methylated base in DNA because cytosine slowly loses its NH2 group and turns into uracil; repair enzymes recognize any uracil in DNA as damage and replace it. In short-lived RNA that bookkeeping is not worth the cost.
  • It is single-stranded, but not unpaired. RNA is made as one strand, so nothing forces %A to equal %U. But wherever two stretches of the same strand happen to be complementary, the strand folds back and they pair, forming a short double helix called a stem closed by a loop of unpaired bases: a hairpin. A long RNA can contain dozens of hairpins, and the way they pack together gives each RNA a specific three-dimensional shape, much as hydrogen bonds and side-chain interactions fold a protein (Chapter 10). Shape is what lets rRNA and tRNA act like machines rather than messages.

Every RNA in a cell is made by transcription (Section 12.4): RNA polymerase reads the template strand of a gene and assembles ribonucleotides 5′→3′. Unlike DNA polymerase, RNA polymerase does not proofread, which matters little for a molecule that will be recycled within hours, but matters a great deal for viruses whose genome is RNA (see below).

Professor Tip

Students often picture RNA as "half a DNA." Picture it instead as a strand of DNA that has been handed a 2′-OH and told to go fold itself. The OH explains the fragility; the folding explains the function. If a question asks why RNA does anything DNA does not, one of those two ideas is the answer.

Messenger RNA

Messenger RNA (mRNA) is the working copy of a gene. Its bases are read in groups of three, the codons, starting at AUG and ending at a stop codon (Section 12.5). In human cells the new transcript is edited before it leaves the nucleus: a modified guanine nucleotide, the 5′ cap, is added to the front, a string of 100 to 200 adenines, the poly-A tail, is added to the end, and the non-coding introns are cut out (see snRNA below). The cap and tail protect the ends from RNA-cutting enzymes and tell the ribosome where to start; as the tail is slowly chewed away, the message is retired. Most mRNAs last minutes to a few hours, which is what lets a cell change which proteins it makes as conditions change.

Transfer RNA

Transfer RNA (tRNA) is the adapter that makes the genetic code work. Nothing about the codon GCA looks like alanine, so something has to connect the two, and tRNA does it with two ends:

  • The anticodon, three bases in the bottom loop of the cloverleaf, pairs antiparallel with the codon on the mRNA. A tRNA whose anticodon is 3′-CGU-5′ pairs with the codon 5′-GCA-3′.
  • The 3′ end, which always ends in the sequence CCA, carries the amino acid. The amino acid's carboxyl group is joined to the 2′- or 3′-OH of the final ribose as an ester (Chapter 6). This is a high-energy ester; its hydrolysis later pays for making the peptide bond.

Who decides which amino acid goes on which tRNA? A family of enzymes, the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases (ligases, Chapter 11), one for each amino acid. Each enzyme recognizes the shape of its amino acid and the shape of its tRNAs and joins them, using ATP. This is the real "translation" step: once alanine is esterified to a tRNA with the anticodon for GCA, base pairing on the ribosome does the rest. A tRNA carrying its amino acid is called an aminoacyl-tRNA. There are roughly 40 to 50 different tRNAs in a human cell, enough to read all 61 sense codons because some anticodons tolerate a mismatch at the third codon position (that is one reason the code is degenerate).

Ribosomal RNA

Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) is the most abundant RNA in any cell. Several rRNA molecules fold together with dozens of proteins to form the two subunits of the ribosome, the particle on which translation happens. The small subunit binds the mRNA and checks that each codon–anticodon pair is correct; the large subunit holds the tRNAs and joins the amino acids. For decades it was assumed that the proteins did the chemistry, but the catalytic site that forms the peptide bond, the peptidyl transferase center, turns out to be made entirely of rRNA. The ribosome is a ribozyme: an enzyme made of RNA. Human ribosomes (called 80S, from their sedimentation rate) are larger than bacterial ribosomes (70S) and differ enough in rRNA shape that several antibiotics bind only the bacterial kind.

Other RNAs: splicing and regulation

Human genes are interrupted. The coding stretches, exons, are separated by non-coding stretches, introns, that are transcribed along with them. Before the mRNA leaves the nucleus a complex of proteins and small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs), the spliceosome, recognizes the boundary sequences, cuts out each intron, and seals the exons together (splicing). The snRNAs find the boundaries by base pairing with them, so a mutation that changes a boundary sequence can leave an intron in place or skip an exon; a sizable share of inherited diseases, including some cases of cystic fibrosis and β-thalassemia, are splicing mutations. Because exons can be joined in more than one pattern (alternative splicing), one gene can give several related proteins.

Cells also make microRNAs (miRNAs), about 22 nucleotides long, that base-pair with matching sequences in mRNAs and either block their translation or mark them for destruction, a fine-tuning control on how much of each protein is made.

How the three RNAs cooperate

Translation is a team effort, and each RNA brings one thing. mRNA brings the information. tRNA brings the amino acids, already matched to their codons by the synthetases. rRNA brings the workbench and the catalyst. The ribosome binds the mRNA at the start codon, accepts the aminoacyl-tRNA whose anticodon pairs with the next codon, transfers the growing chain onto that amino acid by forming a new peptide bond, and shifts one codon down the message. The empty tRNA leaves to be reloaded. When a stop codon arrives, no tRNA fits; a release factor protein frees the finished polypeptide, and it folds (Chapter 10).

Worked examples

Example 1: Identify the RNA

Problem: Identify each RNA from the description. (a) A molecule of about 75 nucleotides with a CCA at its 3′ end and an amino acid attached there as an ester. (b) The most abundant RNA in a cell; part of it catalyzes peptide bond formation. (c) A molecule with a 5′ cap, a poly-A tail, and a reading frame that starts with AUG. (d) A short RNA in the nucleus that pairs with the boundary of an intron.

Solution

(a) tRNA: small, CCA end, carries an amino acid. (b) rRNA: most abundant and the ribozyme of the ribosome. (c) mRNA: cap, tail, and codons. (d) snRNA: part of the spliceosome.

Check: Each description names a feature that only one RNA has: an attached amino acid (tRNA), catalysis in the ribosome (rRNA), codons (mRNA), intron boundaries (snRNA).

Example 2: From anticodon to amino acid

Problem: A tRNA has the anticodon 3′-ACC-5′. Write the mRNA codon it pairs with and name the amino acid this tRNA should carry.

Solution

The anticodon pairs antiparallel with the codon, so write the complement in the opposite direction: 3′-ACC-5′ pairs with 5′-UGG-3′. In the genetic code (Section 12.5), UGG codes for tryptophan (Trp). The tryptophan-tRNA synthetase attaches Trp to this tRNA's 3′ end.

Check: Line them up: codon 5′-U G G-3′ over anticodon 3′-A C C-5′. U pairs with A, G with C, G with C. If you had treated the anticodon as 5′-ACC-3′, you would have written the codon as 5′-GGU-3′ and gotten Gly, the wrong answer; direction matters.

Example 3: Why RNA viruses change so fast

Problem: Influenza needs a new vaccine most years, while the vaccine for hepatitis B, a DNA virus, has not changed since the 1980s. Explain using what you know about RNA polymerases.

Solution

An RNA virus copies its genome with its own RNA-dependent RNA polymerase. Like the cell's RNA polymerase, it has no proofreading: a wrong base is left in place. Its error rate is roughly one mistake per 10,000 to 100,000 bases, thousands of times higher than DNA replication with proofreading and repair. A flu genome is about 13,500 bases, so nearly every new virus particle carries a mutation somewhere. Over a season, mutations in the genes for the surface proteins accumulate until antibodies raised against last year's proteins no longer bind well, and the vaccine must be updated. A DNA virus such as hepatitis B is copied with far fewer errors, so its surface protein stays the same and the vaccine keeps working.

Check: The argument rests on two facts from this chapter: DNA polymerase proofreads, RNA polymerases do not. (Coronaviruses are a partial exception: they carry their own proofreading enzyme, which is one reason SARS-CoV-2 changes more slowly per base than influenza does.)

Clinical Connection: Nursing

An mRNA vaccine is this section in a vial. The active ingredient is a synthetic mRNA with a cap, a poly-A tail, and a reading frame for one viral protein (the SARS-CoV-2 spike), packaged in lipid nanoparticles, tiny spheres of phospholipid and cholesterol (Chapter 9) that fuse with a cell membrane and deliver the message to the cytoplasm. Ribosomes translate it for a few days, the immune system learns the protein, and the mRNA is hydrolyzed; it never enters the nucleus and cannot be reverse-transcribed by human cells. The reason the first vaccines shipped at −70 °C is the chemistry of the 2′-OH: RNA cuts itself apart in water at room temperature, and RNases, the enzymes that digest RNA, are on every surface, which is why thawed vials are used within hours. Influenza and SARS-CoV-2 are RNA viruses that mutate quickly; HIV is a retrovirus whose reverse transcriptase, also without proofreading, generates drug-resistant variants unless several drugs are given together. Finally, several antibiotic classes work because bacterial ribosomes differ from ours: tetracyclines block the aminoacyl-tRNA from docking on the small subunit, macrolides such as erythromycin and azithromycin plug the exit tunnel of the large subunit, and aminoglycosides such as gentamicin make the small subunit misread codons. Mitochondrial ribosomes resemble bacterial ones, which is part of why aminoglycosides can damage the inner ear and kidney.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Doxycycline, a tetracycline, is the antibiotic you will meet most often in periodontal care, and it is used in two different ways. At the usual antibiotic dose it binds the small subunit of bacterial ribosomes and stops the aminoacyl-tRNA from pairing with the codon, so the periodontal pathogens cannot make protein. At a much lower dose it is prescribed not as an antibiotic at all but as an enzyme inhibitor (Chapter 11): it chelates the zinc in the host collagenase enzymes that break down the collagen of the periodontal ligament, slowing attachment loss in periodontitis. Tetracyclines also chelate calcium, which is why they must not be given during tooth development; they are built into the forming hydroxyapatite and leave permanent gray-brown bands (intrinsic tetracycline staining) in the enamel and dentin. The same chelation is why calcium-rich foods and antacids reduce how much tetracycline is absorbed from the gut. Two RNA viruses matter in the operatory as well: influenza and SARS-CoV-2 spread in the aerosols that ultrasonic scalers produce, which is the chemistry behind high-volume evacuation, pre-procedural rinses, and the annual flu vaccine for clinicians.

Summary

  • RNA differs from DNA by a 2′-OH on each ribose, which makes the backbone easy to hydrolyze, and by uracil in place of thymine. It is single-stranded but folds into base-paired stems and loops (hairpins) that give it shape.
  • mRNA carries the codons for one protein; in human cells it has a 5′ cap and a poly-A tail and is short-lived.
  • tRNA is the adapter: its anticodon pairs antiparallel with a codon, and the matching amino acid is attached as an ester at its 3′ CCA end by an aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase.
  • rRNA is the most abundant RNA; it forms the two ribosomal subunits and catalyzes peptide bond formation (the ribosome is a ribozyme).
  • snRNA in the spliceosome removes introns and joins exons; microRNA fine-tunes how much of an mRNA is translated.
  • RNA polymerases do not proofread, so RNA viruses mutate quickly. mRNA vaccines must be kept cold because of RNA hydrolysis and RNases. Tetracyclines, macrolides, and aminoglycosides act on bacterial ribosomes and spare human cytoplasmic ribosomes.

Exercises

Exercise 1

List three structural differences between RNA and DNA, and state which one explains why RNA solutions must be kept cold.

Answer
(1) Ribose instead of deoxyribose (a 2′-OH is present); (2) uracil instead of thymine; (3) a single strand that folds on itself instead of a double helix. The 2′-OH explains the fragility: it attacks the neighboring phosphate ester and cuts the backbone, a reaction that is faster at higher temperature.

Exercise 2

An RNA sample is 28% A and 20% U. A student says this proves the sample is contaminated because A should equal U. Correct the student.

Answer
Chargaff's rule (%A = %T or %U) applies only to a fully base-paired double strand. RNA is single-stranded, and only the stretches that fold into stems are paired, so nothing requires %A to equal %U. The sample may be perfectly good.

Exercise 3

Name the RNA described: (a) the one that is used up and replaced within hours; (b) the one that is a ribozyme; (c) the one that is esterified to an amino acid; (d) the one that removes introns.

Answer
(a) mRNA; (b) rRNA (its peptidyl transferase center); (c) tRNA; (d) snRNA (in the spliceosome).

Exercise 4

A tRNA has the anticodon 3′-GUA-5′. Write the codon it reads and name the amino acid that should be attached to it. Which enzyme attaches it, and what kind of bond is formed?

Answer
Codon 5′-CAU-3′, which codes for histidine (His). Histidine-tRNA synthetase attaches His to the 3′-CCA end of the tRNA as an ester between the amino acid's carboxyl group and a ribose OH, using ATP.

Exercise 5

Draw (or describe) what happens to the RNA 5′-GGCAUCUUCGGAUGCC-3′ in solution. Which bases pair, and what is the underlined part?

Answer
The first six bases (GGCAUC) are complementary and antiparallel to the last six (GAUGCC): G–C, G–C, C–G, A–U, U–A, C–G. The strand folds back so these pair, forming a six-base-pair stem; the underlined UUCG is the unpaired loop. The structure is a hairpin.

Exercise 6

Erythromycin stops bacteria from making protein but does not stop a patient's own cells. Explain, naming the molecule the drug binds.

Answer
Erythromycin binds the rRNA of the bacterial large ribosomal subunit (near the tunnel the growing peptide exits through) and blocks elongation. Human cytoplasmic ribosomes have a different size and rRNA shape (80S rather than 70S), so the drug does not bind them well. The selectivity comes from a structural difference in rRNA, not from a difference in the genetic code.

Exercise 7

A patient asks whether an mRNA vaccine can "change my DNA." Give a two-sentence answer based on where the mRNA goes and what human cells can and cannot do with it.

Answer
The mRNA is delivered to the cytoplasm, where ribosomes translate it; it never enters the nucleus, where DNA is kept. Human cells have no reverse transcriptase to copy RNA into DNA and no way to insert it into a chromosome, and the mRNA is hydrolyzed within days.

Exercise 8

Compare and contrast: a mutation in the gene for a tRNA's anticodon versus a mutation in one codon of an mRNA. Which affects more proteins, and why?

Answer
A mutated codon in one mRNA changes (at most) one amino acid in one protein. A mutated anticodon changes which codon that tRNA reads, so the wrong amino acid would be inserted at every occurrence of that codon in every protein the cell makes. The tRNA mutation affects far more proteins because tRNA is a shared tool, while an mRNA is one message. (Cells usually survive such tRNA mutations only because several tRNA genes read the same codon.)
