Learning Objectives

  • Draw the general structure of an α-amino acid, identify its chiral center, and tell D from L in a Fischer projection.
  • Classify amino acids by their R groups (nonpolar, aromatic, polar neutral, acidic, basic) and use their three- and one-letter codes.
  • Draw an amino acid as a zwitterion at its isoelectric point and predict its structure and net charge above and below the pI.
  • Form peptide bonds by condensation, break them by hydrolysis, and name peptides from the N-terminus to the C-terminus.
  • Describe the four levels of protein structure and the R-group interactions in tertiary and quaternary structure.
  • Explain how denaturation and hydrolysis differ, and predict which denaturing agents disrupt which interactions.

Chapter Summary

1. Amino acids

All 20 amino acids share the same backbone: an α-carbon carrying –NH3+, –COO−, H and a side chain (R group). Only the R group changes, and it decides everything: sort it first as hydrophobic (nonpolar: Gly, Ala, Val, Leu, Ile, Pro, Met, Phe, Trp) or hydrophilic, then sort the hydrophilic ones as acidic (Asp, Glu), basic (His, Lys, Arg) or polar neutral (Ser, Thr, Cys, Tyr, Asn, Gln). Nine are essential (must come from food); protein is not stored, so it is eaten daily. Details, the full table and examples: Section 10.1.

2. Zwitterions and pH

At pH 7.4 an amino acid is a zwitterion: –NH3+ and –COO−, net 0. Strong acid adds H+ to the carboxylate (–COOH, net +1); strong base removes H+ from the ammonium group (–NH2, net −1). Rule: below the pI, protonated; at the pI, zwitterion; above the pI, deprotonated. Count charges last. Details, the Try It widget and examples: Section 10.2.

3. Peptides

Amino acids join by condensation (lose water) to form an amide link called the peptide bond; hydrolysis (add water, acid or enzyme) breaks it. Write and name peptides from the N-terminus (free –NH3+) to the C-terminus (free –COO−); every residue but the last ends in -yl (Gly-Ala = glycylalanine). n residues have n − 1 peptide bonds; dashes mean the order is known, commas only the composition. Lecture cut-offs: oligopeptide 2–20, polypeptide 21–40, protein more than 40. Details, figures and the drawing method: Section 10.4; naming and primary structure: Section 10.6.

4. Levels of protein structure

Primary: the sequence, held by peptide bonds (covalent). Secondary: α-helix and β-pleated sheet, held by hydrogen bonds between backbone C=O and N–H (intramolecular when on one chain); collagen's triple helix is the third type. Tertiary: the 3-D fold of one chain, held by R-group interactions: hydrophobic, hydrogen bonds, salt bridges (+/−), disulfide bonds (the only covalent one). Quaternary: two or more chains (hemoglobin has four; myoglobin has none). Fibrous proteins (collagen, keratin, silk) are insoluble and structural; globular proteins (enzymes, hemoglobin, albumin) are soluble and do chemistry. Overview and the levels diagram: Section 10.5; one level per page in Sections 10.6–10.9.

5. Abbreviations and stereochemistry

Three-letter codes for peptides, one-letter codes for proteins; learn the nine odd ones (F, Y, W, K, R, D, E, N, Q). Every amino acid except glycine has a chiral α-carbon. In a Fischer projection with the carboxylate on top, amino group on the left = L, the form in all human proteins; "L" says nothing about rotating light. We use D/L only, not R/S. Details and the Fischer figure: Section 10.1.

6. Isoelectric point (pI)

The pI is the pH at which the net charge is zero (pure zwitterion). Rule of thumb: most amino acids pI ≈ 5–6; an acidic R group pulls it below 4 (Asp 3.0, Glu 3.2); a basic R group pushes it above 6 (His 7.6, Lys 9.7, Arg 10.8). At pH 7.4, Asp and Glu are net −1, Lys and Arg net +1. In electrophoresis, negative molecules move toward the positive electrode, positive ones toward the negative electrode, and a molecule at its pI stays put; a protein is also least soluble at its pI (casein precipitates at pH 4.6). Details, pI table and examples: Sections 10.2 and 10.3.

7. Denaturation and hydrolysis

Denaturation unfolds secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure; peptide bonds survive, function is lost. Match the agent to the interaction: heat, alcohol and agitation hit hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions; acids and bases hit salt bridges and hydrogen bonds; heavy metals hit disulfides and salt bridges; reducing agents (perms) hit disulfides only. Hydrolysis breaks the peptide bonds themselves (primary structure), which is what digestion does. Misfolded proteins (prions, amyloid) are a third way to lose function. Details, the agent table and examples: Section 10.10.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Alanine at three pH values

Problem. Draw alanine (R = CH3) at pH 1, pH 7.4, and pH 12.

Solution.

  • pH 1: +H3N–CH(CH3)–COOH (net +1)
  • pH 7.4: +H3N–CH(CH3)–COO− (zwitterion, net 0)
  • pH 12: H2N–CH(CH3)–COO− (net −1)

Check. The carboxyl group loses its H+ first (around pH 2) and the ammonium group last (around pH 9–10), so the charge steps down +1 → 0 → −1 as the pH rises.

Example 2: Identify the tertiary interaction

Problem. What interaction forms between the side chains of (a) Glu and Arg, (b) Phe and Val, (c) Ser and Asn?

Solution. (a) Glu is − and Arg is +: salt bridge. (b) Both nonpolar: hydrophobic interaction. (c) Both polar with O–H or N–H: hydrogen bond.

Check. Classify each side chain first (acidic, basic, nonpolar, polar neutral); the pair of classes names the interaction. Only two Cys residues would give the fourth type, a disulfide bond.

Example 3: Reading a peptide

Problem. For the tripeptide Ser-Gly-Val, which amino acid is the N-terminus? How many peptide bonds are there?

Solution. Serine is written first, so it is the N-terminus. Valine is the C-terminus. Three amino acids are joined by 2 peptide bonds.

Check. A peptide of n residues has n − 1 peptide bonds; 3 − 1 = 2.

Example 4: Name a tripeptide and find its hydrolysis products

Problem. A tripeptide has the sequence Ala-Cys-Gly. (a) Which residue is the N-terminus and which is the C-terminus? (b) Give its full name and its one-letter form. (c) What products form when it is completely hydrolyzed?

Solution.

(a) Peptides are written from the N-terminus, so alanine (free –NH3+) is the N-terminus and glycine (free –COO−) is the C-terminus. Cysteine in the middle has used both of its groups in the two peptide bonds.

(b) Change the ending of every residue except the last to -yl: alanine → alanyl, cysteine → cysteinyl, glycine stays glycine. Run them together: alanylcysteinylglycine. One-letter form: A, C, G → ACG.

(c) Complete hydrolysis adds one water across each of the two peptide bonds and returns the three free amino acids as zwitterions: alanine + cysteine + glycine. No new compounds form; the residues simply get their –NH3+ and –COO− groups back.

Check. Three residues, two peptide bonds, two waters consumed in hydrolysis. Reversing the letters (GCA, glycylcysteinylalanine) would be a different tripeptide with glycine at the N-terminus, so the order in the name must match the order in the sequence.

Example 5: Classify from the structure

Problem. Three amino acids have the side chains (a) –CH2CH2COO−, (b) –CH2OH and (c) –CH2CH(CH3)2. Name each, give its three- and one-letter codes, classify its R group, and state its net charge at pH 7.4.

Solution.

  • (a) A second carboxylate two carbons from the α-carbon: glutamate, Glu, E; acidic. At pH 7.4: –NH3+ (+1) and two –COO− (−2), net −1.
  • (b) A hydroxyl group: serine, Ser, S; polar neutral. Net 0 (zwitterion; the OH is uncharged).
  • (c) A branched hydrocarbon: leucine, Leu, L; nonpolar (and essential). Net 0.

Check. Only acidic and basic side chains change the net charge at pH 7.4. If (a) had been one carbon shorter (–CH2COO−) it would be aspartate, Asp, D, with the same classification and charge.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Drawing amino acids in the body as neutral –NH2/–COOH. At pH 7.4 they are zwitterions.
  • Writing peptides from the C-terminus. Always start with the N-terminus.
  • Saying denaturation breaks peptide bonds. It does not; hydrolysis does.
  • Mixing up secondary (backbone hydrogen bonds) and tertiary (R-group interactions) structure.
  • Assuming the pI is 7. For most amino acids it is near 6, and a charged side chain pushes it to about 3 (acidic) or 10 (basic). Always compare the pH with the actual pI.
  • Reading "L" as "levorotatory" or as "left-handed." L and D come from the Fischer projection (amino group on the left = L) and say nothing about which way the compound rotates light.
  • Calling a disulfide bond a hydrogen bond. It is a covalent S–S bond formed by oxidizing two cysteine thiols, and it is the only tertiary interaction a reducing agent can break.
  • Treating "essential" as "most important." An essential amino acid is one the body cannot synthesize and must get from food.
  • Calling the hydrogen bonds of an α-helix "intermolecular." The N–H and C=O are on the same chain, so they are intramolecular hydrogen bonds between backbone groups.
  • Writing Ala, Gly, Ser when you mean Ala-Gly-Ser. Commas say only which residues are present; dashes say the order and stand for the peptide bonds.
  • Forgetting the side-chain charge when drawing a peptide at pH 7.4. The ends are easy; the Asp, Glu, Lys and Arg side chains are where points are lost.

Clinical Update: Rosa’s Story, Part 10 of 15

Protein in the urine and damaged gums

A yearly urine test shows a small amount of albumin in Rosa’s urine: a urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio of 45 mg/g, where under 30 is normal. Albumin is a globular protein that healthy kidneys keep in the blood. A little leaking through is an early warning sign of kidney damage from diabetes and high blood pressure; her lisinopril helps protect the kidneys.

At the dental office, the hygienist explains that in periodontitis, enzymes from bacteria and from Rosa’s own immune cells break down the collagen fibers that hold her teeth in their sockets.

Case Question 10.1

Albumin is a globular protein that dissolves in blood plasma. Where are its polar and nonpolar side chains mostly found?

Answer
Polar and charged side chains are mostly on the outside, facing water; nonpolar side chains are mostly buried inside the folded protein (hydrophobic interactions). That arrangement makes globular proteins water-soluble.

Case Question 10.2

Collagen is a fibrous protein built from three chains wound into a triple helix. When enzymes break collagen down, which bonds do they hydrolyze?

Answer
The peptide (amide) bonds between amino acids in the polypeptide chains. Hydrolysis destroys the primary structure, so the fibers lose their strength.

Case Question 10.3

The glucose attached to hemoglobin in the A1c test is bonded to the amino group of the first amino acid in the chain. Does this change the protein’s primary structure? Explain.

Answer
No. The amino acid sequence is unchanged; a sugar has been chemically attached to an amino group on the existing chain. The primary structure is the sequence of amino acids, which stays the same.

Practice

Exercise 1

Classify Lys, Leu, Asp, and Thr.

Answer
Lys: basic. Leu: nonpolar. Asp: acidic. Thr: polar neutral.

Exercise 2

What is the net charge of glycine at pH 1?

Answer
+1 (–NH3+ and –COOH)

Exercise 3

Which level of structure is lost when a protein is hydrolyzed but not when it is merely denatured?

Answer
Primary structure (the peptide bonds)

Exercise 4

A hair "perm" breaks and then re-forms one type of bond to reshape hair protein (keratin). Which one?

Answer
Disulfide bonds between cysteine side chains

Exercise 5

The pI of lysine is about 9.7. Is lysine positive, negative, or neutral overall at pH 7.4?

Answer
Positive. The pH is below its pI, so it carries a net positive charge.

Exercise 6

Name the tripeptide Val-Ser-Lys and write it in one-letter form.

Answer
Valylseryllysine (valyl-seryl-lysine); VSK. Valine is the N-terminus and lysine the C-terminus.

Exercise 7

In a Fischer projection of serine with the COO− at the top, the NH3+ is on the right. Is this the form found in human proteins?

Answer
No. Amino group on the right is D-serine; proteins are built from L-amino acids (amino group on the left).

Exercise 8

Collagen, hemoglobin, keratin, and an enzyme: which are fibrous and which are globular?

Answer
Fibrous: collagen and keratin (structural, insoluble). Globular: hemoglobin and the enzyme (compact, water-soluble, functional).
