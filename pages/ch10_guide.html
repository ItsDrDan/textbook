Learning Objectives

  • Classify amino acids by their R groups: nonpolar, polar neutral, acidic, or basic.
  • Draw an amino acid as a zwitterion at physiological pH, and in acidic and basic solution.
  • Form peptide bonds and name peptides from the N-terminus to the C-terminus.
  • Describe the four levels of protein structure and the R-group interactions in tertiary structure.
  • Explain how denaturation destroys protein function.

Key Ideas

1. Amino acids

Every amino acid has an amine group, a carboxylic acid group, and an R group, all on the same (α) carbon. The R group decides its personality:

ClassR groupExamplesWhere in a protein
Nonpolar (hydrophobic)HydrocarbonAla, Val, Leu, Ile, PheUsually buried inside
Polar neutral–OH, –SH, amideSer, Thr, Cys, Asn, Gln, TyrOften on the surface
Acidic (− at pH 7.4)–COO−Asp, GluSurface
Basic (+ at pH 7.4)–NH3+ typeLys, Arg, HisSurface

2. Zwitterions and pH

  • At physiological pH (about 7.4) an amino acid is a zwitterion: –NH3+ and –COO−, with a net charge of 0 for neutral R groups.
  • In strong acid (low pH), the COO− picks up H+ → –COOH. Net charge +1.
  • In strong base (high pH), the NH3+ loses H+ → –NH2. Net charge −1.

Professor Tip

"Acid adds H+, base takes H+." Low pH: everything that can be protonated is. High pH: everything that can lose an H+ has.

3. Peptides

The COOH of one amino acid + the NH2 of the next → peptide (amide) bond + H2O. Peptides are written and named from the N-terminus (free NH3+) on the left to the C-terminus (free COO−) on the right: Ser-Gly-Val is seryl-glycyl-valine.

4. Levels of protein structure

LevelWhat it isHeld together by
PrimaryAmino acid sequencePeptide bonds
Secondaryα-helix, β-pleated sheetHydrogen bonds between backbone C=O and N–H
TertiaryOverall 3-D fold of one chainR-group interactions: hydrophobic, hydrogen bonds, salt bridges (+/−), disulfide bonds (Cys–S–S–Cys)
QuaternaryTwo or more chains together (hemoglobin has 4)The same R-group interactions

Denaturation (heat, strong acid or base, alcohol, heavy-metal ions, agitation) disrupts secondary, tertiary, and quaternary structure, and function is lost. Peptide bonds survive. Only hydrolysis breaks the primary structure.

5. Abbreviations and stereochemistry

  • Amino acids have 3-letter and 1-letter codes. Most are obvious (Gly G, Ala A, Val V, Leu L, Ser S), but learn the tricky ones: Phe F, Tyr Y, Trp W, Lys K, Arg R, Asp D, Glu E, Asn N, Gln Q.
  • Every amino acid except glycine has a chiral α-carbon. In a Fischer projection with COO− on top, the NH3+ on the left means L. The amino acids in human proteins are L.

6. Isoelectric point (pI)

The pI is the pH at which an amino acid's net charge is zero (pure zwitterion).

  • pH below pI → extra H+ → net positive.
  • pH above pI → H+ removed → net negative.
  • Typical pI: neutral amino acids about 5–6.5, acidic (Asp, Glu) about 3, basic (Lys, Arg) about 10.

Health Career Connection

  • Dental hygiene – collagen and gum health: Collagen, a triple-helix protein, makes up the periodontal ligament, gingival connective tissue, and the organic matrix of dentin and bone. Vitamin C is needed to modify collagen chains so they can form stable cross-links. Deficiency (scurvy) shows up as bleeding, swollen gums and loose teeth.
  • Nursing – sickle cell disease: A single change in hemoglobin's primary structure (Glu → Val at position 6) puts a hydrophobic R group on the surface. The hemoglobin molecules stick together, and red cells sickle.
  • Nursing – disinfection and sterilization: Alcohol wipes, autoclaves (heat), and heavy-metal antiseptics kill microbes by denaturing their proteins.
  • Nursing – insulin: Insulin is a small two-chain protein held together by disulfide bonds. It is injected, not swallowed, because stomach enzymes would hydrolyze its peptide bonds.
  • Dental hygiene – the pellicle: Within minutes of cleaning, salivary proteins adsorb onto enamel to form a thin protein film, the pellicle. It protects against acid, but it is also the surface plaque bacteria attach to. Tea and coffee stains bind to it, and whitening pastes are rated by how well they remove it.

Further reading: M. Epple and J. Enax, "The Chemistry of Dental Care," ChemistryViews (Part 1, Part 2).

In the Lab

  • Experiment 11 – Amino Acids: classify amino acids by R group, then separate them by paper chromatography, make the spots visible with ninhydrin, and identify an unknown from its Rf value (distance moved by the spot ÷ distance moved by the solvent).
  • Experiment 12 – Peptides and Proteins: denature proteins with heat, acid, base, ethanol, tannic acid, and heavy-metal ions; isolate casein from milk by adding acid until it reaches its isoelectric point; and use the biuret test (blue Cu2+ turns violet when two or more peptide bonds are present) and ninhydrin to tell amino acids from proteins.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Alanine at three pH values

Draw alanine (R = CH3) at pH 1, pH 7.4, and pH 12.

Solution

  • pH 1: +H3N–CH(CH3)–COOH (net +1)
  • pH 7.4: +H3N–CH(CH3)–COO− (zwitterion, net 0)
  • pH 12: H2N–CH(CH3)–COO− (net −1)

Example 2: Identify the tertiary interaction

What interaction forms between the side chains of (a) Glu and Arg, (b) Phe and Val, (c) Ser and Asn?

Solution

(a) Glu is − and Arg is +: salt bridge. (b) Both nonpolar: hydrophobic interaction. (c) Both polar with O–H or N–H: hydrogen bond.

Example 3: Reading a peptide

For the tripeptide Ser-Gly-Val, which amino acid is the N-terminus? How many peptide bonds are there?

Solution

Serine is written first, so it is the N-terminus. Valine is the C-terminus. Three amino acids are joined by 2 peptide bonds.

Example 4: Inside or outside?

Would the segment Ile-Phe-Leu more likely be found on the surface or in the interior of a protein in water?

Solution

All three have nonpolar R groups, so the segment is likely buried in the interior, away from water.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Drawing amino acids in the body as neutral –NH2/–COOH. At pH 7.4 they are zwitterions.
  • Writing peptides from the C-terminus. Always start with the N-terminus.
  • Saying denaturation breaks peptide bonds. It does not; hydrolysis does.
  • Mixing up secondary (backbone hydrogen bonds) and tertiary (R-group interactions) structure.

Practice

Exercise 1

Classify Lys, Leu, Asp, and Thr.

Answer
Lys: basic. Leu: nonpolar. Asp: acidic. Thr: polar neutral.

Exercise 2

What is the net charge of glycine at pH 1?

Answer
+1 (–NH3+ and –COOH)

Exercise 3

Which level of structure is lost when a protein is hydrolyzed but not when it is merely denatured?

Answer
Primary structure (the peptide bonds)

Exercise 4

A hair "perm" breaks and then re-forms one type of bond to reshape hair protein (keratin). Which one?

Answer
Disulfide bonds between cysteine side chains

Exercise 5

The pI of lysine is about 9.7. Is lysine positive, negative, or neutral overall at pH 7.4?

Answer
Positive. The pH is below its pI, so it carries a net positive charge.
