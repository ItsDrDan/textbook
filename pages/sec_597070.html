Learning Objectives

  • To identify the physical properties of alkanes and describe trends in these properties.

Because alkanes have relatively predictable physical properties and undergo relatively few chemical reactions other than combustion, they serve as a basis of comparison for the properties of many other organic compound families. Let’s consider their physical properties first.

Table \(\PageIndex{1}\) describes some of the properties of some of the first 10 straight-chain alkanes. Because alkane molecules are nonpolar, they are insoluble in water, which is a polar solvent, but are soluble in nonpolar and slightly polar solvents. Consequently, alkanes themselves are commonly used as solvents for organic substances of low polarity, such as fats, oils, and waxes. Nearly all alkanes have densities less than 1.0 g/mL and are therefore less dense than water (the density of H2O is 1.00 g/mL at 20°C). These properties explain why oil and grease do not mix with water but rather float on its surface.

Looking Closer: Gas Densities and Fire Hazards

Table \(\PageIndex{1}\) indicates that the first four members of the alkane series are gases at ordinary temperatures. Natural gas is composed chiefly of methane, which has a density of about 0.67 g/L. The density of air is about 1.29 g/L. Because natural gas is less dense than air, it rises. When a natural-gas leak is detected and shut off in a room, the gas can be removed by opening an upper window. On the other hand, bottled gas can be either propane (density 1.88 g/L) or butanes (a mixture of butane and isobutane; density about 2.5 g/L). Both are much heavier than air (density 1.29 g/L). If bottled gas escapes into a building, it collects near the floor. This presents a much more serious fire hazard than a natural-gas leak because it is more difficult to rid the room of the heavier gas.

Also shown in Table \(\PageIndex{1}\) are the boiling points of the straight-chain alkanes, which increase with increasing molar mass. This general rule holds true for the straight-chain homologs of all organic compound families. Larger molecules have greater surface areas and consequently interact more strongly; more energy is therefore required to separate them. For a given molar mass, the boiling points of alkanes are relatively low because these nonpolar molecules have only weak dispersion forces to hold them together in the liquid state.

Looking Closer: An Alkane Basis for Properties of Other Compounds

An understanding of the physical properties of the alkanes is important in that petroleum and natural gas and the many products derived from them—gasoline, bottled gas, solvents, plastics, and more—are composed primarily of alkanes. This understanding is also vital because it is the basis for describing the properties of other organic and biological compound families. For example, large portions of the structures of lipids consist of nonpolar alkyl groups. Lipids include the dietary fats and fatlike compounds called phospholipids and sphingolipids that serve as structural components of living tissues. These compounds have both polar and nonpolar groups, enabling them to bridge the gap between water-soluble and water-insoluble phases. This characteristic is essential for the selective permeability of cell membranes.

Key Takeaway

  • Alkanes are nonpolar compounds that are low boiling and insoluble in water.

Lecture Connection

The source gives you the data; lecture gives you the three questions that rank any set of hydrocarbons by boiling point. Everything in this section follows from one fact: alkanes are nonpolar, so dispersion forces are the only IMF they have, and dispersion grows with size and shrinks with branching.

State, size, and shape

What an alkane is used for depends on its physical state, and its physical state depends on the strength of its dispersion forces, which grow with chain length.

Physical state of alkanes by chain length, with uses and examples
Physical state at room temperatureChain lengthUsesExamples
GasC1–C4FuelNatural gas (methane), propane, butane
LiquidC5–C17Fuels, solventsGasoline, hexane, kerosene, mineral oil
SolidC18 and upWaxes, lubricants, barriersParaffin wax, petroleum jelly
  • Nonpolar, so they have dispersion forces only. They are insoluble in water and less dense than water, so they float, like an oil slick.
  • Melting point and boiling point rise with chain length, because a larger molecule (higher molar mass, more electrons, more surface contact) has more dispersion forces. Branching lowers them: branched molecules cannot pack as tightly together as straight chains, so there is less contact and less dispersion force between neighbors.

The table below shows how the two trends play out in real boiling points. Compare propane, pentane, and octane to see the chain-length effect, and the three C5H12 rows to see the branching effect.

Professor Tip

To rank boiling points, ask three questions in order: (1) Can it hydrogen bond? (not in this chapter) (2) Which is bigger, with more electrons? (3) Among isomers, which is less branched? Bigger and straighter boils higher. Melting points follow the same size trend, and water solubility is the same for all of them: essentially zero.

Example 1: Ranking boiling points with IMFs

Problem. Rank from lowest to highest boiling point: hexane, 2,2-dimethylbutane, and 1-chlorohexane.

Solution. None of the three can hydrogen bond, so compare size and polarity, then branching.

  1. Hexane and 2,2-dimethylbutane are both C6H14 (same number of electrons), so dispersion forces are the only IMF and branching decides. 2,2-Dimethylbutane is compact, so it boils lower (about 50 °C) than hexane (69 °C).
  2. 1-Chlorohexane has the hexane skeleton with a chlorine (17 electrons) in place of one hydrogen (1 electron): many more electrons for stronger dispersion forces and a polar C–Cl bond that adds dipole–dipole attraction. It boils highest (about 135 °C).

Ranking: 2,2-dimethylbutane < hexane < 1-chlorohexane.

Check. All three are insoluble in water, so solubility could not have separated them; boiling point can, and the measured values agree with the ranking. ✓

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Three alkane-based materials turn up in a dental operatory, and each works because alkanes are nonpolar. Orthodontic relief wax and the wax used for bite registration are paraffin, a blend of C20–C40 alkanes: enough dispersion force to be a solid, but weak enough that it softens with finger warmth and molds over a bracket that is irritating the cheek. Petroleum jelly (petrolatum) is painted on the lips and sometimes the gingiva (gum tissue) before in-office bleaching. The bleaching gel is a water-based solution of hydrogen peroxide; because water and a nonpolar alkane film do not mix, the gel beads up on the barrier instead of reaching tissue, protecting it from chemical irritation. PTFE (polytetrafluoroethylene) is a long carbon chain in which every hydrogen is replaced by fluorine. It is the slick coating on some dental floss and the thin tape used to isolate adjacent teeth during composite placement: chemically inert, nonstick, and nonpolar, it slides between tight contacts without shredding and does not bond to the resin.
