Learning Objectives

  • Recognize the acyl group, R–C(=O)–, and use it to identify carboxylic acids, esters, and amides in a structure.
  • Name carboxylic acids, carboxylate salts, and esters, including acids with halo, alkyl, hydroxy, alkoxy, and oxo substituents and derivatives of benzoic acid.
  • Explain the high boiling points of carboxylic acids and compare them with esters, alcohols, and aldehydes.
  • Write the ionization of a carboxylic acid in water and its neutralization with a strong base, and explain why carboxylate salts dissolve in water.
  • Draw the ester formed from an acid and an alcohol (Fischer esterification), and the products of acid-catalyzed hydrolysis and of saponification.
  • Recognize esters of phosphoric acid in biological molecules.

Key Ideas

1. Structures and names

Every compound in this chapter, and the amides of Chapter 7, is built on the same two-atom unit: a carbon double-bonded to an oxygen with one more bond available. This unit, R–C(=O)–, is called an acyl group. What is attached to the acyl carbon decides the family. An –OH gives a carboxylic acid, an –OR′ gives an ester, and an –NH2 (or –NHR, –NR2) gives an amide. (An acyl group bonded to a chlorine is an acid chloride, a reactive lab reagent you will not need to name.) The acid, ester, and amide are often called carboxylic acid derivatives because an acid can be converted into each of the others by swapping the group on the acyl carbon.

Condensed formulas. A carboxylic acid's carboxyl group is abbreviated RCOOH or RCO2H (CH3CH2COOH is propanoic acid). An ester is abbreviated RCOOR′ or RCO2R′ (CH3COOCH2CH3 is ethyl acetate). In both, the first O after the C is the C=O and the second is single-bonded. Two structural facts follow from where the groups sit. A carboxylic acid is always terminal, at the end of a chain, so it cannot be cyclic and never needs a position number. An ester can be cyclic, because its single-bonded O can be part of a ring (Key Idea 6).

Isomers. With enough carbons, both acids and esters have skeletal isomers (butanoic acid and 2-methylpropanoic acid). Carboxylic acids have no positional isomers, since the COOH is always C1; esters do, because the ester link can sit between different carbons of the chain (methyl propanoate and ethyl ethanoate are both C4H8O2). An acid and an ester with the same number of carbons are functional group isomers: butanoic acid and methyl propanoate are both C4H8O2.

  • Naming carboxylic acids: (1) Find the longest chain containing the –COOH group. (2) –COOH is the highest-priority functional group of all, and it is always C1, so omit the 1 from the name. (3) Change the -e of the parent alkane to -oic acid. (4) Apply the other nomenclature rules to build the prefix: CH3CH(CH3)CH(CH3)CH2CH2COOH is 4,5-dimethylhexanoic acid. Common names to know: formic acid (methanoic; a preservative, once used in leather tanning), acetic acid (ethanoic; vinegar), and benzoic acid.
  • Benzoic acid derivatives: a carboxyl group on a benzene ring is benzoic acid; the ring carbon attached to the carboxyl carbon is C1, and the other aromatic nomenclature rules apply: 2-bromobenzoic acid.
  • Carboxylate ion/salt: R–COO−. Named a lot like an ester: metal first, then the acid name with -ic acid changed to -ate: sodium ethanoate (sodium acetate), potassium propanoate.
  • Naming esters (RCOOR′) takes two words. First word: the name of R′, the group attached to the single-bonded O, with a -yl ending. Second word: the name of the acyl group (RCOO) with the -oate suffix. Each word can carry its own prefixes for substituents on that chain: ethyl 2-methylbutanoate; 2-chloroethyl benzoate. Methyl ethanoate (methyl acetate), propyl ethanoate (pear smell).

Professor Tip

In an ester, the side with the C=O came from the carboxylic acid. The side attached through the single-bonded O came from the alcohol. Find the C=O, split the molecule at the single-bonded O, and you can name it or "un-make" it in seconds.

2. Properties

Ask the same three questions you asked for every family so far. Can carboxylic acids hydrogen bond with other carboxylic acids? Yes: they have an H bonded to an O. They also have dipole–dipole forces (the C=O, C–O, and O–H bonds are polar and do not cancel) and, like every molecule, dispersion forces. Can esters hydrogen bond with other esters? No. Like ethers, aldehydes, and ketones, an ester has no H bonded to an O, so esters among themselves have only dipole–dipole and dispersion forces. The carbon of the acyl group has three electron groups, so it is trigonal planar, like the carbonyl carbon of Chapter 5.

  • Carboxylic acids hydrogen bond in pairs (dimers), giving them the highest boiling points of the groups we have studied so far: higher than alcohols of similar size.
  • Esters boil lower than alcohols and carboxylic acids and about the same as aldehydes and ketones of similar size, but higher than alkanes. Many small esters have pleasant fruity smells: ethyl butanoate (mango), pentyl butanoate (apricot).
  • Solubility. Carboxylic acids can both donate protons to and accept them from water, so they are more soluble in water than alcohols, ethers, aldehydes, or ketones of comparable mass; esters can only accept hydrogen bonds from water, much like aldehydes and ketones. For both families, about 5 carbons or fewer means water soluble. Fatty acids, the long-chain carboxylic acids from animal fats and vegetable oils (Chapter 9), are at the insoluble extreme.

Boiling-point trend for similar molar masses: alkane < ester ≈ aldehyde/ketone < alcohol < carboxylic acid.

Why do acids beat alcohols? An alcohol has one O–H to donate. A carboxylic acid has an O–H and a C=O oxygen a short distance away, so two acid molecules can line up head-to-head and form two hydrogen bonds at once, making a dimer that behaves like a molecule of double the mass. Esters and aldehydes have the C=O acceptor but no O–H donor, so among themselves they manage only dipole–dipole attractions. In water, though, the C=O oxygen of an ester can accept a hydrogen bond from water, so small esters (up to about four or five carbons) dissolve somewhat; larger ones are oily and insoluble.

3. Reactions

Two words first. The carbon next to the –COOH group is the α (alpha) carbon, and the carbon bonded to the α carbon is the β (beta) carbon; you will hear both in the metabolism chapters (β-oxidation, β-hydroxybutyrate).

  • Weak acid in water: RCOOH + H2O ⇌ RCOO− + H3O+. Carboxylic acids are proton donors, but weak ones compared with inorganic acids such as HCl or H2SO4: only a small percentage is ionized in aqueous solution.
  • Neutralization: RCOOH + NaOH → RCOO−Na+ + H2O. Carboxylic acids react with bases to form salts, and the acid–base reaction turns a water-insoluble carboxylic acid into a water-soluble carboxylate salt.
  • Preparation of carboxylic acids: oxidation of 1° alcohols. 1° alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid with [O] (Chapter 4; you made these products in the alcohol oxidation lab).
  • Reduction of carboxylic acids runs the same ladder backward with [H]: carboxylic acid → aldehyde → 1° alcohol. (A ketone, by comparison, reduces only to a 2° alcohol, and a 2° alcohol never oxidizes past the ketone.) So CH3CH2COOH + [H] gives propanal and then 1-propanol. In the lab a strong hydride reagent is needed; in the body enzymes do it.
  • Esterification (a condensation reaction): RCOOH + R′OH (H+, heat) ⇌ RCOOR′ + H2O. The –OR′ group replaces the –OH of the acid.
  • Acid hydrolysis (the reverse): ester + H2O (H+) → carboxylic acid + alcohol. The –OH replaces the –OR′.
  • Base hydrolysis (saponification): ester + NaOH → carboxylate salt + alcohol. Think of it as hydrolysis followed by an acid–base reaction: the carboxylic acid that forms is immediately neutralized by the base. This is how soap is made from fats (Chapter 9).

4. Substituents and benzoic acid derivatives

  • Because carboxylic acids have the highest naming priority, other groups appear as prefixes: CH3COCH2COOH is 3-oxobutanoic acid (acetoacetic acid, a ketone body you will meet in Chapter 15). CH3CH(OH)COOH is 2-hydroxypropanoic acid (lactic acid).
  • Benzoic acid derivatives are numbered from the COOH carbon (C1): 2-hydroxybenzoic acid is salicylic acid (the parent of aspirin), and 4-aminobenzoic acid is PABA (released when benzocaine is hydrolyzed).
  • The acid-catalyzed reaction of a carboxylic acid with an alcohol is called Fischer esterification.

The prefixes you need are the same ones you used for aldehydes and ketones, plus the carboxylic acid now outranks everything else, so a ketone C=O becomes oxo- and an –OH becomes hydroxy-. A short checklist for naming a substituted acid:

  1. Find the longest chain that contains the COOH carbon; that carbon is always C1, so no position number is written for the acid itself.
  2. Name each substituent with its prefix and locant: chloro-, bromo-, fluoro- (halogens); methyl-, ethyl- (alkyl); hydroxy- (–OH); methoxy-, ethoxy- (–OR, an alkoxy group); oxo- (a C=O inside the chain).
  3. List the prefixes alphabetically (ignore di-, tri-), then the chain name ending in -oic acid.

Examples: CH3CH(OCH3)COOH is 2-methoxypropanoic acid; CH3CHBrCH2CH2COOH is 4-bromopentanoic acid; CH3COCH(CH3)COOH is 2-methyl-3-oxobutanoic acid. Ring acids are named as derivatives of benzoic acid, with the ring carbon carrying the COOH as C1 and the lowest possible numbers for the other groups; ortho/meta/para are still used in common names.

5. Acidity and carboxylate salts

Carboxylic acids are weak acids: in water only a small fraction of the molecules give up their proton at any instant, so the ionization is written with equilibrium arrows. Their strength is described by the acid dissociation constant, usually quoted as the pKa (the lower the pKa, the stronger the acid). Most simple carboxylic acids have pKa values of about 4–5 (acetic acid 4.8, benzoic acid 4.2, lactic acid 3.9). For comparison, phenol has a pKa near 10 and an alcohol near 16, while HCl is a strong acid and ionizes completely. So carboxylic acids are the most acidic organic group you have met, but still far weaker than the mineral acids.

The pKa tells you the pH at which an acid is half ionized. At any pH more than about two units above the pKa, essentially all of the molecules have lost their proton. Blood is at pH 7.4, roughly three units above the pKa of a typical carboxylic acid, so more than 99% of lactic acid, pyruvic acid, citric acid, aspirin, and ibuprofen in the blood exist as the carboxylate ion, RCOO−. That is why these appear on lab reports as lactate, pyruvate, citrate, and salicylate.

Carboxylate salts dissolve in water; the acids often do not. A carboxylic acid with more than about five carbons is mostly nonpolar and has low water solubility, but neutralizing it with a base makes an ionic compound, and ion–dipole attractions to water pull the salt into solution. Pharmaceutical chemists use this constantly:

  • Sodium benzoate is added to soft drinks and liquid medicines as a preservative because it dissolves readily, whereas benzoic acid itself barely dissolves.
  • Sodium salicylate and sodium citrate are the water-soluble forms used in solutions and IV preparations.
  • Ibuprofen is a carboxylic acid that is nearly insoluble in water. The salt formed with the basic amino acid lysine, ibuprofen lysine, dissolves well enough to be given intravenously and to be absorbed faster from fast-acting oral products. The drug molecule is unchanged; only its charge has.

Two bases are used for neutralization in this course. A strong base such as NaOH or KOH gives the salt and water. Sodium bicarbonate, NaHCO3, also works, and it gives off carbon dioxide gas: RCOOH + NaHCO3 → RCOO−Na+ + H2O + CO2(g). The fizz is a quick lab test for a carboxylic acid, because phenols and alcohols are too weak to react with bicarbonate.

6. Fischer esterification: a condensation at equilibrium

Heating a carboxylic acid with an alcohol and a trace of strong acid (usually H2SO4) joins them into an ester and releases a molecule of water; this is Fischer esterification. A condensation reaction is one in which two molecules combine with the loss of a small molecule (water, HCl, methanol, and so on); a dehydration is the subset of condensations in which the small molecule lost is water. You will see the same pattern when amides, glycosidic bonds, and peptide bonds form. The H+ is a catalyst: it speeds the reaction up but is not used up, so it appears above the arrow rather than as a reactant. The water comes from the –OH of the acid plus the H of the alcohol's –OH; the alcohol's oxygen ends up in the ester.

The scheme shows the forward direction, but esterification is reversible (which is why the Key Ideas list writes it with equilibrium arrows), and the mixture at equilibrium typically contains large amounts of unreacted acid and alcohol. To push the equilibrium toward the ester, chemists use Le Chatelier's principle in two ways: add a large excess of one reactant (usually the cheaper alcohol), or remove the water as it forms. Either change shifts the position of equilibrium to the right. In Experiment 6 you will use excess alcohol and concentrated sulfuric acid, which also absorbs water. The reverse direction, adding water with an acid catalyst, is exactly the acid-catalyzed hydrolysis of the next section.

Cyclic esters. A molecule that contains both a –COOH and an –OH (a hydroxy acid) can esterify with itself, in the same way a hydroxy aldehyde closes to a cyclic hemiacetal (Chapter 5). Internal esterification happens when a five- or six-membered ring can form, which means the OH must be on the γ (C4) or δ (C5) carbon. The cyclic ester is called a lactone; 4-hydroxybutanoic acid, for example, closes to a five-membered lactone and water. A thiol can take the place of the alcohol in a condensation with a carboxylic acid; the product, R–CO–S–R′, is a thioester, and acetyl-CoA, the central molecule of metabolism (Chapter 13), is one.

7. Two ways to hydrolyze an ester

Hydrolysis is a chemical reaction in which water is used to break a covalent bond. Esters hydrolyze only very slowly in plain water, even boiling water; the reaction becomes much faster when the ester is heated in aqueous acid or base, or when an enzyme does the job. An ester can be hydrolyzed back to its parent acid and alcohol in acid, or it can be split by a base. Both break the same bond (the single bond between the acyl carbon and the oxygen), both give the alcohol unchanged, and in both the C=O side becomes the acid part. The differences matter, so compare them side by side.

The word saponification comes from the Latin for soap. Fats and oils are triesters of glycerol with three long-chain fatty acids; boiling them with NaOH gives glycerol plus the sodium salts of the fatty acids. If the acyl R group is a long carbon chain, the carboxylate anion is a soap molecule: an ionic head that dissolves in water and a long nonpolar tail that dissolves grease, so that in water the soap molecules cluster into micelles (pronounced my-SELLS) with the grease trapped inside. In the body, enzymes called esterases and lipases do the same job at pH 7.4 without strong acid or base.

8. Esters of phosphoric acid

A preview, not an exam topic for this chapter. Not every ester is a carboxylic ester: any acid with an –OH can condense with an alcohol. Phosphoric acid, H3PO4, has three –OH groups, so it forms phosphate esters, which you will meet in the sugar phosphates of glycolysis (Chapter 13), the backbone of DNA and RNA (Chapter 12), and the phospholipids of cell membranes (Chapter 9). Recognize the pattern, C–O–P, when it appears; the names and chemistry come later. Nitric acid forms esters the same way, and nitroglycerin, the chest-pain medication, is the trinitrate ester of glycerol, not a carboxylic ester at all.

Health Career Connection

  • Dental hygiene – topical anesthetics: Benzocaine, the active ingredient in topical numbing gels used before injections and in oral pain gels, is an ester. Ester-type anesthetics are hydrolyzed quickly by esterase enzymes in the blood. One of the hydrolysis products (PABA) is behind most true allergies to this class. That is why patients are asked about anesthetic allergies, and why amide-type anesthetics (Chapter 7) are used for injections.
  • Nursing – aspirin: Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid) contains both a carboxylic acid and an ester. Old aspirin tablets that smell like vinegar have been hydrolyzed by moisture, releasing acetic acid. They should be discarded.
  • Nursing – hand hygiene: Soap is a carboxylate salt. Its long nonpolar tail dissolves grease and its ionic head dissolves in water, which lets rinse water carry oils and microbes away.
  • Nursing – citrate in blood tubes: The light-blue-top tube used for coagulation tests contains sodium citrate. Citrate, with its three carboxylate groups, binds calcium ions (Ca2+) tightly, and because clotting requires free Ca2+, the blood stays liquid. The same chemistry is why rapid transfusion of citrate-preserved blood can lower blood calcium (hypocalcemia).
  • Dental hygiene – acid erosion and the critical pH: Enamel is the mineral hydroxyapatite, Ca5(PO4)3OH. Below a mouth pH of about 5.5 it starts to dissolve: Ca5(PO4)3OH + 4 H+ → 5 Ca2+ + 3 HPO42− + H2O. Lactic acid from plaque bacteria causes caries; citric acid in juices and sports drinks causes erosion without any bacteria. Softened enamel is easily brushed away, which is why brushing right after an acidic drink is discouraged.

Further reading: M. Epple and J. Enax, "The Chemistry of Dental Care," ChemistryViews (Part 1, Part 2).

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Aspirin is a two-functional-group molecule: a carboxylic acid on the ring and an ester made from the phenol –OH of salicylic acid and acetic acid. In the stomach and blood, the ester is hydrolyzed to salicylate, the carboxylate ion that actually circulates and is measured in a serum salicylate level. In a large overdose, salicylate does two things you can now explain. First, it is an acid, and it also disrupts the cell's energy metabolism so that lactic acid and ketone acids build up; the result is metabolic acidosis, a blood pH below 7.35 caused by excess acid. Second, because a carboxylic acid is ionized at high pH, making the urine alkaline with sodium bicarbonate traps salicylate in the kidney tubule as the charged carboxylate, which cannot diffuse back into the blood, so it is excreted faster. The same ionization logic explains lactic acidosis in sepsis and shock: poorly oxygenated tissue makes lactic acid, which gives up its proton at blood pH, and the rising lactate level is what the lab reports.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Most of the plastics in a dental operatory are esters. Tooth-colored composite fillings are made from methacrylate esters such as bis-GMA (a large dimethacrylate) mixed with glass filler; the blue curing light starts an addition polymerization of the C=C bonds (Chapter 3), and the ester groups stay in the hardened resin. Acrylic denture bases are PMMA, poly(methyl methacrylate), polymerized from methyl methacrylate, the methyl ester of 2-methylpropenoic acid. A small amount of unreacted monomer can remain in a new denture or a chairside reline, and the ester monomer is what causes the burning sensation or contact sensitivity some patients report; soaking the appliance in water for a day lets the residual monomer leach out. Before the composite is placed, the enamel is roughened with 37% phosphoric acid (the acid-etch step), which dissolves a few micrometers of hydroxyapatite in the same way plaque acid does, but under control. Two more esters you will handle: benzocaine gel, the ethyl ester of PABA used to numb tissue before scaling or injections, and methyl salicylate (oil of wintergreen), the ester of salicylic acid and methanol that flavors many mouthwashes.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Naming an acid and its salt

Problem. Name CH3CH(CH3)CH2COOH and its sodium salt.

Solution. The COOH carbon is C1. The four-carbon chain has a methyl on C3: 3-methylbutanoic acid. The sodium salt is sodium 3-methylbutanoate.

Check. The acid carbon is unnumbered, and the methyl is on C3, not C2, because numbering starts at the COOH end.

Example 2: Making and naming an ester

Problem. Acetic acid reacts with 1-pentanol (H+ catalyst). Draw and name the ester. (It smells like pears.)

Solution.

CH3COOH + HOCH2CH2CH2CH2CH3 → CH3COOCH2CH2CH2CH2CH3 + H2O

Alcohol part first, then the acid part: pentyl ethanoate (pentyl acetate). You will make this ester in Experiment 6.

Check. The C=O side has two carbons (from acetic acid); the single-bonded O carries five carbons (from the alcohol). Water is the other product.

Example 3: Saponification

Problem. Give the products when ethyl butanoate, CH3CH2CH2COOCH2CH3, is heated with NaOH.

Solution. Split at the single-bonded O. The C=O side becomes the carboxylate salt and the other side becomes the alcohol:

CH3CH2CH2COO−Na+ (sodium butanoate) + CH3CH2OH (ethanol)

Check. Base hydrolysis gives the salt, not the free acid; the NaOH is consumed, not a catalyst.

Example 4: Boiling point ranking

Problem. Acetic acid, 1-propanol, and methyl formate all have a molar mass of 60 g/mol. Rank their boiling points.

Solution.

Methyl formate (ester, no self hydrogen bonding, 32 °C) < 1-propanol (hydrogen bonding, 97 °C) < acetic acid (hydrogen-bonded dimers, 118 °C).

Check. Same mass, so only the IMFs differ: no O–H < one O–H < O–H plus C=O (dimer).

Example 5: Naming esters from their structures

Problem. Name (a) CH3CH2CH2COOCH2CH3 (the pineapple-scented ester) and (b) the wintergreen ester whose structure is a benzene ring carrying –COOCH3 on one carbon and –OH on the carbon next to it.

Solution. (a) Find the C=O. The carbon chain on the C=O side has four carbons (counting the C=O carbon), so the acid part is butanoate. The group on the single-bonded oxygen is CH2CH3, an ethyl group. Alcohol part first: ethyl butanoate. (b) The C=O is attached to the ring, so the acid part is benzoate, and the group on the single-bonded oxygen is methyl: methyl benzoate. The ring also carries an –OH on C2 (C1 is the ring carbon with the ester): methyl 2-hydroxybenzoate, better known as methyl salicylate.

Check. Un-make each ester. (a) Ethanol + butanoic acid: 2 + 4 carbons = 6, matching the formula. (b) Methanol + salicylic acid (2-hydroxybenzoic acid): the –OH on the ring is a phenol, which stays untouched; only the COOH was esterified. The names rebuild the same pieces, so they are consistent.

Example 6: Naming an acid with several substituents

Problem. Name CH3CH(Cl)CH2C(=O)CH2COOH and draw 3-hydroxy-4-methoxybutanoic acid.

Solution. The COOH carbon is C1; the chain has six carbons, so the parent is hexanoic acid. Counting from C1: C3 is a ketone C=O (oxo), C5 carries Cl (chloro). Alphabetical order puts chloro before oxo: 5-chloro-3-oxohexanoic acid. For the second compound, start with a four-carbon chain ending in COOH (C1). Put –OH on C3 and –OCH3 on C4: CH3OCH2CH(OH)CH2COOH.

Check. In the first name, the acid is unnumbered (always C1), and 3 + 5 is the lowest set of locants (numbering from the other end would make it a ketone with a COOH substituent, which is not allowed because the acid has priority). In the second, C4 is the last carbon, and a CH2 with one O attached is an ether-type carbon, so methoxy, not hydroxy, is correct for the OCH3.

Example 7: Neutralization with two different bases

Problem. Ibuprofen can be written as R–COOH, where R is the rest of the drug. Write the equation for its reaction with (a) NaOH and (b) NaHCO3, and explain why the product dissolves in water when ibuprofen itself does not.

Solution. (a) R–COOH + NaOH → R–COO−Na+ + H2O. (b) R–COOH + NaHCO3 → R–COO−Na+ + H2O + CO2. In both cases the base removes the proton from the carboxyl group and the product is the same ionic salt, sodium ibuprofenate. Ibuprofen is a large, mostly hydrocarbon molecule, so dispersion forces dominate and water cannot pull it apart. The carboxylate salt has a full negative charge and a Na+ ion, and ion–dipole attractions to water are strong enough to dissolve it.

Check. Count atoms in (b): the H from COOH and the H from HCO3− make H2O, and the remaining CO2 leaves as gas, so hydrogen, carbon, and oxygen balance. Adding strong acid (HCl) to the salt would regenerate the insoluble acid, which is what happens to a sodium-salt tablet when it reaches stomach acid.

Example 8: Reduction of a carboxylic acid, and the oxidation ladder

Problem. (a) What are the products when 3-methylbutanoic acid is reduced, first partially and then completely, with [H]? (b) What 1° alcohol would you oxidize to make 3-methylbutanoic acid? (c) Could any reduction turn 3-methylbutanoic acid into a 2° or 3° alcohol?

Solution. (a) Reduction removes oxygen or adds hydrogen. The carboxylic acid first becomes the aldehyde, 3-methylbutanal, (CH3)2CHCH2CHO, and then the aldehyde becomes the 1° alcohol 3-methyl-1-butanol, (CH3)2CHCH2CH2OH. (b) The same alcohol, 3-methyl-1-butanol: 1° alcohol → aldehyde → acid with [O] is the Chapter 4 reaction run forward. (c) No. The carbon that carries the oxygen in a carboxylic acid is bonded to only one other carbon, and reduction never changes the carbon skeleton, so the alcohol must be 1°. 2° alcohols come from ketones.

Check. Write the ladder: 1° alcohol ⇌ aldehyde ⇌ carboxylic acid, with [O] going up and [H] coming down. The carbon count (five) is the same on every rung.

Example 9: Classifying isomers of acids and esters

Problem. Classify each pair as skeletal isomers, positional isomers, functional group isomers, or the same compound: (a) pentanoic acid and 3-methylbutanoic acid; (b) methyl butanoate and ethyl propanoate; (c) ethyl propanoate and pentanoic acid; (d) CH3CH2COOCH3 and CH3OOCCH2CH3.

Solution. (a) Both are C5H10O2 acids; one chain is straight, the other branched: skeletal isomers. (b) Both are C5H10O2 esters with five carbons in a row, but the ester link sits after the fourth carbon in one and after the third in the other: positional isomers. (c) Same formula, C5H10O2, different functional groups: functional group isomers. (d) The second formula is the first written from the other end (CH3O–C(=O)–CH2CH3): the same compound, methyl propanoate.

Check. Carboxylic acids never have positional isomers, because the COOH is always C1; esters do, because the single-bonded O can sit between different carbons. Always count the carbons on each side of the C=O before deciding.

Example 10: Ester names with substituents on both halves

Problem. (a) Name CH3CH(CH3)CH2COOCH2CH2Cl. (b) Draw 1-methylethyl 3-oxobutanoate.

Solution. (a) Find the C=O and split at the single-bonded O. Acid side (counting the C=O carbon): four carbons with a methyl on C3, so the acyl word is 3-methylbutanoate. Alcohol side: a two-carbon group with Cl on the carbon farther from the oxygen (C2 of that group): 2-chloroethyl. Name: 2-chloroethyl 3-methylbutanoate. (b) The second word, 3-oxobutanoate, is a four-carbon acyl group with a ketone C=O on C3: CH3COCH2C(=O)O–. The first word, 1-methylethyl (the IUPAC spelling of isopropyl), is attached to the single-bonded oxygen through its middle carbon: CH3COCH2COOCH(CH3)2.

Check. Each word is numbered separately, each from its own attachment point: the acyl chain from the C=O carbon, the alkyl group from the carbon bonded to the oxygen. Un-make (a): 2-chloroethanol + 3-methylbutanoic acid; the carbons add up to 2 + 5 = 7, matching the formula.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Naming esters in the wrong order. The alkyl group from the alcohol comes first.
  • Giving a carboxylic acid a positional isomer or a ring. The COOH is always at the end of the chain (C1), so "2-butanoic acid" and "cyclohexanoic acid" cannot exist.
  • Reducing a carboxylic acid to a 2° alcohol. The ladder is acid → aldehyde → 1° alcohol; 2° alcohols come only from ketones.
  • Forgetting the water. Esterification releases H2O, and hydrolysis uses it.
  • Writing a carboxylic acid (instead of its salt) as the product of base hydrolysis.
  • Calling a carboxylic acid a strong acid. It is a weak acid and only partly ionizes.
  • Numbering the chain from the wrong end. The COOH carbon is always C1, so an acid never gets a locant, and a ketone in the same molecule becomes an oxo prefix rather than the suffix.
  • Treating H+ like NaOH. In acid-catalyzed hydrolysis the H+ is a catalyst and the product is the free acid; in saponification the hydroxide is a reactant that is used up, and the product is the carboxylate salt.
  • Assuming an ester can hydrogen bond with itself. The two oxygens of an ester can accept hydrogen bonds from water, but with no O–H the ester has nothing to donate, so its boiling point is near that of an aldehyde of similar mass.
