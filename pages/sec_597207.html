Learning Objectives

  • To describe the reactions needed to completely oxidize a fatty acid to carbon dioxide and water.

Like glucose, the fatty acids released in the digestion of triglycerides and other lipids are broken down in a series of sequential reactions accompanied by the gradual release of usable energy. Some of these reactions are oxidative and require nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD). The enzymes that participate in fatty acid catabolism are located in the mitochondria, along with the enzymes of the citric acid cycle, the electron transport chain, and oxidative phosphorylation. This localization of enzymes in the mitochondria is of the utmost importance because it facilitates efficient utilization of energy stored in fatty acids and other molecules.

Fatty acid oxidation is initiated on the outer mitochondrial membrane. There the fatty acids, which like carbohydrates are relatively inert, must first be activated by conversion to an energy-rich fatty acid derivative of coenzyme A called fatty acyl-coenzyme A (CoA). The activation is catalyzed by acyl-CoA synthetase. For each molecule of fatty acid activated, one molecule of coenzyme A and one molecule of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) are used, equaling a net utilization of the two high-energy bonds in one ATP molecule (which is therefore converted to adenosine monophosphate [AMP] rather than adenosine diphosphate [ADP]):

The fatty acyl-CoA diffuses to the inner mitochondrial membrane, where it combines with a carrier molecule known as carnitine in a reaction catalyzed by carnitine acyltransferase. The acyl-carnitine derivative is transported into the mitochondrial matrix and converted back to the fatty acyl-CoA.

Where the Fatty Acid Goes Next

Once the fatty acyl group is inside the mitochondrial matrix, it is broken down two carbons at a time by β-oxidation. The four reactions, the repetition count and the ATP tally are taught in Section 15.3; what the liver does with surplus acetyl CoA (ketone bodies) is in Section 15.7.

Digesting fats

About 98% of dietary lipids are triacylglycerols (TAG), the triesters of glycerol and three fatty acids from Chapter 9. Excess energy from carbohydrates and proteins is also stored as TAG in adipocytes (fat cells). TAGs are insoluble in water, so the water-soluble enzymes of saliva have little effect on them, and only about 10% are hydrolyzed by gastric lipase in the stomach. Digesting the rest is a two-part problem: the fat globules must be broken into small particles so that a water-soluble enzyme can reach them, and then the ester bonds must be hydrolyzed.

  • Emulsification. Bile salts, made in the liver from cholesterol and secreted from the gallbladder into the small intestine, act like soaps: a steroid ring that dissolves in fat plus an ionic group that dissolves in water. They break the fat globules into small droplets, giving a water-soluble enzyme far more fat surface to reach. (After lipase acts, bile salts and the products form even smaller clusters called micelles, which carry them to the intestinal cell.) No bonds are broken yet; this is a physical change.
  • Incomplete hydrolysis. Pancreatic lipase (a hydrolase) hydrolyzes the ester linkages between glycerol and the fatty acid units. In the intestine this hydrolysis is incomplete: it removes the two outer fatty acids and leaves a monoacylglycerol. Set up the table: reaction type, hydrolysis (water splits an ester); how can you tell, an ester became a carboxylic acid plus an alcohol; enzyme class, hydrolase; coenzyme, none. Compare saponification in Chapter 9: same ester hydrolysis, but at body pH with an enzyme instead of hot NaOH.
  • Repackaging and absorption. Micelles of free fatty acids and monoacylglycerols pass through the membranes of intestinal cells. Inside, the fatty acids recombine with the monoacylglycerols to re-form TAG, which is coated with phospholipids and proteins to make chylomicrons, water-soluble particles that carry TAG through the lymphatic system into the bloodstream (Section 15.5).
  • Storage, then complete hydrolysis. Stored TAG sits in adipocytes, whose cytoplasm is mostly one large fat droplet, beneath the skin and around the organs. When carbohydrate cannot meet energy needs, the hormones glucagon and epinephrine activate a lipase in adipose tissue that completely hydrolyzes stored TAG to glycerol + 3 fatty acids. The fatty acids bind the plasma protein albumin for transport; glycerol dissolves in blood and goes to the liver (Section 15.3). Section 15.6 covers storage and mobilization in detail.

Activation and the carnitine shuttle

Activation. A carboxylic acid is unreactive, so the first job is to convert it into a thioester. Acyl CoA synthetase (a ligase) on the outer mitochondrial membrane joins the fatty acid to coenzyme A:

R–COO− + CoA–SH + ATP → R–CO–S–CoA + AMP + PPi

ATP is split to AMP, not ADP, and the pyrophosphate (PPi) is then hydrolyzed to two phosphates. Two high-energy bonds are spent, so we count the cost as 2 ATP-equivalents per fatty acid, no matter how long the chain.

The carnitine shuttle. Acyl CoA is too large to pass through the inner mitochondrial membrane to the matrix, so it needs a shuttle. Carnitine acyltransferase catalyzes both acyl transfers: on the outside it moves the acyl group from CoA onto carnitine, a small carrier molecule; acyl-carnitine crosses the membrane; and on the inside the same kind of enzyme gives the acyl group back to a mitochondrial CoA. Both transfers are transferase reactions. The shuttle is also a control point: malonyl CoA, the three-carbon unit used to build fatty acids (Section 15.8), blocks the transport of acyl groups into the matrix, so a cell that is making fat does not burn it at the same time.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Carnitine and MCT oil. Patients with an inherited carnitine deficiency, or with a defect in carnitine acyltransferase, cannot move long-chain fatty acids into the mitochondria. They do fine after a meal, when glucose is available, but become hypoglycemic, weak, and sometimes dangerously ill when they fast or get a fever, because the fat they mobilize cannot be burned and the liver cannot spare glucose. Part of the dietary management is medium-chain triglyceride (MCT) oil: 8- and 10-carbon fatty acids are small enough to enter the matrix without the shuttle, so they can be activated and oxidized inside. The same shortcut is why MCT oil appears in some tube-feeding and ketogenic-diet formulas. A nurse who knows where the shuttle sits can explain why skipping meals is the danger, not the fat itself.
