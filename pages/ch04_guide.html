Learning Objectives

  • Identify and name alcohols, phenols, ethers, and thiols.
  • Classify alcohols as primary (1°), secondary (2°), or tertiary (3°).
  • Use IMFs to compare boiling points and water solubility of alcohols, ethers, and hydrocarbons.
  • Predict products of dehydration and oxidation of alcohols, and oxidation of thiols to disulfides.

Key Ideas

1. The functional groups

GroupGeneral formName endingExample
AlcoholR–OH (OH on an sp3 carbon)-olCH3CH2OH, ethanol
PhenolOH directly on a benzene ringphenolC6H5OH
EtherR–O–R′"alkyl alkyl ether" (common)CH3CH2OCH2CH3, diethyl ether
ThiolR–SH-thiolCH3SH, methanethiol

All four groups have an oxygen or sulfur single-bonded to carbon. If you see a C=O, it is a different functional group (Chapters 5 and 6). The suffix or prefix tells you which family you have: -ol = alcohol, -thiol = thiol, phenol = the parent name for an OH on a benzene ring, and -oxy = the ether prefix.

Naming alcohols. Use the same checklist every time:

  1. Find the longest carbon chain that contains the carbon bonded to the OH. A longer chain that does not include the OH carbon is not the parent.
  2. Decide whether that chain is an alkane, alkene, or alkyne, then remove the final -e and add -ol.
  3. Number the chain to give the OH carbon the lower number.
  4. Name and number the other substituents (alkyl groups and halogens) as prefixes.

Example: CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3 is 2-butanol (butan-2-ol); CH3CH(OH)CH2CHClCH3 is 4-chloro-2-pentanol. Two OH groups give a -diol, and here the -e stays: 1,2-ethanediol (ethylene glycol). For a cycloalkanol (a cyclic alcohol) the OH carbon is automatically C1 and the "1-" is usually omitted; number around the ring to give the next substituent the lower number: 3-methylcyclohexanol.

Naming phenols. Phenol is the IUPAC name for an OH bonded to a benzene ring. Assign C1 to the ring carbon holding the OH, number the ring to give the other substituents the lowest numbers, and use either numbers or the ortho/meta/para prefixes from Chapter 3: 3-methylphenol is m-cresol. Do not name a phenol as a "hydroxybenzene" or as a "cyclohexenol"; the parent word is phenol.

Naming ethers. Ethers get two kinds of names.

  • Common name: name both R groups, arrange them alphabetically, and follow with the separate word "ether": CH3OCH2CH3 is ethyl methyl ether. If both groups are the same, use "di": diethyl ether.
  • IUPAC name: the longer R group is the parent chain and the shorter R group, together with its oxygen, is a substituent on that chain called an alkoxy group. Instead of the -yl ending, use -oxy: methoxy (CH3O–), ethoxy (CH3CH2O–). CH3OCH2CH3 is methoxyethane; CH3CH2OCH2CH2CH3 is 1-ethoxypropane; CH3OCH(CH3)CH2CH3 is 2-methoxybutane. Number the chain so the alkoxy group gets the lowest number.

Naming thiols. Thiols are named like alcohols: name the parent carbon chain, keep the full alkane name (do not drop the -e), add the suffix -thiol, and number the chain to give the SH the lowest number: CH3CH2SH is ethanethiol, CH3CH(SH)CH2CH3 is 2-butanethiol, (CH3)2CHCH2SH is 2-methyl-1-propanethiol. Common names use "mercaptan" (ethyl mercaptan), a word you will still see on gas-odorant and safety labels. Thiols are responsible for the smells of onions, garlic, skunk spray, and the methanethiol added to natural gas so leaks can be smelled.

Branched alkyl substituents. When a substituent is attached through a middle carbon, you will see common prefixes instead of a systematic name: n- (normal, unbranched), iso- (a Y-shaped end, two CH3 groups on the same carbon), sec- (attached through a 2° carbon), and tert- (attached through a 3° carbon). Isopropyl alcohol (2-propanol) and tert-butyl alcohol (2-methyl-2-propanol) are the two you will hear most.

Keep an eye out for isomers. Alcohols with three or more carbons have constitutional isomers: skeletal (different carbon-chain connectivity, such as 1-pentanol and 3-methyl-1-butanol), positional (same chain, OH in a different place, such as 1-pentanol and 2-pentanol), and functional group (an alcohol and an ether with the same formula; see Key Idea 7).

2. Classifying alcohols

Alcohols are classified by the number of carbon atoms bonded to the carbon that holds the OH group. A primary (1°) alcohol has the OH on a carbon bonded to only one other carbon, R–CH2–OH. A secondary (2°) alcohol has the OH on a carbon bonded to two other carbons, R–CH(R)–OH. A tertiary (3°) alcohol has the OH on a carbon bonded to three other carbons, R–CR(R)–OH. The class decides what happens in the oxidation reaction of Key Idea 4, so classify first and predict second.

Professor Tip

Go to the carbon the alcohol is connected to, then count the C–C bonds on that carbon. 1 carbon = 1°, 2 carbons = 2°, 3 carbons = 3°. Do not count the oxygen, and do not count carbons anywhere else in the molecule.

3. Physical properties

Start with the geometry. The oxygen of an alcohol or ether has four electron groups (two bonds and two lone pairs), so its electron-group geometry is tetrahedral, its molecular geometry is bent, and the C–O–H (or C–O–C) angle is about 109.5°. Two polar bonds in a bent shape do not cancel, so every alcohol, phenol, ether, and thiol is a polar molecule. What differs is whether the molecule can hydrogen bond.

  • Alcohols and phenols have an H bonded to O, so they hydrogen bond with each other: the H on one oxygen is attracted to a lone pair on another oxygen. Hydrogen bonding is the strongest intermolecular force in this chapter and requires an H bonded to O, N, or F.
  • Ethers and thiols are polar, so they have dipole–dipole forces, but with no H on the O or S they cannot hydrogen bond with each other. (S–H is barely polar, so thiols are weak even at dipole–dipole; they are known for strong odors, not strong attractions.) Ethers can hydrogen bond to an R–OH or to water, because the ether oxygen's lone pairs accept the H.
  • Solubility in water. Alcohols, phenols, and ethers can all hydrogen bond with water. Alcohols and phenols are both hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors; ethers are acceptors only, so an ether is less soluble than an alcohol with a similar alkyl group. How much dissolves depends on the length of the alkyl chain: about 4 carbons or fewer, soluble; longer chains, increasingly insoluble. Each extra OH increases solubility. A rule of thumb from the lecture: if a molecule's intermolecular forces are similar to water's, it dissolves in water.
  • Boiling and melting points. Boiling point increases with the size of the alkyl chain (more dispersion forces). For molecules of similar molecular weight, first look at which intermolecular forces are possible, then look at the shape (straight chains pack and attract better than branched ones). Stronger forces = higher melting and boiling points. Straight-chain alcohols with fewer than about 12 carbons are liquids at room temperature.

Ranking example. The cleanest comparison is three molecules of nearly the same mass with different functional groups. The table below lists boiling points for a 44–46 g/mol trio; read it with the question "can this molecule hydrogen bond with itself?"

Ranking by boiling point: alkane < ether < alcohol at similar mass. The ether sits only slightly above the alkane; the alcohol sits far above both. Ethanethiol, although heavier than ethanol, boils more than 40 °C lower because S–H hydrogen bonds are very weak. Water solubility follows a different question, "can this molecule hydrogen bond with water?", so ethers and alcohols of the same size are closer together in solubility than in boiling point (see Example 4).

4. Reactions

Alcohols do three things in this course: they burn (combustion), they lose water (dehydration), and they lose hydrogen (oxidation). Thiols do one: they oxidize to disulfides.

Combustion. Like hydrocarbons, alcohols burn in oxygen to give carbon dioxide and water: C2H5OH + 3 O2 → 2 CO2 + 3 H2O. Balance it the same way you balanced hydrocarbon combustion in Chapter 2, remembering that the alcohol already contributes one oxygen atom. Ethanol burns with a clean, nearly invisible flame, which is why it is used in fondue burners and lab alcohol lamps.

Dehydration (strong acid catalyst and heat): water is eliminated from an alcohol to form an alkene. The OH leaves from one carbon and an H leaves from a neighboring carbon; the double bond forms between them. You already know the reverse reaction: in hydration (Chapter 3 and the lab), water adds to an alkene to form an alcohol. Dehydration is an elimination reaction, hydration is an addition reaction, and the two are related the same way oxidation and reduction are.

Symmetric vs. asymmetric alcohols. If the two carbons next to the OH carbon are identical (3-pentanol, cyclohexanol), only one alkene is possible. If they are different, two alkenes can form, and you need Zaitsev's rule (also spelled Saytzeff): the alkene formed in the greatest amount is the one made by removing the hydrogen from the neighboring carbon that has the fewest hydrogens. Put another way, the major product has the most alkyl groups bonded to the C=C: the more substituted alkene wins. Notice that this is the opposite of Markovnikov's rule for addition, where the H goes to the carbon that already has more hydrogens. Count the alkyl groups on each possible C=C to decide.

Example: Zaitsev's rule with two possible alkenes

Problem. Predict the major and minor products when 2-butanol, CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3, is dehydrated with H2SO4 and heat.

Solution. The OH is on C2. Its neighbors are C1 (a CH3, three H atoms) and C3 (a CH2, two H atoms). Removing an H from C3 gives CH3CH=CHCH3, 2-butene, whose double bond carries two alkyl groups. Removing an H from C1 gives CH2=CHCH2CH3, 1-butene, whose double bond carries only one alkyl group. Zaitsev's rule: 2-butene is major, 1-butene is minor.

Check. Both products are C4H8, and each is 2-butanol (C4H10O) minus H2O. Notice that C3 had fewer hydrogens to give up yet still produced the major product; it is the substitution of the alkene, not the number of available H atoms, that decides.

Oxidation. In organic chemistry an oxidation is an increase in the number of C–O bonds and/or a decrease in the number of C–H bonds. The symbol [O] over the arrow stands for any oxidizing agent, such as potassium dichromate, K2Cr2O7. The alcohol loses the H from its O–H and an H from the OH carbon, and a C=O forms, so the class of the alcohol decides the outcome:

  • Primary alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid. The aldehyde still has an H on its C=O carbon, so with excess [O] it is oxidized a second time.
  • Secondary alcohol → ketone. The ketone carbon has no H left, so the reaction stops there.
  • Tertiary alcohol → no reaction. There is no H bonded to the C–OH carbon to remove.

Reduction, written [H], is the reverse: an aldehyde goes back to a 1° alcohol and a ketone back to a 2° alcohol (Chapter 5). Oxidation/reduction and elimination/addition are the two reversible pairs you should be able to run in either direction.

Thiol oxidation and reduction: 2 R–SH → R–S–S–R + 2 [H] (a disulfide). Two thiol molecules lose their S–H hydrogens and the sulfur atoms join. Disulfides are reduced back to thiols under reducing conditions ([H]). This same S–S bond holds proteins in shape (Chapter 10). When hair is "permed," the disulfide bonds in the hair are first reduced to thiols, the hair is wrapped on curlers, and the thiols are then oxidized to new disulfide bonds that lock in the curl.

5. Acidity: alcohols vs. phenols

  • Alcohol solutions have about the same pH as water, so for our purposes alcohols are neutral. They do not react with NaOH.
  • Phenols are more acidic than alcohols in water: a phenol solution has pH below 7. Phenol is a weak acid (pKa about 10) because the benzene ring stabilizes the negative charge left behind when the O–H loses H+, so phenol reacts with NaOH: C6H5OH + NaOH → C6H5O−Na+ (sodium phenoxide) + H2O. This difference is one way to tell alcohols and phenols apart in lab.
  • Thiols are slightly more acidic than alcohols (pKa about 10–11) but, like alcohols, they are far weaker acids than carboxylic acids (Chapter 6).

Section 4.10 works through the acidity comparison in detail, including the pKa table and the phenol/cyclohexanol NaOH test.

6. Hydration and dehydration are reversible

Hydration (Chapter 3) and dehydration (this chapter) are the same equilibrium read in opposite directions. For propene and 2-propanol:

CH3CH=CH2 + H2O ⇌ CH3CH(OH)CH3   (H+ catalyst; the same acid catalyzes both directions)

By Le Chatelier's principle, a system at equilibrium shifts to relieve whatever stress you apply:

  • Toward the alcohol (hydration): run the reaction in excess water with dilute acid at moderate temperature. Adding a reactant (water) pushes the equilibrium to the right.
  • Toward the alkene (dehydration): use concentrated acid (which contains very little water), heat strongly, and distill off the low-boiling alkene or absorb the water as it forms. Removing a product pulls the equilibrium to the left, and because dehydration absorbs heat (it is endothermic), heating favors it as well.

So "H+, heat, concentrated H2SO4" on an exam signals dehydration, while "H+, H2O" signals hydration.

7. Functional group isomers

Isomers can have entirely different functional groups. Ethanol (CH3CH2OH) and dimethyl ether (CH3OCH3) are both C2H6O. Alongside skeletal isomers (different carbon chains) and positional isomers (1-propanol vs. 2-propanol), these complete the three types of constitutional isomers. The formula C4H10O shows all three at once:

  • Positional: 1-butanol and 2-butanol have the same chain; only the OH position differs.
  • Skeletal: 2-methyl-1-propanol has a branched chain, so it is a skeletal isomer of both butanols.
  • Functional group: diethyl ether and methyl propyl ether put the oxygen inside the chain. They are ethers, with no O–H, so they boil far lower than the alcohols and have different chemistry (no oxidation, no dehydration).

Ask two questions to classify a pair: "Same functional group?" (no → functional group isomers), then "Same carbon skeleton?" (no → skeletal; yes → positional).

8. Glycols and glycerol

A glycol is a diol with OH groups on adjacent carbons. The two you will meet most often are ethylene glycol (1,2-ethanediol, HOCH2CH2OH) and propylene glycol (1,2-propanediol). Glycerol (glycerin; 1,2,3-propanetriol, HOCH2CH(OH)CH2OH) is a triol. Each extra OH adds more hydrogen bonding, so these compounds are thick, syrupy liquids that mix with water in any proportion and boil far above single alcohols of similar size (ethanol 78 °C, ethylene glycol 197 °C, glycerol 290 °C). Those properties explain their uses: ethylene glycol as antifreeze and coolant, propylene glycol and glycerol as moisture-holding ingredients in foods, cosmetics, and medicines. Glycerol is also the backbone of fats and oils (Chapter 9).

Ethylene glycol toxicity. Ethylene glycol tastes sweet and is not very toxic by itself; the danger is what the body makes from it. The same liver enzyme that oxidizes ethanol, alcohol dehydrogenase, oxidizes each 1° OH group of ethylene glycol through aldehydes to acids, ending in oxalic acid. Oxalate precipitates with calcium as calcium oxalate crystals in the kidneys, and the acids cause metabolic acidosis (blood pH below normal). Propylene glycol, by contrast, is oxidized to lactic and pyruvic acids, both normal metabolites, which is why it is the glycol allowed in foods and medicines.

Health Career Connection

  • Nursing – antiseptics: 70% isopropyl alcohol (2-propanol) is the standard skin prep before injections. The water in the mixture helps the alcohol get into bacterial cells and denature their proteins.
  • Nursing – alcohol and methanol poisoning: The liver oxidizes ethanol (1°) to acetaldehyde, then to acetic acid, exactly the 1° alcohol oxidation pattern. Methanol is oxidized the same way to formaldehyde and formic acid, which are toxic and can cause blindness. Treatment blocks the first oxidation step (see the Nursing box below).
  • Dental hygiene – halitosis and phenols: Bad breath is largely thiol chemistry, and several dental antiseptics are phenols (see the Dental Hygiene box below).
  • Dental – triclosan: Triclosan, an antibacterial once common in toothpastes, is a chlorinated phenol that also contains an ether link. That one molecule shows two functional groups from this chapter. (It has since been removed from most U.S. toothpastes.)

Clinical Connection: Nursing

One enzyme, three poisons. Ethanol is absorbed in the stomach and small intestine, and in the liver the enzymes alcohol dehydrogenase and aldehyde dehydrogenase act as the oxidizing agents. (A breathalyzer runs the same oxidation chemically, using the color change of an acidic dichromate reagent, orange to green, to measure the ethanol in exhaled breath.) Alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) treats ethanol, methanol, and ethylene glycol as the same kind of molecule: a 1° alcohol to be oxidized to an aldehyde and then an acid. Ethanol's products (acetaldehyde, acetic acid) are handled routinely. Methanol's (formaldehyde, formic acid) damage the optic nerve; ethylene glycol's (ending in oxalic acid) cause metabolic acidosis and calcium oxalate crystals in the kidneys. The antidote concept follows from the chemistry: give ADH something else to work on. Fomepizole blocks the enzyme, and ethanol itself is a competitive substrate that keeps ADH busy while the unchanged methanol or glycol is cleared. (Concept only; dosing belongs to pharmacology.)

Strength labels. "Proof" is twice the percent alcohol by volume: 80-proof spirits are 40% ethanol. Isopropyl alcohol swabs are usually 70% because that water content helps the alcohol enter and denature bacterial proteins. Phenol was the original surgical antiseptic (Lister, 1860s); today phenolic rinses compete with chlorhexidine, an amine-containing antiseptic (Chapter 7). Propofol, a common IV anesthetic, is a substituted phenol; glycerin (glycerol) is the water-holding base of suppositories and lubricating gels.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Phenols in the operatory. Eugenol, the phenol from clove oil, is mixed with zinc oxide to make zinc oxide–eugenol (ZOE) temporary cement and dressings; its mild antiseptic and soothing action comes with the phenol group. Thymol, another phenol, is one of the essential oils in antiseptic mouthrinses (with eucalyptol, an ether, and menthol, an alcohol). Chlorhexidine rinse is the stronger prescription alternative but can stain teeth.

Sugar alcohols. Xylitol and sorbitol are polyols, molecules with an OH on nearly every carbon. They taste sweet, yet plaque bacteria cannot ferment them to acid, so they are noncariogenic (do not cause dental caries, tooth decay). Glycerin in toothpaste is a humectant, a polyol that holds water so the paste does not dry out.

Halitosis is thiol chemistry. Anaerobic bacteria on the tongue and in periodontal pockets break down sulfur-containing proteins into volatile sulfur compounds: methanethiol (CH3SH), hydrogen sulfide (H2S), and dimethyl sulfide. The low polarity and weak IMFs you learned for thiols are exactly why these molecules escape into the breath. Zinc-containing rinses work by binding the thiol sulfur as a nonvolatile zinc salt.

Further reading: M. Epple and J. Enax, "The Chemistry of Dental Care," ChemistryViews (Part 2).

In the Lab

  • Experiment 4 – Alcohols and Phenols
  • Solubility: alcohols with four or fewer carbons dissolve in water; longer ones do not. Phenol is only moderately soluble in water (its OH hydrogen bonds, but the ring is nonpolar), yet it dissolves completely in NaOH solution because it is converted to the ionic phenoxide salt.
  • Chromate oxidation test: orange chromium(VI) (dichromate, Cr2O72−, in the acidic reagent) turns green (Cr3+) when it oxidizes a 1° or 2° alcohol. A 3° alcohol cannot be oxidized, so the solution stays orange.
  • NaOH test: phenol dissolves in 5% NaOH (it is neutralized to sodium phenoxide); an alcohol such as cyclohexanol does not react and stays as a separate layer.
  • Ferric chloride (FeCl3) test: phenols form strongly colored complexes with Fe3+ (from yellow to purple). Ordinary alcohols do not.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Name and classify

Problem. Name and classify CH3CH(OH)CH2CH2CH3.

Solution. Five-carbon chain, numbered so the OH is on C2: 2-pentanol. The OH carbon is bonded to 2 other carbons, so it is a secondary (2°) alcohol.

Check. Numbering from the other end would put the OH on C4; 2 is lower, so 2-pentanol is correct.

Example 2: Oxidation

Problem. Give the oxidation product(s) of (a) 1-propanol, (b) cyclohexanol, (c) 2-methyl-2-butanol.

Solution.

(a) 1°: CH3CH2CH2OH → CH3CH2CHO (propanal) → CH3CH2COOH (propanoic acid)

(b) 2°: cyclohexanol → cyclohexanone

(c) 3°: no reaction

Check. Classify first, then predict: 1° goes two steps, 2° stops at the ketone, 3° has no H on the OH carbon to lose.

Example 3: Dehydration

Problem. What is the major product when 3-methyl-2-butanol, CH3CH(OH)CH(CH3)CH3, is dehydrated?

Solution. H can be removed from C1 (giving 3-methyl-1-butene) or from C3 (giving 2-methyl-2-butene). 2-Methyl-2-butene, (CH3)2C=CHCH3, has the more substituted double bond and is major.

Check. Count alkyl groups on each C=C: 2-methyl-2-butene has three, 3-methyl-1-butene has one. Both are C5H10, the alcohol minus H2O.

Example 4: Comparing boiling points and solubility

Problem. Compare the boiling points and water solubility of 1-butanol, diethyl ether, and pentane (all about 72–74 g/mol).

Solution.

Boiling point: Pentane (36 °C) and diethyl ether (35 °C) boil at nearly the same temperature. Neither can hydrogen bond with itself, and the ether's weak dipole adds very little. 1-Butanol (118 °C) is far higher because its O–H groups hydrogen bond with each other.

Solubility: Diethyl ether and 1-butanol are both moderately soluble (about 7 g/100 mL) because the oxygen in each can accept hydrogen bonds from water. Pentane has no polar group and is insoluble.

Check. Hydrogen bonding with itself controls boiling point. Hydrogen bonding with water controls solubility.

Example 5: Naming an ether and a thiol both ways

Problem. Give a common name and an IUPAC name for CH3OCH2CH2CH3, and name CH3CH2CH(SH)CH3.

Solution. The ether has a methyl group and a propyl group on oxygen. Common name: list them alphabetically, then "ether": methyl propyl ether. IUPAC: the longer chain (propane) is the parent and CH3O– is a methoxy substituent on C1: 1-methoxypropane. The thiol has a four-carbon chain; number from the end nearer the SH so it lands on C2: 2-butanethiol.

Check. Ether common names end in a separate word "ether"; IUPAC ether names contain "-oxy" and no "ether" at all. A thiol name keeps the "e" of the alkane (butane + thiol), unlike an alcohol name (butan + ol).

Example 6: Ranking by boiling point and solubility

Problem. Rank from lowest to highest boiling point: 1-propanol, ethyl methyl ether, 1,2-propanediol, and butane. Then state which are soluble in water.

Solution. All four have 58–76 g/mol, so look at the IMFs. Butane: dispersion only. Ethyl methyl ether: a weak dipole, no O–H. 1-Propanol: one O–H, hydrogen bonding. 1,2-Propanediol: two O–H groups, more hydrogen bonding per molecule. Ranking: butane < ethyl methyl ether < 1-propanol < 1,2-propanediol (actual values: −1, 8, 97, and 188 °C). Solubility: the ether, the alcohol, and the diol all have oxygen atoms that accept hydrogen bonds from water and short carbon chains, so all three are soluble (the diol and 1-propanol are miscible). Butane is insoluble.

Check. Each added O–H group raised the boiling point by roughly 90 °C in this series, a bigger effect than the few grams per mole of mass difference, which is the sign that hydrogen bonding dominates.

Example 7: Name from structure, structure from name

Problem. (a) Name a benzene ring that carries an OH on one carbon, a chlorine on the next carbon around the ring, and a methyl group on the carbon directly across from the OH. (b) Name a cyclopentane ring with an OH on one carbon and an ethyl group two carbons away. (c) Draw the condensed structural formula of 2,2-dimethyl-1-propanol.

Solution. (a) An OH directly on a benzene ring makes the parent phenol, so the OH carbon is C1. Number toward the nearer substituent: Cl on C2, CH3 on C4. Prefixes in alphabetical order: 2-chloro-4-methylphenol. (b) A cyclic alcohol is a cycloalkanol; the OH carbon is C1 and the "1-" is omitted. Count around the ring the short way to the ethyl group: C3. 3-ethylcyclopentanol. (c) "Propanol" means a three-carbon chain with the OH on C1; "2,2-dimethyl" puts two CH3 groups on C2: HOCH2C(CH3)3, a 1° alcohol with a tert-butyl group (its common name is neopentyl alcohol).

Check. In (a), numbering the other way would put Cl on C6, so 2 is correct; "2-chloro-4-methylphenol" also beats any name that calls the ring a "hydroxybenzene" or a "cyclohexenol." In (b), going the long way would give 4-ethyl; 3 is lower. In (c), the OH carbon in HOCH2C(CH3)3 is bonded to only one carbon, so the alcohol is 1° even though the neighboring carbon is quaternary.

Example 8: Combustion of an alcohol

Problem. Write and balance the equation for the complete combustion of 2-propanol, C3H7OH.

Solution. Products are always CO2 and H2O. Balance C first (3 CO2), then H (8 H → 4 H2O), then O. The right side has 6 + 4 = 10 O atoms; the alcohol supplies 1, so O2 must supply 9, which is 4½ O2. Double everything to clear the fraction:

2 C3H7OH + 9 O2 → 6 CO2 + 8 H2O

Check. C: 6 = 6. H: 16 = 16. O: 2 + 18 = 20 on the left; 12 + 8 = 20 on the right. The one difference from hydrocarbon combustion is the oxygen atom already in the alcohol; forgetting it is the most common error.

Example 9: Which reagent makes which product?

Problem. Starting from cyclohexanol, which set of conditions gives (a) cyclohexanone, (b) cyclohexene, (c) CO2 and H2O? Choose from: [O]; H+ and heat; O2 and a flame.

Solution. (a) A C=O in place of the C–OH is an oxidation: [O]. Cyclohexanol is a 2° alcohol, so the product is the ketone and the reaction stops there. (b) Losing H and OH from neighboring carbons to make a C=C is dehydration, an elimination: H+ and heat. Cyclohexanol is symmetric, so only one alkene (cyclohexene) is possible. (c) Complete breakdown to CO2 and H2O is combustion: O2 and a flame.

Check. Read the product first, then pick the reagent: a new C=O means [O]; a new C=C and a lost water means H+/heat; CO2 means burning. Reverse the arrows and you have the Chapter 3 and Chapter 5 reactions: hydration (H2O, H+) turns cyclohexene back into cyclohexanol, and reduction ([H]) turns cyclohexanone back into cyclohexanol.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Choosing a longer carbon chain that does not contain the OH carbon as the parent. The parent chain must include the carbon bonded to the OH, even if a longer chain exists elsewhere.
  • Naming a phenol as "hydroxybenzene" or "cyclohexenol." The parent name is phenol, and the OH carbon is C1.
  • Counting the oxygen when classifying an alcohol. Only count carbons on the C–OH carbon.
  • Oxidizing a 2° alcohol "all the way" to a carboxylic acid. Ketones stop.
  • Claiming ethers hydrogen bond with each other. They have no O–H.
  • Calling C6H5CH2OH a phenol. The OH is on a CH2, not on the ring, so it is an alcohol (benzyl alcohol).
  • Choosing the Zaitsev product by counting which neighbor has more hydrogens. Count the alkyl groups on the product's double bond instead; the more substituted alkene wins.
  • Dropping the "e" in thiol names ("butanthiol"). It is butanethiol; only -ol replaces the -e.
  • Forgetting the oxygen inside the alcohol when balancing a combustion equation. Count it before you count the O2.
  • Writing an IUPAC ether name with the alkoxy group on the longer chain. The shorter group becomes the alkoxy prefix; the longer chain is the parent (1-methoxypropane, not propoxymethane).
  • Treating ethylene glycol as harmless because it is "just a diol." Its oxidation products, not the glycol itself, are the toxic species.

Ethyl methyl ether (alkyl groups in alphabetical order)

Exercise 3

What alcohol would you oxidize to make butanone, CH3COCH2CH3?

Answer
2-butanol, CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3 (a 2° alcohol gives a ketone)

Exercise 4

Write the product when two molecules of ethanethiol are oxidized.

Answer
CH3CH2–S–S–CH2CH3 (diethyl disulfide)

Exercise 5

Which is more soluble in water: 1-hexanol or 1,2-propanediol? Why?

Answer
1,2-Propanediol. It has two OH groups for hydrogen bonding and a much smaller nonpolar chain (it is fully miscible with water).

Exercise 6

Which reacts with aqueous NaOH: cyclohexanol or phenol? Write the reaction.

Answer
Phenol: C6H5OH + NaOH → C6H5O−Na+ + H2O. Cyclohexanol is an alcohol and does not react.

Exercise 7

Classify each pair as skeletal, positional, or functional group isomers: (a) 1-butanol and 2-butanol; (b) 1-butanol and diethyl ether.

Answer
(a) Positional; (b) functional group isomers (both C4H10O)
