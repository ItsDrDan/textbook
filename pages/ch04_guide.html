Learning Objectives

  • Identify and name alcohols, phenols, ethers, and thiols.
  • Classify alcohols as primary (1°), secondary (2°), or tertiary (3°).
  • Use IMFs to compare boiling points and water solubility of alcohols, ethers, and hydrocarbons.
  • Predict products of dehydration and oxidation of alcohols, and oxidation of thiols to disulfides.

Key Ideas

1. The functional groups

GroupGeneral formName endingExample
AlcoholR–OH (OH on an sp3 carbon)-olCH3CH2OH, ethanol
PhenolOH directly on a benzene ringphenolC6H5OH
EtherR–O–R′"alkyl alkyl ether" (common)CH3CH2OCH2CH3, diethyl ether
ThiolR–SH-thiolCH3SH, methanethiol

Naming alcohols: Find the longest chain containing the carbon bonded to OH, number so the OH carbon gets the lowest number, and replace the final -e with -ol: 2-butanol (butan-2-ol). Two OH groups give a -diol (1,2-ethanediol is ethylene glycol). Halogen and alkyl substituents are listed as prefixes with their own numbers: 3-chloro-1-butanol.

Naming phenols: The parent is phenol, and the ring carbon holding the OH is C1. Number toward the nearest substituent: 3-methylphenol (also called m-cresol). The ortho/meta/para prefixes from Chapter 3 work the same way here.

Naming ethers: Ethers get two kinds of names.

  • Common name: name the two groups on the oxygen in alphabetical order, then add "ether": CH3OCH2CH3 is ethyl methyl ether. If both groups are the same, use "di": diethyl ether.
  • IUPAC name: treat the smaller group plus its oxygen as an alkoxy substituent (methoxy, CH3O–; ethoxy, CH3CH2O–) on the longer alkane chain: CH3OCH2CH3 is methoxyethane; CH3CH2OCH2CH2CH3 is 1-ethoxypropane. Number the chain so the alkoxy group gets the lowest number.

Naming thiols: Keep the full alkane name (do not drop the -e) and add -thiol, numbering so the SH carbon is as low as possible: CH3CH2SH is ethanethiol, CH3CH(SH)CH2CH3 is 2-butanethiol. Common names use "mercaptan" (ethyl mercaptan), a word you will still see on gas-odorant and safety labels.

2. Classifying alcohols

Professor Tip

Go to the carbon that holds the OH, then count how many carbons are attached to that carbon. 1 carbon = 1°, 2 carbons = 2°, 3 carbons = 3°. Do not count the oxygen.

3. Physical properties

  • Alcohols hydrogen bond with each other (O–H), so they have high boiling points for their size.
  • Ethers have no O–H, so they cannot hydrogen bond with each other (dipole–dipole only). Their boiling points are close to those of alkanes of similar size. They can still accept hydrogen bonds from water.
  • Solubility: Alcohols with about 1–4 carbons mix with water. As the nonpolar carbon chain grows, solubility drops. Each extra OH increases solubility.
  • Thiols have weak IMFs (S–H is barely polar, so thiols do not hydrogen bond well) and are known for strong, unpleasant odors.

Ranking example. The cleanest comparison is three molecules of nearly the same mass with different functional groups. The table below lists boiling points for a 44–46 g/mol trio; read it with the question "can this molecule hydrogen bond with itself?"

CH3OCH3, dimethyl etherether46dipole–dipole−24 °C
CH3CH2OH, ethanolalcohol46hydrogen bonding78 °C
CH3CH2SH, ethanethiolthiol62dipole–dipole (weak)35 °C

Ranking by boiling point: alkane < ether < alcohol at similar mass. The ether sits only slightly above the alkane; the alcohol sits far above both. Ethanethiol, although heavier than ethanol, boils more than 40 °C lower because S–H hydrogen bonds are very weak. Water solubility follows a different question, "can this molecule hydrogen bond with water?", so ethers and alcohols of the same size are closer together in solubility than in boiling point (see Example 4).

4. Reactions

Dehydration (H+, heat): removes H2O from an alcohol to make an alkene. The OH leaves from one carbon and an H leaves from a neighboring carbon; the double bond forms between them. This is an elimination reaction and the reverse of hydration from Chapter 3.

Zaitsev's rule. When the OH carbon has two different neighbors that each carry an H, two alkenes are possible. The major product is the more substituted alkene, the one whose double-bond carbons are attached to more carbon groups (and fewer H atoms). Count the alkyl groups on the C=C to decide.

Example: Zaitsev's rule with two possible alkenes

Problem. Predict the major and minor products when 2-butanol, CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3, is dehydrated with H2SO4 and heat.

Solution. The OH is on C2. Its neighbors are C1 (a CH3, three H atoms) and C3 (a CH2, two H atoms). Removing an H from C3 gives CH3CH=CHCH3, 2-butene, whose double bond carries two alkyl groups. Removing an H from C1 gives CH2=CHCH2CH3, 1-butene, whose double bond carries only one alkyl group. Zaitsev's rule: 2-butene is major, 1-butene is minor.

Check. Both products are C4H8, and each is 2-butanol (C4H10O) minus H2O. Notice that C3 had fewer hydrogens to give up yet still produced the major product; it is the substitution of the alkene, not the number of available H atoms, that decides.

Oxidation (adding O or removing H; written [O]):

  • 1° alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid
  • 2° alcohol → ketone (stops there)
  • 3° alcohol → no reaction. There is no H on the OH carbon to remove.

Thiol oxidation: 2 R–SH → R–S–S–R + 2 [H] (a disulfide). Two thiol molecules lose their S–H hydrogens and the sulfur atoms join. The reaction runs backward under reducing conditions ([H]), which splits the disulfide back into two thiols. This same S–S bond holds proteins in shape (Chapter 10), and it is the bond a permanent-wave solution breaks and re-forms in hair keratin.

5. Acidity: alcohols vs. phenols

  • Alcohols are about as acidic as water, so for our purposes they are neutral. They do not react with NaOH.
  • Phenols are weak acids (pKa about 10). The benzene ring stabilizes the negative charge left behind when the O–H loses H+, so phenol reacts with NaOH: C6H5OH + NaOH → C6H5O−Na+ (sodium phenoxide) + H2O. This difference is one way to tell alcohols and phenols apart in lab.
  • Thiols are slightly more acidic than alcohols (pKa about 10–11) but, like alcohols, they are far weaker acids than carboxylic acids (Chapter 6).

Section 4.10 works through the acidity comparison in detail, including the pKa table and the phenol/cyclohexanol NaOH test.

6. Hydration and dehydration are reversible

Hydration (Chapter 3) and dehydration (this chapter) are the same equilibrium read in opposite directions. For propene and 2-propanol:

CH3CH=CH2 + H2O ⇌ CH3CH(OH)CH3   (H+ catalyst; the same acid catalyzes both directions)

By Le Chatelier's principle, a system at equilibrium shifts to relieve whatever stress you apply:

  • Toward the alcohol (hydration): run the reaction in excess water with dilute acid at moderate temperature. Adding a reactant (water) pushes the equilibrium to the right.
  • Toward the alkene (dehydration): use concentrated acid (which contains very little water), heat strongly, and distill off the low-boiling alkene or absorb the water as it forms. Removing a product pulls the equilibrium to the left, and because dehydration absorbs heat (it is endothermic), heating favors it as well.

So "H+, heat, concentrated H2SO4" on an exam signals dehydration, while "H+, H2O" signals hydration.

7. Functional group isomers

Isomers can have entirely different functional groups. Ethanol (CH3CH2OH) and dimethyl ether (CH3OCH3) are both C2H6O. Alongside skeletal isomers (different carbon chains) and positional isomers (1-propanol vs. 2-propanol), these complete the three types of constitutional isomers. The formula C4H10O shows all three at once:

  • Positional: 1-butanol and 2-butanol have the same chain; only the OH position differs.
  • Skeletal: 2-methyl-1-propanol has a branched chain, so it is a skeletal isomer of both butanols.
  • Functional group: diethyl ether and methyl propyl ether put the oxygen inside the chain. They are ethers, with no O–H, so they boil far lower than the alcohols and have different chemistry (no oxidation, no dehydration).

Ask two questions to classify a pair: "Same functional group?" (no → functional group isomers), then "Same carbon skeleton?" (no → skeletal; yes → positional).

8. Glycols and glycerol

A glycol is a diol with OH groups on adjacent carbons. The two you will meet most often are ethylene glycol (1,2-ethanediol, HOCH2CH2OH) and propylene glycol (1,2-propanediol). Glycerol (glycerin; 1,2,3-propanetriol, HOCH2CH(OH)CH2OH) is a triol. Each extra OH adds more hydrogen bonding, so these compounds are thick, syrupy liquids that mix with water in any proportion and boil far above single alcohols of similar size (ethanol 78 °C, ethylene glycol 197 °C, glycerol 290 °C). Those properties explain their uses: ethylene glycol as antifreeze and coolant, propylene glycol and glycerol as moisture-holding ingredients in foods, cosmetics, and medicines. Glycerol is also the backbone of fats and oils (Chapter 9).

Ethylene glycol toxicity. Ethylene glycol tastes sweet and is not very toxic by itself; the danger is what the body makes from it. The same liver enzyme that oxidizes ethanol, alcohol dehydrogenase, oxidizes each 1° OH group of ethylene glycol through aldehydes to acids, ending in oxalic acid. Oxalate precipitates with calcium as calcium oxalate crystals in the kidneys, and the acids cause metabolic acidosis (blood pH below normal). Propylene glycol, by contrast, is oxidized to lactic and pyruvic acids, both normal metabolites, which is why it is the glycol allowed in foods and medicines.

Health Career Connection

  • Nursing – antiseptics: 70% isopropyl alcohol (2-propanol) is the standard skin prep before injections. The water in the mixture helps the alcohol get into bacterial cells and denature their proteins.
  • Nursing – alcohol and methanol poisoning: The liver oxidizes ethanol (1°) to acetaldehyde, then to acetic acid, exactly the 1° alcohol oxidation pattern. Methanol is oxidized the same way to formaldehyde and formic acid, which are toxic and can cause blindness. Treatment blocks the first oxidation step (see the Nursing box below).
  • Dental hygiene – halitosis and phenols: Bad breath is largely thiol chemistry, and several dental antiseptics are phenols (see the Dental Hygiene box below).
  • Dental – triclosan: Triclosan, an antibacterial once common in toothpastes, is a chlorinated phenol that also contains an ether link. That one molecule shows two functional groups from this chapter. (It has since been removed from most U.S. toothpastes.)

Clinical Connection: Nursing

One enzyme, three poisons. Alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) in the liver treats ethanol, methanol, and ethylene glycol as the same kind of molecule: a 1° alcohol to be oxidized to an aldehyde and then an acid. Ethanol's products (acetaldehyde, acetic acid) are handled routinely. Methanol's (formaldehyde, formic acid) damage the optic nerve; ethylene glycol's (ending in oxalic acid) cause metabolic acidosis and calcium oxalate crystals in the kidneys. The antidote concept follows from the chemistry: give ADH something else to work on. Fomepizole blocks the enzyme, and ethanol itself is a competitive substrate that keeps ADH busy while the unchanged methanol or glycol is cleared. (Concept only; dosing belongs to pharmacology.)

Strength labels. "Proof" is twice the percent alcohol by volume: 80-proof spirits are 40% ethanol. Isopropyl alcohol swabs are usually 70% because that water content helps the alcohol enter and denature bacterial proteins. Phenol was the original surgical antiseptic (Lister, 1860s); today phenolic rinses compete with chlorhexidine, an amine-containing antiseptic (Chapter 7). Propofol, a common IV anesthetic, is a substituted phenol; glycerin (glycerol) is the water-holding base of suppositories and lubricating gels.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Phenols in the operatory. Eugenol, the phenol from clove oil, is mixed with zinc oxide to make zinc oxide–eugenol (ZOE) temporary cement and dressings; its mild antiseptic and soothing action comes with the phenol group. Thymol, another phenol, is one of the essential oils in antiseptic mouthrinses (with eucalyptol, an ether, and menthol, an alcohol). Chlorhexidine rinse is the stronger prescription alternative but can stain teeth.

Sugar alcohols. Xylitol and sorbitol are polyols, molecules with an OH on nearly every carbon. They taste sweet, yet plaque bacteria cannot ferment them to acid, so they are noncariogenic (do not cause dental caries, tooth decay). Glycerin in toothpaste is a humectant, a polyol that holds water so the paste does not dry out.

Halitosis is thiol chemistry. Anaerobic bacteria on the tongue and in periodontal pockets break down sulfur-containing proteins into volatile sulfur compounds: methanethiol (CH3SH), hydrogen sulfide (H2S), and dimethyl sulfide. The low polarity and weak IMFs you learned for thiols are exactly why these molecules escape into the breath. Zinc-containing rinses work by binding the thiol sulfur as a nonvolatile zinc salt.

Further reading: M. Epple and J. Enax, "The Chemistry of Dental Care," ChemistryViews (Part 2).

In the Lab

  • Experiment 4 – Alcohols and Phenols
  • Solubility: alcohols with four or fewer carbons dissolve in water; longer ones do not. Phenol is only moderately soluble in water (its OH hydrogen bonds, but the ring is nonpolar), yet it dissolves completely in NaOH solution because it is converted to the ionic phenoxide salt.
  • Chromate oxidation test: orange chromium(VI) (dichromate, Cr2O72−, in the acidic reagent) turns green (Cr3+) when it oxidizes a 1° or 2° alcohol. A 3° alcohol cannot be oxidized, so the solution stays orange.
  • NaOH test: phenol dissolves in 5% NaOH (it is neutralized to sodium phenoxide); an alcohol such as cyclohexanol does not react and stays as a separate layer.
  • Ferric chloride (FeCl3) test: phenols form strongly colored complexes with Fe3+ (from yellow to purple). Ordinary alcohols do not.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Name and classify

Problem. Name and classify CH3CH(OH)CH2CH2CH3.

Solution. Five-carbon chain, numbered so the OH is on C2: 2-pentanol. The OH carbon is bonded to 2 other carbons, so it is a secondary (2°) alcohol.

Check. Numbering from the other end would put the OH on C4; 2 is lower, so 2-pentanol is correct.

Example 2: Oxidation

Problem. Give the oxidation product(s) of (a) 1-propanol, (b) cyclohexanol, (c) 2-methyl-2-butanol.

Solution.

(a) 1°: CH3CH2CH2OH → CH3CH2CHO (propanal) → CH3CH2COOH (propanoic acid)

(b) 2°: cyclohexanol → cyclohexanone

(c) 3°: no reaction

Check. Classify first, then predict: 1° goes two steps, 2° stops at the ketone, 3° has no H on the OH carbon to lose.

Example 3: Dehydration

Problem. What is the major product when 3-methyl-2-butanol, CH3CH(OH)CH(CH3)CH3, is dehydrated?

Solution. H can be removed from C1 (giving 3-methyl-1-butene) or from C3 (giving 2-methyl-2-butene). 2-Methyl-2-butene, (CH3)2C=CHCH3, has the more substituted double bond and is major.

Check. Count alkyl groups on each C=C: 2-methyl-2-butene has three, 3-methyl-1-butene has one. Both are C5H10, the alcohol minus H2O.

Example 4: Comparing boiling points and solubility

Problem. Compare the boiling points and water solubility of 1-butanol, diethyl ether, and pentane (all about 72–74 g/mol).

Solution.

Boiling point: Pentane (36 °C) and diethyl ether (35 °C) boil at nearly the same temperature. Neither can hydrogen bond with itself, and the ether's weak dipole adds very little. 1-Butanol (118 °C) is far higher because its O–H groups hydrogen bond with each other.

Solubility: Diethyl ether and 1-butanol are both moderately soluble (about 7 g/100 mL) because the oxygen in each can accept hydrogen bonds from water. Pentane has no polar group and is insoluble.

Check. Hydrogen bonding with itself controls boiling point. Hydrogen bonding with water controls solubility.

Example 5: Naming an ether and a thiol both ways

Problem. Give a common name and an IUPAC name for CH3OCH2CH2CH3, and name CH3CH2CH(SH)CH3.

Solution. The ether has a methyl group and a propyl group on oxygen. Common name: list them alphabetically, then "ether": methyl propyl ether. IUPAC: the longer chain (propane) is the parent and CH3O– is a methoxy substituent on C1: 1-methoxypropane. The thiol has a four-carbon chain; number from the end nearer the SH so it lands on C2: 2-butanethiol.

Check. Ether common names end in a separate word "ether"; IUPAC ether names contain "-oxy" and no "ether" at all. A thiol name keeps the "e" of the alkane (butane + thiol), unlike an alcohol name (butan + ol).

Example 6: Ranking by boiling point and solubility

Problem. Rank from lowest to highest boiling point: 1-propanol, ethyl methyl ether, 1,2-propanediol, and butane. Then state which are soluble in water.

Solution. All four have 58–76 g/mol, so look at the IMFs. Butane: dispersion only. Ethyl methyl ether: a weak dipole, no O–H. 1-Propanol: one O–H, hydrogen bonding. 1,2-Propanediol: two O–H groups, more hydrogen bonding per molecule. Ranking: butane < ethyl methyl ether < 1-propanol < 1,2-propanediol (actual values: −1, 8, 97, and 188 °C). Solubility: the ether, the alcohol, and the diol all have oxygen atoms that accept hydrogen bonds from water and short carbon chains, so all three are soluble (the diol and 1-propanol are miscible). Butane is insoluble.

Check. Each added O–H group raised the boiling point by roughly 90 °C in this series, a bigger effect than the few grams per mole of mass difference, which is the sign that hydrogen bonding dominates.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Counting the oxygen when classifying an alcohol. Only count carbons on the C–OH carbon.
  • Oxidizing a 2° alcohol "all the way" to a carboxylic acid. Ketones stop.
  • Claiming ethers hydrogen bond with each other. They have no O–H.
  • Calling C6H5CH2OH a phenol. The OH is on a CH2, not on the ring, so it is an alcohol (benzyl alcohol).
  • Choosing the Zaitsev product by counting which neighbor has more hydrogens. Count the alkyl groups on the product's double bond instead; the more substituted alkene wins.
  • Dropping the "e" in thiol names ("butanthiol"). It is butanethiol; only -ol replaces the -e.
  • Writing an IUPAC ether name with the alkoxy group on the longer chain. The shorter group becomes the alkoxy prefix; the longer chain is the parent (1-methoxypropane, not propoxymethane).
  • Treating ethylene glycol as harmless because it is "just a diol." Its oxidation products, not the glycol itself, are the toxic species.
