Learning Objectives

  • Identify and name alcohols, phenols, ethers, and thiols.
  • Classify alcohols as primary (1°), secondary (2°), or tertiary (3°).
  • Use IMFs to compare boiling points and water solubility of alcohols, ethers, and hydrocarbons.
  • Predict products of dehydration and oxidation of alcohols, and oxidation of thiols to disulfides.

Key Ideas

1. The functional groups

GroupGeneral formName endingExample
AlcoholR–OH (OH on an sp3 carbon)-olCH3CH2OH, ethanol
PhenolOH directly on a benzene ringphenolC6H5OH
EtherR–O–R′"alkyl alkyl ether" (common)CH3CH2OCH2CH3, diethyl ether
ThiolR–SH-thiolCH3SH, methanethiol

Naming alcohols: Find the longest chain containing the carbon bonded to OH, number so the OH carbon gets the lowest number, and replace the final -e with -ol: 2-butanol (butan-2-ol). Two OH groups give a -diol (1,2-ethanediol is ethylene glycol).

2. Classifying alcohols

Professor Tip

Go to the carbon that holds the OH, then count how many carbons are attached to that carbon. 1 carbon = 1°, 2 carbons = 2°, 3 carbons = 3°. Do not count the oxygen.

3. Physical properties

  • Alcohols hydrogen bond with each other (O–H), so they have high boiling points for their size.
  • Ethers have no O–H, so they cannot hydrogen bond with each other (dipole–dipole only). Their boiling points are close to those of alkanes of similar size. They can still accept hydrogen bonds from water.
  • Solubility: Alcohols with about 1–4 carbons mix with water. As the nonpolar carbon chain grows, solubility drops. Each extra OH increases solubility.
  • Thiols have weak IMFs (S–H is barely polar) and are known for strong, unpleasant odors.

4. Reactions

Dehydration (H+, heat): removes H2O from an alcohol to make an alkene (Zaitsev: the more substituted alkene is major). This is the reverse of hydration from Chapter 3.

Oxidation (adding O or removing H; written [O]):

  • 1° alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid
  • 2° alcohol → ketone (stops there)
  • 3° alcohol → no reaction. There is no H on the OH carbon to remove.

Thiol oxidation: 2 R–SH → R–S–S–R (a disulfide). This same S–S bond holds proteins in shape (Chapter 10).

5. Acidity: alcohols vs. phenols

  • Alcohols are about as acidic as water, so for our purposes they are neutral. They do not react with NaOH.
  • Phenols are weak acids. The benzene ring stabilizes the negative charge left behind when the O–H loses H+, so phenol reacts with NaOH: C6H5OH + NaOH → C6H5O−Na+ (sodium phenoxide) + H2O. This difference is one way to tell alcohols and phenols apart in lab.

6. Hydration and dehydration are reversible

Alkene + H2O ⇌ alcohol. By Le Chatelier's principle, adding lots of water pushes the reaction toward the alcohol (hydration), while heating and removing water as it forms pushes it toward the alkene (dehydration).

7. Functional group isomers

Isomers can have entirely different functional groups. Ethanol (CH3CH2OH) and dimethyl ether (CH3OCH3) are both C2H6O. Alongside skeletal isomers (different carbon chains) and positional isomers (1-propanol vs. 2-propanol), these complete the three types of constitutional isomers.

Health Career Connection

  • Nursing – antiseptics: 70% isopropyl alcohol (2-propanol) is the standard skin prep before injections. The water in the mixture helps the alcohol get into bacterial cells and denature their proteins.
  • Nursing – alcohol and methanol poisoning: The liver oxidizes ethanol (1°) to acetaldehyde, then to acetic acid, exactly the 1° alcohol oxidation pattern. Methanol is oxidized the same way to formaldehyde and formic acid, which are toxic and can cause blindness. Treatment blocks the first oxidation step.
  • Dental hygiene – halitosis: Much of "bad breath" comes from volatile sulfur compounds such as methanethiol (CH3SH) and hydrogen sulfide produced by bacteria on the tongue and in periodontal pockets.
  • Dental – phenols: Eugenol (clove oil) is the phenol in zinc oxide–eugenol temporary fillings and dressings. Thymol, another phenol, is an active ingredient in some antiseptic mouthwashes.
  • Dental – triclosan: Triclosan, an antibacterial once common in toothpastes, is a chlorinated phenol that also contains an ether link. That one molecule shows two functional groups from this chapter. (It has since been removed from most U.S. toothpastes.)

Further reading: M. Epple and J. Enax, "The Chemistry of Dental Care," ChemistryViews (Part 2).

In the Lab

  • Experiment 4 – Alcohols and Phenols
  • Solubility: alcohols with four or fewer carbons dissolve in water; longer ones do not. Phenol dissolves partly because it is a weak acid.
  • Chromate oxidation test: orange CrO42− turns green (Cr3+) when it oxidizes a 1° or 2° alcohol. A 3° alcohol cannot be oxidized, so the solution stays orange.
  • Ferric chloride (FeCl3) test: phenols form strongly colored complexes with Fe3+ (from yellow to purple). Ordinary alcohols do not.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Name and classify

Name and classify CH3CH(OH)CH2CH2CH3.

Solution

Five-carbon chain, numbered so the OH is on C2: 2-pentanol. The OH carbon is bonded to 2 other carbons, so it is a secondary (2°) alcohol.

Example 2: Oxidation

Give the oxidation product(s) of (a) 1-propanol, (b) cyclohexanol, (c) 2-methyl-2-butanol.

Solution

(a) 1°: CH3CH2CH2OH → CH3CH2CHO (propanal) → CH3CH2COOH (propanoic acid)

(b) 2°: cyclohexanol → cyclohexanone

(c) 3°: no reaction

Example 3: Dehydration

What is the major product when 3-methyl-2-butanol, CH3CH(OH)CH(CH3)CH3, is dehydrated?

Solution

H can be removed from C1 (giving 3-methyl-1-butene) or from C3 (giving 2-methyl-2-butene). 2-Methyl-2-butene, (CH3)2C=CHCH3, has the more substituted double bond and is major.

Example 4: Comparing boiling points and solubility

Compare the boiling points and water solubility of 1-butanol, diethyl ether, and pentane (all about 72–74 g/mol).

Solution

Boiling point: Pentane (36 °C) and diethyl ether (35 °C) boil at nearly the same temperature. Neither can hydrogen bond with itself, and the ether's weak dipole adds very little. 1-Butanol (118 °C) is far higher because its O–H groups hydrogen bond with each other.

Solubility: Diethyl ether and 1-butanol are both moderately soluble (about 7 g/100 mL) because the oxygen in each can accept hydrogen bonds from water. Pentane has no polar group and is insoluble.

Lesson: hydrogen bonding with itself controls boiling point. Hydrogen bonding with water controls solubility.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Counting the oxygen when classifying an alcohol. Only count carbons on the C–OH carbon.
  • Oxidizing a 2° alcohol "all the way" to a carboxylic acid. Ketones stop.
  • Claiming ethers hydrogen bond with each other. They have no O–H.
  • Calling C6H5CH2OH a phenol. The OH is on a CH2, not on the ring, so it is an alcohol (benzyl alcohol).

Practice

Exercise 1

Name and classify (CH3)2C(OH)CH2CH3.

Answer
2-methyl-2-butanol, a tertiary (3°) alcohol

Exercise 2

Give the common name of CH3OCH2CH3.

Answer
Ethyl methyl ether (alkyl groups in alphabetical order)

Exercise 3

What alcohol would you oxidize to make butanone, CH3COCH2CH3?

Answer
2-butanol, CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3 (a 2° alcohol gives a ketone)

Exercise 4

Write the product when two molecules of ethanethiol are oxidized.

Answer
CH3CH2–S–S–CH2CH3 (diethyl disulfide)

Exercise 5

Which is more soluble in water: 1-hexanol or 1,2-propanediol? Why?

Answer
1,2-Propanediol. It has two OH groups for hydrogen bonding and a much smaller nonpolar chain (it is fully miscible with water).

Exercise 6

Which reacts with aqueous NaOH: cyclohexanol or phenol? Write the reaction.

Answer
Phenol: C6H5OH + NaOH → C6H5O−Na+ + H2O. Cyclohexanol is an alcohol and does not react.

Exercise 7

Classify each pair as skeletal, positional, or functional group isomers: (a) 1-butanol and 2-butanol; (b) 1-butanol and diethyl ether.

Answer
(a) Positional; (b) functional group isomers (both C4H10O)
