Short Answer

Problem 14.11

A meal contains 45 g carbohydrate, 20 g protein, and 15 g fat. Calculate its energy in kcal.

Answer
45(4) + 20(4) + 15(9) = 180 + 80 + 135 = 395 kcal.

Problem 14.12

Explain why cyanide poisoning is so rapidly fatal.

Answer
Cyanide blocks cytochrome oxidase (Complex IV), so electrons cannot pass to O2. ATP production by oxidative phosphorylation stops.

Problem 14.13

Show how the total of 32 ATP per glucose is reached.

Answer
Glycolysis: 2 ATP + 2 NADH (5) = 7; pyruvate → acetyl CoA: 2 NADH = 5; citric acid cycle (2 turns): 6 NADH (15) + 2 FADH2 (3) + 2 GTP (2) = 20. Total = 32.

More Practice: Analyze and Compare

Problem 14.42

Which statement about the citric acid cycle step succinyl CoA → succinate is correct?

  1. It is an oxidative decarboxylation catalyzed by an oxidoreductase.
  2. It is a substrate-level phosphorylation catalyzed by a ligase and produces GTP.
  3. It is a hydration catalyzed by a lyase.
  4. It is the step that reduces FAD to FADH2.
Answer
B. Breaking the high-energy thioester of succinyl CoA is coupled to GDP + Pi → GTP; the enzyme (succinyl CoA synthetase) is a ligase. No carbon is lost, no coenzyme is reduced, and no water is added, so A, C and D describe other steps (3/4, 7 and 6).

Problem 14.43

A cell is treated with an uncoupler such as 2,4-dinitrophenol. Which combination of changes would you expect?

  1. O2 consumption falls, ATP falls, heat falls
  2. O2 consumption rises, ATP falls, heat rises
  3. O2 consumption rises, ATP rises, heat rises
  4. O2 consumption falls, ATP rises, heat rises
Answer
B. An uncoupler lets H+ leak back across the membrane without passing through ATP synthase. The gradient never builds, so the chain runs flat-out (more O2 used), but the energy is released as heat rather than captured as ATP.

Problem 14.45

The malate–aspartate shuttle gives 2 more ATP per glucose than the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle because

  1. it carries 2 NADH across instead of 1
  2. it delivers electrons to Complex I (as matrix NADH) rather than to coenzyme Q (as FADH2)
  3. it makes an extra GTP in the matrix
  4. it bypasses Complex IV
Answer
B. Both shuttles move the electrons of 2 cytosolic NADH. The malate–aspartate shuttle regenerates NADH inside the matrix (2.5 ATP each); the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle hands the electrons to an FAD enzyme that feeds CoQ, skipping Complex I's proton pumping (1.5 ATP each). 2 × (2.5 − 1.5) = 2 ATP.

Problem 14.46

Compare and contrast. Isocitrate dehydrogenase and succinate dehydrogenase are both oxidoreductases in the cycle. Give two ways they are alike and three ways they differ (think about coenzyme, carbon count, location, and what bond is formed).

Answer
Alike: both remove 2 H from their substrate (oxidation) and both store the electrons in a reduced coenzyme that goes to electron transport. Different: isocitrate dehydrogenase uses NAD+ (2.5 ATP), succinate dehydrogenase uses FAD (1.5 ATP); isocitrate dehydrogenase also releases CO2 (6 C → 5 C, an oxidative decarboxylation) while succinate dehydrogenase keeps all 4 C; isocitrate dehydrogenase oxidizes an alcohol to a ketone, succinate dehydrogenase makes a C=C; and succinate dehydrogenase is embedded in the inner membrane as Complex II, whereas isocitrate dehydrogenase is free in the matrix and is the cycle's main regulated (allosteric) step.

Problem 14.47

Predict. A cell is given rotenone, which blocks Complex I. Predict the ATP yield per glucose if the citric acid cycle somehow kept running (assume the malate–aspartate shuttle) and explain which carriers would still be useful.

Answer
With Complex I blocked, none of the 10 NADH can be oxidized, so their 25 ATP are lost. Electrons from FADH2 enter at Complex II/CoQ, downstream of the block, so the 2 FADH2 still give 3 ATP. Add the 4 substrate-level ATP (2 glycolysis + 2 GTP): 7 ATP per glucose. In reality the cycle would stall quickly because NAD+ is not regenerated, so the true yield would fall toward 2.

Problem 14.48

Analyze the diagram. The electron transport chain can be sketched as: NADH → [I] → CoQ → [III] → cyt c → [IV] → O2, with FADH2 → [II] → CoQ joining at CoQ, and H+ arrows leaving the matrix at I, III and IV. Using only this sketch, answer: (a) If Complex III is blocked, which carriers end up fully reduced and which fully oxidized? (b) Would FADH2 still be useful? (c) How many proton-pumping complexes do electrons from FADH2 pass through, and what does that predict about its ATP value relative to NADH?

Answer
(a) Everything upstream of the block stays reduced: NADH, Complex I, FADH2, Complex II and CoQ (as CoQH2). Everything downstream is drained to O2 and stays oxidized: cytochrome c and Complex IV. (b) No. FADH2 enters at CoQ, which is before Complex III, so its electrons are also stuck. (c) FADH2 electrons pass through III and IV only (two pumps) versus I, III and IV for NADH (three pumps), so FADH2 should be worth less: 1.5 ATP versus 2.5.

Problem 14.50

ATP tally. Complete the tally for one glucose oxidized in a heart muscle cell (malate–aspartate shuttle), giving the ATP from each line and the total. Then state how the total would change in skeletal muscle.

Answer
Glycolysis: 2 ATP; 2 NADH × 2.5 = 5. Pyruvate dehydrogenase: 2 NADH × 2.5 = 5. Cycle (2 turns): 6 NADH × 2.5 = 15; 2 FADH2 × 1.5 = 3; 2 GTP = 2. Total = 2 + 5 + 5 + 15 + 3 + 2 = 32 ATP. In skeletal muscle the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle delivers the 2 cytosolic NADH as FADH2 (2 × 1.5 = 3 instead of 5), so the total is 30.

Problem 14.51

ATP tally. A cell oxidizes 5 glucose molecules completely with the malate–aspartate shuttle. How many ATP, CO2, and O2 molecules are involved, and how many of the ATP came from substrate-level phosphorylation?

Answer
ATP: 5 × 32 = 160. CO2: 5 × 6 = 30. O2: 5 × 6 = 30 (one O2 per CO2 for glucose). Substrate-level: 4 per glucose (2 in glycolysis + 2 GTP) = 20; the other 140 came from oxidative phosphorylation.

More Practice: Clinical and Integrative

Problem 14.52

Nursing. A patient rescued from a smoke-filled apartment has a normal pulse-oximeter reading and a high arterial oxygen level, yet blood lactate is very high and the venous blood drawn is unusually bright red. Explain each finding in terms of electron transport, and explain why hydroxocobalamin is given rather than simply more oxygen.

Answer
Smoke carries cyanide, which binds Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase). Oxygen is being delivered normally (so saturation and arterial O2 look fine) but cannot be used as the final electron acceptor, so it passes through the tissues unused and venous blood stays bright red. With the chain blocked, NADH piles up, the cycle stops, and cells fall back on glycolysis plus lactate fermentation, which raises lactate and causes metabolic acidosis. More oxygen cannot help an enzyme that is blocked; hydroxocobalamin binds the cyanide ion (forming cyanocobalamin, which is excreted), freeing Complex IV to work again.

Problem 14.53

Nursing. A patient with alcohol use disorder and poor nutrition is admitted with confusion. The team notes thiamine deficiency. Which enzyme complex in this chapter is directly affected, what happens to pyruvate as a result, and why does the brain show symptoms first?

Answer
The pyruvate dehydrogenase complex requires thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP); so does α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase in the cycle. Without TPP, pyruvate cannot be converted to acetyl CoA, so it accumulates and is reduced to lactate, and the full 32-ATP yield drops toward the 2 ATP of glycolysis. The brain depends almost entirely on glucose oxidation and has a high, constant ATP demand, so it is the first tissue to fail (Wernicke encephalopathy). This is why thiamine is given along with, or before, glucose in patients at risk.

Problem 14.54

Dental hygiene, integrative. Streptococcus mutans in plaque ferments sucrose to lactic acid; a human cheek cell oxidizes the same glucose to CO2. (a) How many ATP does each cell make per glucose, and what does the bacterium do with its pyruvate that the cheek cell does not? (b) Using Chapter 13, explain why the product is lactic acid and what it does to enamel below pH 5.5. (c) Why does disrupting the plaque biofilm and exposing it to oxygen favor less acid-producing species?

Answer
(a) The bacterium, lacking a full electron transport chain, makes only the 2 ATP of glycolysis and must reduce pyruvate to lactate to regenerate NAD+; the cheek cell, with mitochondria and oxygen, sends pyruvate to acetyl CoA and the cycle for about 30–32 ATP. (b) Lactate is the conjugate base of lactic acid, a carboxylic acid (pKa about 3.9), so the fermentation releases H+; below the critical pH of about 5.5 the acid dissolves hydroxyapatite (demineralization). (c) Many of the most acid-tolerant plaque organisms are anaerobes or do best at low oxygen; bringing oxygen back and removing the thick biofilm lets aerobic, oxidative species compete, which produce less acid per sugar because they can oxidize it completely.
