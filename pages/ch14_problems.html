Explain why cyanide poisoning is so rapidly fatal.

Answer
Cyanide blocks cytochrome oxidase (Complex IV), so electrons cannot pass to O2. ATP production by oxidative phosphorylation stops.

Problem 14.13

Show how the total of 32 ATP per glucose is reached.

More Practice: Analyze and Compare

Problem 14.42

Which statement about the citric acid cycle step succinyl CoA → succinate is correct?

  1. It is an oxidative decarboxylation catalyzed by an oxidoreductase.
  2. It is a substrate-level phosphorylation catalyzed by a ligase and produces GTP.
  3. It is a hydration catalyzed by a lyase.
  4. It is the step that reduces FAD to FADH2.
Answer
B. Breaking the high-energy thioester of succinyl CoA is coupled to GDP + Pi → GTP; the enzyme (succinyl CoA synthetase) is a ligase. No carbon is lost, no coenzyme is reduced, and no water is added, so A, C and D describe other steps (3/4, 7 and 6).

Problem 14.43

A cell is treated with an uncoupler such as 2,4-dinitrophenol. Which combination of changes would you expect?

  1. O2 consumption falls, ATP falls, heat falls
  2. O2 consumption rises, ATP falls, heat rises
  3. O2 consumption rises, ATP rises, heat rises
  4. O2 consumption falls, ATP rises, heat rises
Answer
B. An uncoupler lets H+ leak back across the membrane without passing through ATP synthase. The gradient never builds, so the chain runs flat-out (more O2 used), but the energy is released as heat rather than captured as ATP.

Problem 14.44

Which coenzyme of the citric acid cycle is built from riboflavin (vitamin B2), and in which step does it do its work?

  1. NAD+, in step 3
  2. coenzyme A, in step 1
  3. FAD, in step 6
  4. GDP, in step 5
Answer
C. Flavin adenine dinucleotide contains riboflavin and is the oxidizing agent in step 6, succinate → fumarate, where a C–C single bond becomes a C=C. NAD+ comes from niacin (B3) and coenzyme A from pantothenic acid (B5); GDP is a nucleotide, not a vitamin-derived coenzyme.

Problem 14.45

The malate–aspartate shuttle gives 2 more ATP per glucose than the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle because

  1. it carries 2 NADH across instead of 1
  2. it delivers electrons to Complex I (as matrix NADH) rather than to coenzyme Q (as FADH2)
  3. it makes an extra GTP in the matrix
  4. it bypasses Complex IV
Answer
B. Both shuttles move the electrons of 2 cytosolic NADH. The malate–aspartate shuttle regenerates NADH inside the matrix (2.5 ATP each); the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle hands the electrons to an FAD enzyme that feeds CoQ, skipping Complex I's proton pumping (1.5 ATP each). 2 × (2.5 − 1.5) = 2 ATP.

Problem 14.46

Compare and contrast. Isocitrate dehydrogenase and succinate dehydrogenase are both oxidoreductases in the cycle. Give two ways they are alike and three ways they differ (think about coenzyme, carbon count, location, and what bond is formed).

Answer
Alike: both remove 2 H from their substrate (oxidation) and both store the electrons in a reduced coenzyme that goes to electron transport. Different: isocitrate dehydrogenase uses NAD+ (2.5 ATP), succinate dehydrogenase uses FAD (1.5 ATP); isocitrate dehydrogenase also releases CO2 (6 C → 5 C, an oxidative decarboxylation) while succinate dehydrogenase keeps all 4 C; isocitrate dehydrogenase oxidizes an alcohol to a ketone, succinate dehydrogenase makes a C=C; and succinate dehydrogenase is embedded in the inner membrane as Complex II, whereas isocitrate dehydrogenase is free in the matrix and is the cycle's main regulated (allosteric) step.

Problem 14.47

Predict. A liver cell has just been flooded with ATP and NADH after a large meal. Predict what happens to citrate synthase, isocitrate dehydrogenase, and the rate of electron transport, and explain in one sentence why the cell behaves this way. Then predict what reverses all three changes.

Answer
High ATP inhibits citrate synthase; high NADH inhibits isocitrate dehydrogenase; and with little ADP available, ATP synthase and therefore electron transport slow down (the chain is limited by the availability of ADP, Pi, O2, and NADH, and ADP is the one in short supply here). The cell already has the energy it needs, so it stops oxidizing acetyl CoA and diverts it to fat synthesis instead (Chapter 15). A rise in ADP, as ATP is spent, reverses all three: ADP activates both enzymes and lets protons flow back through ATP synthase.

Problem 14.48

Analyze the diagram. The electron transport chain can be sketched as: NADH → [I] → CoQ → [III] → cyt c → [IV] → O2, with FADH2 → [II] → CoQ joining at CoQ, and H+ arrows leaving the matrix at I, III and IV. Using only this sketch, answer: (a) If Complex III is blocked, which carriers end up fully reduced and which fully oxidized? (b) Would FADH2 still be useful? (c) How many proton-pumping complexes do electrons from FADH2 pass through, and what does that predict about its ATP value relative to NADH?

Answer
(a) Everything upstream of the block stays reduced: NADH, Complex I, FADH2, Complex II and CoQ (as CoQH2). Everything downstream is drained to O2 and stays oxidized: cytochrome c and Complex IV. (b) No. FADH2 enters at CoQ, which is before Complex III, so its electrons are also stuck. (c) FADH2 electrons pass through III and IV only (two pumps) versus I, III and IV for NADH (three pumps), so FADH2 should be worth less: 1.5 ATP versus 2.5.

Problem 14.50

ATP tally. Complete the tally for one glucose oxidized in a heart muscle cell (malate–aspartate shuttle), giving the ATP from each line and the total. Then state how the total would change in skeletal muscle.

Answer
Glycolysis: 2 ATP; 2 NADH × 2.5 = 5. Pyruvate dehydrogenase: 2 NADH × 2.5 = 5. Cycle (2 turns): 6 NADH × 2.5 = 15; 2 FADH2 × 1.5 = 3; 2 GTP = 2. Total = 2 + 5 + 5 + 15 + 3 + 2 = 32 ATP. In skeletal muscle the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle delivers the 2 cytosolic NADH as FADH2 (2 × 1.5 = 3 instead of 5), so the total is 30.

Problem 14.51

ATP tally. A cell oxidizes 5 glucose molecules completely with the malate–aspartate shuttle. How many ATP, CO2, and O2 molecules are involved, and how many of the ATP came from substrate-level phosphorylation?

Answer
ATP: 5 × 32 = 160. CO2: 5 × 6 = 30. O2: 5 × 6 = 30 (one O2 per CO2 for glucose). Substrate-level: 4 per glucose (2 in glycolysis + 2 GTP) = 20; the other 140 came from oxidative phosphorylation.

More Practice: Clinical and Integrative

Problem 14.52

Nursing. A patient rescued from a smoke-filled apartment has a normal pulse-oximeter reading and a high arterial oxygen level, yet blood lactate is very high and the venous blood drawn is unusually bright red. Explain each finding in terms of electron transport, and explain why hydroxocobalamin is given rather than simply more oxygen.

Answer
Smoke carries cyanide, which binds Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase). Oxygen is being delivered normally (so saturation and arterial O2 look fine) but cannot be used as the final electron acceptor, so it passes through the tissues unused and venous blood stays bright red. With the chain blocked, NADH piles up, the cycle stops, and cells fall back on glycolysis plus lactate fermentation, which raises lactate and causes metabolic acidosis. More oxygen cannot help an enzyme that is blocked; hydroxocobalamin binds the cyanide ion (forming cyanocobalamin, which is excreted), freeing Complex IV to work again.

Problem 14.53

Nursing. A patient with alcohol use disorder and poor nutrition is admitted with confusion. The team notes thiamine deficiency. Which enzyme complex in this chapter is directly affected, what happens to pyruvate as a result, and why does the brain show symptoms first?

Answer
The pyruvate dehydrogenase complex requires a cofactor made from thiamine; so does α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase in the cycle. Without it, pyruvate cannot be converted to acetyl CoA, so it accumulates and is reduced to lactate, and the full 32-ATP yield drops toward the 2 ATP of glycolysis. The brain depends almost entirely on glucose oxidation and has a high, constant ATP demand, so it is the first tissue to fail (Wernicke encephalopathy). This is why thiamine is given along with, or before, glucose in patients at risk.

Problem 14.54

Dental hygiene, integrative. Streptococcus mutans in plaque ferments sucrose to lactic acid; a human cheek cell oxidizes the same glucose to CO2. (a) How many ATP does each cell make per glucose, and what does the bacterium do with its pyruvate that the cheek cell does not? (b) Using Chapter 13, explain why the product is lactic acid and what it does to enamel below pH 5.5. (c) Why does disrupting the plaque biofilm and exposing it to oxygen favor less acid-producing species?

Answer
(a) The bacterium, lacking a full electron transport chain, makes only the 2 ATP of glycolysis and must reduce pyruvate to lactate to regenerate NAD+; the cheek cell, with mitochondria and oxygen, sends pyruvate to acetyl CoA and the cycle for about 30–32 ATP. (b) Lactate is the conjugate base of lactic acid, a carboxylic acid (pKa about 3.9), so the fermentation releases H+; below the critical pH of about 5.5 the acid dissolves hydroxyapatite (demineralization). (c) Many of the most acid-tolerant plaque organisms are anaerobes or do best at low oxygen; bringing oxygen back and removing the thick biofilm lets aerobic, oxidative species compete, which produce less acid per sugar because they can oxidize it completely.

Problem 14.59

Matching. Several cycle steps are “two reactions in one.” Match each step to the pair of reactions it is deconstructed into: (1) step 1, oxaloacetate + acetyl CoA → citrate (2) step 2, citrate → isocitrate (3) step 3, isocitrate → α-ketoglutarate (4) step 5, succinyl CoA → succinate. Pairs: (a) oxidation then decarboxylation; (b) hydrolysis then phosphorylation; (c) condensation then hydrolysis; (d) dehydration then hydration.

Answer
(1)–(c): the two molecules condense and the thioester is hydrolyzed to release CoA–SH. (2)–(d): water is removed to make a C=C and added back on the neighboring carbon, moving the OH. (3)–(a): NAD+ oxidizes the 2° alcohol to a ketone, then CO2 leaves. (4)–(b): the thioester is hydrolyzed and the energy released converts GDP to GTP.

Problem 14.60

A step is described in words: “A 3° alcohol on a six-carbon tricarboxylic acid is converted to a 2° alcohol on the neighboring carbon; the formula does not change.” Set up the table: reaction type, how you can tell, enzyme class, coenzyme.

Answer
Isomerization (the OH moves from one carbon to the next; same formula in and out). How you can tell: no change in formula, no ATP, no coenzyme reduced. Enzyme: aconitase, classified as a lyase because it works by removing water and adding it back (dehydration then hydration). Coenzyme: none. This is step 2, citrate → isocitrate.

Problem 14.61

Step 4 of the cycle converts five-carbon α-ketoglutarate into four-carbon succinyl CoA. Set up the table and say which coenzyme is the oxidizing agent and which coenzyme supplies the sulfur.

Answer
Reaction type: decarboxylation and oxidation. How you can tell: 5 C → 4 C (CO2 released) and NAD+ → NADH; the carbon that gains a bond to sulfur is “oxidized” in the same sense as gaining a bond to oxygen. Enzyme: α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, an oxidoreductase. Coenzymes: NAD+ is the oxidizing agent (it is reduced to NADH); coenzyme A supplies the –SH that forms the thioester.

Problem 14.62

In the malate–aspartate shuttle, cytosolic oxaloacetate is converted to malate while NADH becomes NAD+. (a) What type of reaction is this for oxaloacetate, and how can you tell? (b) What class of enzyme is involved? (c) Why does the cell bother, since the same reaction runs the other way inside the mitochondrion?

Answer
(a) A reduction: the ketone of oxaloacetate becomes the 2° alcohol of malate, and the coenzyme NADH gives up its hydrogen (it is oxidized to NAD+), so the substrate must have been reduced. (b) An oxidoreductase (malate dehydrogenase, running in the reduction direction). (c) NADH itself cannot cross the inner mitochondrial membrane, but malate can. Inside, malate is oxidized back to oxaloacetate, reducing a matrix NAD+ to NADH that can enter Complex I. The electrons were shuttled across, not the NADH.

Problem 14.63

Matching. Match each electron transport complex to its name: (1) Complex I (2) Complex II (3) Complex III (4) Complex IV. Names: cytochrome c oxidase; NADH–coenzyme Q reductase; coenzyme Q–cytochrome c reductase; succinate–coenzyme Q reductase. Then state which complex does not pump protons.

Answer
(1) NADH–coenzyme Q reductase; (2) succinate–coenzyme Q reductase (it is also succinate dehydrogenase, step 6 of the cycle); (3) coenzyme Q–cytochrome c reductase; (4) cytochrome c oxidase. Complex II does not pump protons, which is why FADH2 gives 1.5 ATP instead of 2.5. The names tell the story: each complex is named for what it takes electrons from and what it hands them to.

Problem 14.64

True or false; correct any false statement. (a) The citric acid cycle is a linear pathway. (b) The “fuel” for the cycle is acetyl CoA, which can come from carbohydrates, fats, or proteins. (c) Cytochrome c carries oxygen, as hemoglobin does. (d) Our cells convert about 34% of the energy in glucose to ATP and lose the rest as heat.

Answer
(a) False: it is cyclic; the final product, oxaloacetate, is the initial reactant. (b) True. (c) False: cytochrome c is a heme protein, but its heme is bound so that the iron shuttles electrons (Fe3+ ↔ Fe2+) instead of binding O2. (d) True; the heat helps maintain body temperature.

Problem 14.65

Fill in the blanks. Each NADH that enters the electron transport chain produces enough energy to make ____ ATP, and each FADH2 makes ____ ATP. Two turns of the citric acid cycle give ____ NADH, ____ FADH2, and ____ GTP, which together are worth ____ ATP.

Answer
2.5; 1.5; 6; 2; 2; 20 (6 × 2.5 = 15, plus 2 × 1.5 = 3, plus 2 GTP = 2).

Problem 14.66

Describe the two subunits of ATP synthase and what each does. Then explain why a cell with very little ADP makes very little ATP even when NADH and O2 are plentiful.

Answer
F0 is the channel through which H+ flows back into the matrix; F1 is the site where ADP + Pi are joined to make ATP, using the energy released by the proton flow. ATP synthase can only phosphorylate ADP, so when ADP is scarce the protons have no work to do, the gradient stays high, and electron transport slows down. Electron transport is regulated by the availability of ADP (and Pi, O2, and NADH); a working cell that produces ADP rapidly speeds the whole chain up.

Cumulative Problem 14.1 (Chapters 4, 6 and 14)

Acetyl CoA, the molecule that enters the citric acid cycle, is formed from pyruvate. Which statement is correct?

  1. Pyruvate loses CO2 and is oxidized, producing NADH; the acetyl group is joined to coenzyme A by a thioester bond
  2. Pyruvate gains CO2 and is reduced; the acetyl group is joined to CoA by an amide bond
  3. Pyruvate is split into two acetyl groups; each is joined to CoA by an ester bond
  4. Pyruvate is reduced to lactate, which is joined to CoA by an ether bond
Answer
A. The pyruvate dehydrogenase complex removes the carboxyl carbon as CO2 and oxidizes the remaining two carbons, passing the hydrogens to NAD+. The resulting acetyl group is attached to the –SH of coenzyme A (a thiol, Chapter 4) as a thioester, the sulfur analogue of an ester (Chapter 6). The thioester is a high-energy bond, which is what lets acetyl CoA hand its two carbons to oxaloacetate in the first step of the cycle. Only one acetyl CoA forms per pyruvate, so two per glucose.

Cumulative Problem 14.2 (Chapters 3, 4, 5 and 14)

Three consecutive steps of the citric acid cycle are: succinate → fumarate (FAD → FADH2); fumarate + H2O → malate; malate → oxaloacetate (NAD+ → NADH). For each step, name the organic reaction type you learned in Chapters 3–5, and state whether fumarate is cis or trans.

Answer
Step 1 is an oxidation (dehydrogenation) that removes two H atoms from adjacent carbons to create a C=C double bond; the product fumarate is the trans alkene (Chapter 3; the cis isomer is maleate, which the enzyme does not make). Step 2 is a hydration, the addition of water across the double bond (Chapter 3), giving a 2° alcohol. Step 3 is the oxidation of a 2° alcohol to a ketone (Chapters 4 and 5), with NAD+ taking the hydrogens. The same three-reaction sequence (oxidize to an alkene, hydrate, oxidize the alcohol) is used again in β-oxidation of fatty acids in Chapter 15.

Cumulative Problem 14.3 (Chapters 13 and 14)

For one acetyl CoA passing through the citric acid cycle, state how many NADH, FADH2, GTP (ATP) and CO2 are produced. Then, using about 2.5 ATP per NADH and 1.5 per FADH2, estimate the ATP from one acetyl CoA and from one complete glucose (glycolysis + pyruvate oxidation + two turns of the cycle).

Cumulative Problem 14.4 (Chapters 4, 11 and 14)

(a) In the electron transport chain, what is the final electron acceptor and what does it become? (b) How does electron transport lead to ATP synthesis, if the chain itself makes no ATP? (c) Cyanide and carbon monoxide both bind to cytochrome c oxidase (complex IV). Using Chapter 11 language, what kind of inhibitors are they, and why does death follow within minutes?

Answer
(a) O2, which accepts four electrons and four H+ to become two H2O. (b) As electrons pass down the chain, the complexes pump H+ out of the matrix, building a concentration gradient across the inner membrane. H+ flows back in only through ATP synthase, and that flow turns the enzyme and drives ADP + Pi → ATP (chemiosmosis, oxidative phosphorylation). (c) They bind tightly to the iron (and copper) of the heme a3–CuB center of cytochrome c oxidase, the very site where O2 is reduced. Carbon monoxide competes with O2 for that site, and cyanide binds so tightly that the block is essentially irreversible at toxic doses. With complex IV blocked, electrons cannot reach O2, NADH piles up, the gradient collapses, and ATP production stops in every cell at once even though the blood is full of oxygen.

Cumulative Problem 14.5 (Chapters 4, 5, 13 and 14)

Keep oxidation and reduction straight: for each conversion, say whether the named compound is oxidized or reduced, and which coenzyme is used. (a) lactate → pyruvate; (b) NAD+ → NADH; (c) ethanol → acetaldehyde; (d) FADH2 → FAD in the electron transport chain.

Answer
(a) Lactate’s 2° alcohol becomes a ketone, a loss of two hydrogens: lactate is oxidized; NAD+ is the acceptor and becomes NADH. (b) NAD+ gains a hydride (two electrons and a proton): it is reduced; it is the coenzyme itself. (c) A 1° alcohol becomes an aldehyde: ethanol is oxidized (Chapter 4), NAD+ accepts. (d) FADH2 gives up its hydrogens to the chain: it is oxidized to FAD. Rule from Chapter 4 that still works: in organic molecules, oxidation is losing H or gaining O; reduction is the opposite. In metabolism, whatever is oxidized hands its hydrogens to NAD+ or FAD, and those carry them to the chain.

Cumulative Problem 14.6 (Chapters 4, 6, 11 and 14)

In the clinic. 2,4-Dinitrophenol (DNP) was sold as a diet pill in the 1930s and killed several users; a large aspirin overdose does something similar. Both are weak acids that can carry H+ across the inner mitochondrial membrane. (a) What functional group makes DNP an acid, and why can both its acid and its anion form cross the membrane? (b) Explain why such an “uncoupler” causes weight loss, a high fever, and very little ATP all at once. (c) Brown fat in infants contains a natural uncoupler protein. What is it for?

Answer
(a) The phenol –OH (Chapter 4), made more acidic by the two electron-withdrawing nitro groups. The molecule is a large, nonpolar aromatic ring, so even its anion is lipid-soluble enough to slip through the membrane; it picks up an H+ on the outside, crosses, and drops it inside. (b) The H+ gradient leaks away without passing through ATP synthase, so the chain keeps burning NADH and oxygen (and the fat and sugar that make them) but little ATP is made. The energy that would have gone into ATP is released as heat instead; the patient burns fuel uncontrollably (weight loss), overheats (fatal hyperthermia) and cannot make enough ATP to live. (c) The same leak, on purpose: thermogenin in brown fat lets newborns, who cannot shiver, convert fat directly into heat.
