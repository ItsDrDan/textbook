Learning Objectives

  • Understand the citric acid cycle, its reactions, and the yield of energetic molecules.

What is the citric acid cycle?

The citric acid cycle is the first part of the third stage of food catabolism, including carbohydrates, fats, and proteins. It is called the citric acid cycle because a \(\ce{2C's}\) acetyl-\(\ce{CoA}\) produced in the second stage of catabolism of foods reacts with a \(\ce{4C's}\) oxaloacetate and produces \(\ce{6C's}\)-citrate in the first reaction. The citrate is oxidized through a series of eight reactions producing two carbon dioxide (\(\ce{CO2}\)) and re-generates the oxaloacetate to repeat the next round of the cycle, as shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\). It is also called the tricarboxylic acid cycle because citrate has three carboxylate (\(\ce{-COO^{-}}\)) groups, i.e., triacid. Another name for it is the Krebs cycle in honor of Hans Krebs, who discovered this metabolic pathway.

The oxidation reactions in the citric acid cycle release energy, which is coupled to the reduction of \(\ce{NAD^{+}}\) to \(\ce{NADH}\), \(\ce{FAD}\) to \(\ce{FADH2}\), or conversions of \(\ce{ADP}\) to high-energy \(\ce{ATP}\). Guanosine triphosphate (\(\ce{GTP}\)) -another high-energy molecule is also produced from guanosine diphosphate (\(\ce{GDP}\)), but this reaction ultimately reverses to transfer its energy to make \(\ce{ATP}\).

Reactions of the citric acid cycle

The following equations of the reactions are adapted from a public-domain source on the Russian-language Wikipedia.

1. Citrate synthase joins the two-carbon acetyl group of acetyl CoA to the four-carbon oxaloacetate, forming a new C–C bond and the six-carbon citrate. The thioester bond to coenzyme A is hydrolyzed in the same step, releasing HS–CoA; that hydrolysis is what makes the step strongly downhill. The overall reaction is shown below.

2. The –OH group of citrate is on a 3° carbon, and a 3° alcohol cannot be oxidized. Aconitase therefore moves it: water is removed (a dehydration) and then added back the other way (a hydration), giving isocitrate, whose –OH is on a 2° carbon and can be oxidized in the next step. Nothing is split by water, so this is not a hydrolysis.

3. Isocitrate dehydrogenase oxidizes the 2° –OH of isocitrate to a ketone (C=O), and NAD+ is reduced to NADH. A carboxylate group next to the new ketone then leaves as CO2 (a decarboxylation; the cofactor Mg2+ helps), giving five-carbon α-ketoglutarate, as shown below.

4. The fourth reaction resembles the previous link reaction's oxidative decarboxylation of pyruvate to acetyl-\(\ce{CoA}\). In this reaction, \(\ce{NAD^{+}}\) is reduced into \(\ce{NADH}\) at the expense of oxidative decarboxylation of \(\alpha\)-ketoglutarate and the resulting acyl-group is transferred to \(\ce{HS-CoA}\) resulting in succinyl-\(\ce{CoA}\) product as shown in the reaction below.

5. The fifth reaction has two steps. In the first step, a phosphate (\(\ce{Pi}\)) group substitutes \(\ce{-S-CoA}\) group from succinyl-\(\ce{CoA}\). In the second step, the phosphate group is transferred to \(\ce{GDP}\) that converts to a \(\ce{GTP}\) in the same way as in steps 7 and 10 of glycolysis. \(\ce{GTP}\) reverts back to \(\ce{GDP}\) and transfers its phosphate group to \(\ce{ADP}\) that converts to one \(\ce{ATP}\). So, production of one \(\ce{GTP}\) is equal to production of one \(\ce{ATP}\). The overall reaction five is shown below.

6. In the sixth reaction, one \(\ce{FAD}\) is reduced into \(\ce{FADH_{2}}\) at the expense of oxidation of succinate to fumarate by the following overall reaction.

7. In the seventh reaction \(\ce{H2O}\) hydrates the \(\ce{C=C}\) of fumarate producing malate, as shown below.

Recall that the enzymes are stereospecific. In this case, only fumarate (not its cis-isomer) reacts with the enzyme, and only one enantiomer of malate, L-malate, is produced.

8. In the eighth step, one more \(\ce{NAD^{+}}\) is reduced into \(\ce{NADH}\) at the expense of oxidation of malate into oxaloacetate, as shown in the reaction below.

The oxaloacetate is the reactant of step 1 and begins the cycle again by reacting with another acetyl-CoA. One citric acid cycle has the following overall reaction with one acetyl-\(\ce{CoA}\).

\(\ce{ \underbrace{CH3-\!\!-\!\!{\overset{\overset{\Huge\enspace\!{\ce{O}}}|\!\!|\enspace}{\ce{C}}}\!\!-S-CoA}_{Acetyl\;CoA} + 3NAD^{+} + FAD + GDP + P_{i} + 2H2O -> HS-CoA + 3NADH + 3H^{+} + FADH_{2} + GTP + 2CO2}\)

Recall that the catabolism of one glucose molecule produces two pyruvates that, in turn, produce two acetyl-\(\ce{CoA}\) in the oxidative decarboxylation of pyruvate under aerobic conditions. So, the citric acid cycle runs twice for the catabolism of one glucose molecule. One \(\ce{GTP}\) ultimately converts into one \(\ce{ATP}\).

Two turns of the citric acid cycle per glucose molecule yield ten energetic molecules: \(\ce{6NADH}\), \(\ce{2FADH_{2}}\), and \(\ce{2ATP}\)

Predicting the production or consumption of energetic molecules in food catabolism

The information in this note is meant to help predict which energetic molecule will be produced or consumed during food catabolism reactions. In food catabolism, oxidizing a fuel molecule releases energy overall because its electrons are passed on, through NAD+ or FAD, to O2; the reverse reductions used to build molecules consume energy. (Oxidation does not release energy in every reaction; it is the overall transfer of electrons to oxygen that makes catabolism downhill.) The energy released from oxidation reactions in food catabolism is utilized to produce one of the two energetic coenzymes, i.e., either \(\ce{NADH}\) from the reduction of \(\ce{NAD^{+}}\) or \(\ce{FADH2}\) from the reduction of \(\ce{FAD}\). The rule is empirical: an oxidation that turns an alcohol into a carbonyl (\(\ce{C=O}\)) group produces \(\ce{NADH}\), and an oxidation that turns a \(\ce{C-C}\) single bond into a \(\ce{C=C}\) double bond produces \(\ce{FADH2}\). \(\ce{NADH}\) is worth more ATP than \(\ce{FADH2}\) (2.5 versus 1.5, Section 14.4). Two reactions in this book oxidize a \(\ce{C-C}\) bond to a \(\ce{C=C}\) bond and produce \(\ce{FADH2}\): reaction 6 in the citric acid cycle and the first reaction of β-oxidation (Section 15.4). All other oxidation reactions in the citric acid cycle and glycolysis involve \(\ce{C=O}\) group, i.e., either alcohol (\(\ce{CH-OH}\)) to carbonyl (\(\ce{C=O}\)) conversion, or ketone (\(\ce{R-\!\!-\!\!{\overset{\overset{\Huge\enspace\!{\ce{O}}}|\!\!|\enspace}{\ce{C}}}\!\!-R'}\)) to thioester (\(\ce{R-\!\!-\!\!{\overset{\overset{\Huge\enspace\!{\ce{O}}}|\!\!|\enspace}{\ce{C}}}\!\!-S-R"}\)) conversion. These oxidations produce \(\ce{NADH}\). Conversions of pyruvate to lactate and ethanol under anaerobic conditions are reduction reactions involving \(\ce{C=O}\) group and consume \(\ce{NADH}\).

Reactions that are not redox reactions, e.g., acyl (\(\ce{ R-\!\!-\!\!{\overset{\overset{\Huge\enspace\!{\ce{O}}}|\!\!|\enspace}{\ce{C}}}\!\!-}\)) transfer, hydration (addition of \(\ce{H2O}\)) , dehydration (elimination of \(\ce{H2O}\)), and isomerization, involve a small amount of energy. They are usually not coupled with the formation or consumption of energetic molecules like \(\ce{NADH}\), \(\ce{FADH2}\), or \(\ce{ATP}\).

Exceptions include phosphate ester or thioester group, which are high-energy groups. The addition or removal of phosphate ester or thioester groups without redox reaction is usually coupled with \(\ce{ATP}\)/ \(\ce{ADP}\) conversion. Two reactions, i.e., reactions 1 and 3 in glycolysis (Section 13.5) add phosphate ester bond and consume \(\ce{ATP}\), and two reactions, i.e., reactions 7 and 10, remove phosphate ester and produce \(\ce{ATP}\). One reaction, i.e., reaction 5 in the citric acid cycle (Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\)), involves replacing a thioester group with a carboxylate group without redox. It is coupled with the production of \(\ce{GTP}\), which, in turn, is associated with the output of \(\ce{ATP}\).

In summary:

  • redox reactions involving \(\ce{C=O}\) group relate to \(\ce{NADH}\),
  • redox reactions involving \(\ce{C=C}\) group relate to \(\ce{FADH2}\), and
  • all other reactions that involve energetic groups like phosphate esters or thioesters relate to \(\ce{ATP}\) in food catabolism.

\(\ce{NADH}\) and \(\ce{FADH2}\) are high-energy molecules that are oxidized by \(\ce{O2}\) in part two of the third stage of catabolism of food. The energy released from the oxidation of \(\ce{NADH}\) and \(\ce{FADH2}\) is used to produce more \(\ce{ATPs}\), which is described in the next section.

Lecture Connection

Set up the table (turn your paper sideways): for each of the eight steps, what type of reaction, how you can tell, what class of enzyme, what coenzyme. Several steps are really two reactions in a row with an intermediate in between; the lecture calls those “deconstructed.” Watch the carbon count in every row, and remember the one rule that sorts the coenzymes: an oxidation that makes a C=O (alcohol → carbonyl) uses NAD+; an oxidation that makes a C=C uses FAD.

Professor Tip

For each step you are responsible for the reaction type, the enzyme class and the coenzyme, not for how the enzyme does the chemistry. Keep the rule from this section: oxidizing a C–OH to C=O goes with NADH, oxidizing a C–C to C=C goes with FADH2, and a thioester or phosphate change goes with GTP/ATP.

Three patterns are worth noticing. Every oxidation uses NAD+ except step 6, which uses FAD because a C–C single bond to a C=C is FAD’s reaction (NAD+ handles alcohols and aldehydes, where C–O bonds are forming). Steps 6–8 (alkane-like → alkene → alcohol → ketone) are exactly the alkene hydration and alcohol oxidation you learned in Chapters 3 and 4, now run by enzymes. And the only step that makes an energy carrier directly is step 5; all the rest of the cycle’s energy is stored in NADH and FADH2 for electron transport. The product of step 8 is the starting material for step 1, which is what makes it a cycle. Lactate, pyruvate, and glucose-6-phosphate are not cycle intermediates, a favorite distractor.

Energy in, energy out. No ATP is spent in the cycle. Step 1 is driven by coupling to the hydrolysis of the high-energy thioester in acetyl CoA, which is strongly downhill overall because the products (citrate and free CoA–SH) are more stable; step 5 captures energy as GTP; the four oxidations (steps 3, 4, 6, 8) store energy in 3 NADH and 1 FADH2. Water enters in steps 1 and 7 (step 2 removes a water and then puts one back), which is where the extra hydrogens and oxygens come from.

Practice: (a) In the first half of step 3, isocitrate’s 2° alcohol becomes a ketone. Which coenzyme is involved: FADH2, FAD, NAD+, or NADH + H+? (b) What type of reaction is the second half of step 3: isomerization, dehydration, decarboxylation, or reduction? (c) In step 6, succinate becomes fumarate. Which coenzyme is responsible?

Answer
(a) NAD+: it turns a secondary alcohol into a ketone and is reduced to NADH. (b) Decarboxylation: a carbon leaves as CO2 (6 C → 5 C). (c) FAD: a –CH2–CH2– is oxidized to –CH=CH–, so FAD is reduced to FADH2 (the enzyme is an oxidoreductase).

Regulation and carbon balance

The reaction rate of the cycle is controlled by the body’s need for ATP, through allosteric control (Chapter 11), at two control points:

  • Citrate synthase (step 1): when ATP levels are high, ATP inhibits it; when ATP levels are low, ADP activates it.
  • Isocitrate dehydrogenase (step 3): NADH inhibits it (plenty of reduced coenzyme already waiting for the chain) and ADP activates it.

So the cycle is slowed by high ATP and NADH (the cell has enough energy) and sped up by high ADP, the same logic as phosphofructokinase in glycolysis. Electron transport is regulated the same way (Section 14.4).

Carbon balance. Each turn takes in 2 carbons (acetyl) and releases 2 carbons as CO2, so oxaloacetate is regenerated unchanged; it is a catalyst for the cycle, and one molecule can process acetyl CoA after acetyl CoA. The two carbons released as CO2 in a given turn are not, strictly, the two that just came in as acetyl (they come off the oxaloacetate end of citrate), but the count is what matters: 2 C in, 2 C out, 4-carbon oxaloacetate back. For a whole glucose: 6 C → 2 CO2 (pyruvate dehydrogenase, Section 14.1) + 4 CO2 (two turns) = 6 CO2.

Practice: Which of the following is not an intermediate in the citric acid cycle: citrate, lactate, succinate, or fumarate?

Answer
Lactate. It is the anaerobic product of pyruvate in the cytosol (Section 13.4) and never enters the mitochondrial cycle.

Summary

  • The citric acid cycle oxidizes the two carbons of acetyl CoA to two CO2 in eight steps in the mitochondrial matrix.
  • One turn makes 3 NADH, 1 FADH2 and 1 GTP (worth 1 ATP), which add up to 10 ATP after electron transport.
  • Oxaloacetate is used in step 1 and made again in step 8, so the cycle continues as long as acetyl CoA and NAD+ are available.
  • Oxidizing a C–OH to C=O goes with NADH; making a C=C goes with FADH2.
  • High ATP and NADH slow the cycle; ADP speeds it up.
