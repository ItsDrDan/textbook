What is an acid-base reaction?

An acid-base reaction is a reaction in which a proton (\(\ce{H^{+}}\)) is exchanged between reactants. For example, when acetic acid (\(\ce{CH3COOH}\)) is mixed with water, a proton is transferred from acetic acid to water, as shown in the reaction equation below.

\(\ce{CH3COOH + H2O <=> CH3COO^{-} + H3O^{+}}\)

An acid donates a proton, and a base accepts a proton. \(\ce{CH3COOH}\) is an acid and \(\ce{H2O}\) is a base in the forward reaction. \(\ce{CH3COO^{-}}\) is a base and \(\ce{H3O^{+}}\) is an acid in the reverse reaction. Two species that are related by the addition or removal of a proton are conjugate acid-base pairs. For example, \(\ce{CH3COOH}\) and \(\ce{CH3COO^{-}}\) are a conjugate acid-base pair. Similarly, \(\ce{H3O^{+}}\) and \(\ce{H2O}\) are a conjugate acid-base pair. Since a proton has a +1 charge, its removal from an acid decreases the charge by one, and its addition to a base increases the charge by one. So, a general formula of an acid-conjugate acid-base pair could be represented as \(\ce{HB}\) and \(\ce{B^{-}}\), e.g., \(\ce{CH3COOH}\) and \(\ce{CH3COO^{-}}\) or as \(\ce{HB^{+}}\) and \(\ce{B}\), e.g., \(\ce{H3O^{+}}\) and \(\ce{H2O}\). Acid-base reactions are generally fast and reversible, establishing equilibrium. That is why equilibrium arrows are shown separating reactants and products.

Strength of an acid: pKa

The strength of an acid is its ability to donate a proton (H+) to a base, usually water: HA + H2O → A− + H3O+. Chemists summarize it with one number, the pKa: the lower the pKa, the stronger the acid. Strong mineral acids such as HCl have pKa values below 0. Among the organic acids in this course, carboxylic acids (pKa about 4–5) are much stronger than phenols (about 10), which are stronger than alcohols (about 16). A carboxylic acid gives up its proton easily because, in the carboxylate ion that forms, the negative charge is shared by two oxygen atoms instead of sitting on one.

A base works the other way, by accepting a proton. Amines are the organic bases of this course (Chapter 7): R–NH2 + H2O → R–NH3+ + OH−.

Examples of organic acid-base reactions

Reactions of organic acids

Carboxylic acids are organic acids. The following example shows that when a carboxylic acid is dissolved in water, it dissociates by donating its proton to water.

\(\ce{CH3COOH + H2O <=> CH3COO^{-} + H3O^{+}}\)

The reaction is written as an equilibrium because it goes in both directions, forward and reverse. Acetic acid \(\ce{CH3COOH}\) is the acid in reactants and hydronium ion \(\ce{H3O^{+}}\) is the acid in products.

The general rule is that a stronger acid goes to a weaker one in an acid-base reaction. It is equally correct to say a stronger base goes to a weaker base.

For example, it is more accurate to say that the above reaction is reverse directed, or there is more concentration of reactants than the products at equilibrium, because \(\ce{H3O^{+}}\) is a stronger acid than \(\ce{CH3COOH}\). This fact is represented by a longer equilibrium arrow in the direction of weaker acid in the equilibrium, as shown below.

\[\ce{\underset{pK_a\;4.76}{CH3COOH} + H2O <<=> CH3COO^{-} + \underset{pK_a\;-1.74}{H3O^+}}\nonumber \]

This reaction is made strongly forward directed by using a strong base like \(\ce{^{-}OH}\) that produces a weaker acid \(\ce{H2O}\), making the reaction almost irreversible, as shown below.

\[\ce{\underset{pK_a \;\text 4.76}{CH3COOH} + ^{-}OH -> CH3COO^{-} + \underset{pK_a \;\text 15.7}{H2O}}\nonumber \]

This is an example of an acid-base neutralization reaction. The \(\ce{^{-}OH}\) ion is obtained by dissolving sodium hydroxide \(\ce{NaOH}\) in water, but \(\ce{Na^{+}}\) is a spectator ion, i.e., it does not take part in the reaction and is usually not shown in the equation. As shown below, other organic acids, like phenols and thiols, do similar acid-base neutralization reactions.

\[\ce{\underset{pK_a \;\text 9.95}{C6H5OH} + ^{-}OH -> C6H5O^{-} + \underset{pK_a \;\text 15.7}{H2O}}\nonumber \]

\[\ce{\underset{pK_a \;\text 10.4}{CH3SH} + ^{-}OH -> CH3S^{-} + \underset{pK_a \;\text 15.7}{H2O}}\nonumber \]

Terminal alkyne has acidic protons but they are weak acids (\(\ce{pK_a \;\text ~25}\)). A base like \(\ce{^{-}OH}\) is not a strong enough base to remove all of the alkyne protons, as shown by the following equilibrium reaction.

\[\ce{\underset{pK_a \;\text 25}{R-C≡CH} + ^{-}OH <<=> R-C≡C^{-} + \underset{pK_a \;\text 15.7}{H2O}}\nonumber \]

An amide anion (\(\ce{^{-}NH2}\) in the form of \(\ce{NaNH2}\)) is a sufficiently strong base to neutralize alkyne, as shown below.

\[\ce{\underset{pK_a \;\text 25}{R-C≡CH} + ^{-}NH2 -> R-C≡C^{-} + \underset{pK_a \;\text 38}{NH3}}\nonumber \]

Alcohols (\(\ce{R-OH, pK_a \;\text ~16}\)) are also weak acids and not fully neutralized with bases like \(\ce{^{-}OH}\), as shown below.

\[\ce{\underset{pK_a \;\text 16}{R-OH} + ^{-}OH <=> R-O^{-} + \underset{pK_a \;\text 15.7}{H2O}}\nonumber \]

A hydride ion (\(\ce{H^{-}}\) in the form of \(\ce{NaH}\)) is a sufficiently strong base to neutralize alcohols, as shown below.

\[\ce{\underset{pK_a \;\text 16}{R-OH} + H^{-} -> R-O^{-} + \underset{pK_a \;\text 35}{H2}}\nonumber \]

Reactions of organic bases

Amines (\(\ce{R-NH2}\)) are organic bases that produce \(\ce{^{-}OH}\) ions in water, as shown below.

\[\ce{\underset{pK_a \;\text 15.7}{H2O} + R-NH2 <<=> ^{-}{OH} + \underset{pK_a \;\text 10}{R-NH3^{+}}}\nonumber \]

Amines neutralize strong acids like (\(\ce{HCl}\)) and produce ammonium salts, as shown below.

\[\ce{\underset{pK_a\;-7}{HCl} + R-NH2 -> R-NH3^{+}{Cl^{-}}}\nonumber \]

Amine.HCl drugs: increasing shelf-life and solubility in body fluids

Many drugs contain amine functional groups. Many amine drugs are large molecules that are poorly soluble in water and less stable as the free base. Neutralizing the amine (\(\ce{R-NH2}\)) with \(\ce{HCl}\) converts them to ammonium chloride salts (\(\ce{R-NH3^{+}Cl^{-}}\)), also called amine.HCl salts (\(\ce{R-NH2.HCl}\)), which are water-soluble and more stable. Therefore, drugs containing amine functional groups are usually sold in ammonium chloride form, making them soluble in body fluids like blood plasma and increasing their shelf-life. For example, methadone, a narcotic analgesic, and procaine, a local anesthetic, are marketed as hydrochloride salts, as shown below.

Alcohols are amphoteric substances like water, i.e., they can donate a proton as acid and accept a proton as bases. Like alcohols, the oxygen of carbonyl (\(\ce{C=O}\) group also has two lone pairs and can accept a proton. A strong acid like \(\ce{HCl}\) or \(\ce{H2SO4}\)can protonate an alcohol or a carbonyl group, as shown below.

\[\ce{\underset{pK_a\;-7}{HCl} + R-OH <=>> Cl^{-} + \underset{pK_a\;~-3}{R-OH2^{+}}}\nonumber \]

\[\ce{\underset{pK_a\;-9}{H2SO4} + R-\!\!{\overset{\overset{\Huge\enspace\!{\ce{O}}}|\!\!|\enspace}{\ce{C}}}\!\!-R <=>> HSO4^{-} + \underset{pK_a\;~-7}{R-\!\!\!\!\!\!{\overset{\overset{\Huge\enspace{\overset{\Large{+}}{\ce{O}}\ce{H}}}|\!\!\!\!\!\!|\enspace\enspace}{\ce{C}}}\!\!\!\!\!-R}}\nonumber \]

The acid-base reactions that produce cations by accepting protons, like \(\ce{R-\overset{+}{\ce{O}}\ce{H2}}\) or \(\ce{R-\!\!\!\!\!\!{\overset{\overset{\Huge\enspace{\overset{\Large{+}}{\ce{O}}\ce{H}}}|\!\!\!\!\!\!|\enspace\enspace}{\ce{C}}}\!\!\!\!\!-R}\) and anions by donating protons, like \(\ce{R-O^{-}}\), \(\ce{R-C≡C^{-}}\), \(\ce{R-S^{-}}\), etc. are important intermediates in organic reactions described in the later sections.

Ionization of acidic and basic functional groups at physiological pH

Whether a group is ionized depends on the pH around it compared with its pKa. If the pH is at least two units above the pKa of an acid, almost all of it has lost its proton (A−). If the pH is at least two units below the pKa of a protonated amine (R–NH3+), almost all of it keeps its proton.

The following conclusions are drawn by applying the above rules to the functional groups commonly found in cells.

  • The \(\ce{pH}\) of cells is generally between 7 and 8.5. Carboxylic acids (\(\ce{R-\!\!{\overset{\overset{\Huge\enspace\!{\ce{O}}}|\!\!|\enspace}{\ce{C}}}\!\!-OH}\)) have \(\ce{pK_a}\) 4 to 5. So, carboxylic acids exist in an ionized form \(\ce{R-\!\!{\overset{\overset{\Huge\enspace\!{\ce{O}}}|\!\!|\enspace}{\ce{C}}}\!\!-O^{-}}\) in a physiological medium.
  • The conjugate acid forms of amines \(\ce{R-NH3^{+}}\) have \(\ce{pK_{a}}\) 9 to 11. So, amines exist in the ionized form \(\ce{R-NH3^{+}}\) in a physiological medium.
  • Amino acids \(\ce{R-\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!{\underset{\underset{\Huge\enspace\,{\ce{NH2}}}|}{\ce{C}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!H-\!\!{\overset{\overset{\Huge\enspace\!{\ce{O}}}|\!\!|\enspace}{\ce{C}}}\!\!-OH}\) have amine and carboxylic acid groups. Amino acids exist as \(\ce{R-\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!{\underset{\underset{\Huge\enspace\;\underset{\small{+}}{\ce{N}}{\ce{H3}}}|}{\ce{C}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!H-\!\!{\overset{\overset{\Huge\enspace\!{\ce{O}}}|\!\!|\enspace}{\ce{C}}}\!\!-O^{-}}\). A species with separate positive and negative charge groups on it is called a zwitterion. Amino acids exist as zwitterions.
  • Phosphodiesters \(\ce{RO-\!\!\!\!{\overset{\overset{\Huge\,{\ce{O}}}|\!\!|\enspace}{\underset{\underset{\Huge\;{\ce{OR}}}|}{\ce{P}}}}\!\!\!\!-OH}\) present in DNA and RNA molecules have \(\ce{pK_{a}}\) 1 to 3. So, phosphodiesters also exist in an ionized form \(\ce{RO-\!\!\!{\overset{\overset{\Huge\,{\ce{O}}}|\!\!|\enspace}{\underset{\underset{\Huge\;{\ce{OR}}}|}{\ce{P}}}}\!\!\!-O^{-}}\) in a physiological medium.

Lecture Connection

This block pulls out the three ideas you will use most: what pKa means, where carboxylic acids sit relative to phenols and alcohols, and the rule that tells you whether a drug or metabolite is ionized at blood pH. That rule is the one piece of this section a nurse uses every shift.

Professor Tip

For this section, know: (1) pKa measures acid strength, lower = stronger; (2) the approximate order carboxylic acid (about 4–5) < phenol (about 10) < alcohol (about 16), with strong mineral acids such as HCl below 0; and (3) at a pH well above its pKa, an acid exists as its conjugate base. No Ka or Henderson–Hasselbalch calculations are asked.

pKa: how weak is weak?

Carboxylic acids are weak acids: in water only a small fraction of the molecules give up their proton at any instant, so the ionization is written with equilibrium arrows (Section 6.4). Their strength is described by the acid dissociation constant, usually quoted as the pKa (the lower the pKa, the stronger the acid). Most simple carboxylic acids have pKa values of about 4–5 (acetic acid 4.8, benzoic acid 4.2, lactic acid 3.9). For comparison, phenol has a pKa near 10 and an alcohol near 16, while HCl is a strong acid and ionizes completely. So carboxylic acids are the most acidic organic group you have met, but still far weaker than the mineral acids. Section 4.10 built the alcohol–phenol end of this scale; this section adds the carboxylic acids at the strong end.

Ionized or not? Compare pH with pKa

The pKa tells you the pH at which an acid is half ionized. At any pH more than about two units above the pKa, essentially all of the molecules have lost their proton. Blood is at pH 7.4, roughly three units above the pKa of a typical carboxylic acid, so more than 99% of lactic acid, pyruvic acid, citric acid, aspirin and ibuprofen in the blood exist as the carboxylate ion, RCOO−. That is why these appear on lab reports as lactate, pyruvate, citrate and salicylate. Run the comparison the other way in the stomach (pH about 1–2, well below pKa 4–5) and the same molecules are un-ionized, neutral acids, which is the form that crosses membranes.

Professor Tip

You do not need the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation to answer "ionized or not." Subtract: pH minus pKa. Positive by two or more means the acid has lost its proton (carboxylate, RCOO−); negative by two or more means it has kept it (RCOOH); close to zero means a roughly even mixture. The same arithmetic works for the amines of Chapter 7 and the amino acids of Chapter 10.

Acidosis, in the language of this section

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Salicylate overdose. After aspirin is hydrolyzed (Section 6.8), the circulating salicylate is a carboxylic acid at a pH three units above its pKa, so it is ionized. In a large overdose, salicylate does two things you can now explain. First, it is an acid, and it also disrupts the cell's energy metabolism so that lactic acid and ketone acids build up; the result is metabolic acidosis, a blood pH below 7.35 caused by excess acid. Second, because a carboxylic acid is ionized at high pH, making the urine alkaline with sodium bicarbonate traps salicylate in the kidney tubule as the charged carboxylate, which cannot diffuse back into the blood, so it is excreted faster. Lactic acidosis. The same ionization logic explains lactic acidosis in sepsis and shock: poorly oxygenated tissue makes lactic acid (pKa 3.9), which gives up its proton at blood pH, and the rising lactate level is what the lab reports while the released H+ lowers the blood pH.

Summary

  • pKa measures acid strength: the lower the pKa, the stronger the acid. Carboxylic acid (about 4–5) > phenol (about 10) > alcohol (about 16).
  • Carboxylic acids react with bases to form carboxylate salts; amines react with acids to form ammonium salts.
  • Compare pH with pKa: two or more units above, an acid has lost its proton; two or more units below, it keeps it.
  • At blood pH 7.4, carboxylic acids exist as carboxylate ions and amines as ammonium ions.
