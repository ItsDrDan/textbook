What is an acid-base reaction?

An acid-base reaction is a reaction in which a proton (\(\ce{H^{+}}\)) is exchanged between reactants. For example, when acetic acid (\(\ce{CH3COOH}\)) is mixed with water, a proton is transferred from acetic acid to water, as shown in the reaction equation below.

\(\ce{CH3COOH + H2O <=> CH3COO^{-} + H3O^{+}}\)

An acid donates a proton, and a base accepts a proton. \(\ce{CH3COOH}\) is an acid and \(\ce{H2O}\) is a base in the forward reaction. \(\ce{CH3COO^{-}}\) is a base and \(\ce{H3O^{+}}\) is an acid in the reverse reaction. Two species that are related by the addition or removal of a proton are conjugate acid-base pairs. For example, \(\ce{CH3COOH}\) and \(\ce{CH3COO^{-}}\) are a conjugate acid-base pair. Similarly, \(\ce{H3O^{+}}\) and \(\ce{H2O}\) are a conjugate acid-base pair. Since a proton has a +1 charge, its removal from an acid decreases the charge by one, and its addition to a base increases the charge by one. So, a general formula of a conjugate acid-base pair could be represented as \(\ce{HB}\) and \(\ce{B^{-}}\), e.g., \(\ce{CH3COOH}\) and \(\ce{CH3COO^{-}}\) or as \(\ce{HB^{+}}\) and \(\ce{B}\), e.g., \(\ce{H3O^{+}}\) and \(\ce{H2O}\). Acid-base reactions are generally fast and reversible, establishing equilibrium. That is why equilibrium arrows are shown separating reactants and products.

Strength of an acid

The strength of an acid is its ability to donate a proton (H+) to a base, usually water: HA + H2O \(\rightleftharpoons\) A− + H3O+. A stronger acid gives up its proton more readily. Strong mineral acids such as HCl give up essentially all of their protons in water. Among the organic acids in this course, carboxylic acids are much stronger than phenols, which are stronger than alcohols (alcohols are about as weak as water itself). A carboxylic acid gives up its proton easily because, in the carboxylate ion that forms, the negative charge is shared by two oxygen atoms instead of sitting on one.

A base works the other way, by accepting a proton. Amines are the organic bases of this course (Chapter 7): R–NH2 + H2O \(\rightleftharpoons\) R–NH3+ + OH−.

Examples of organic acid-base reactions

Reactions of organic acids

Carboxylic acids are organic acids. The following example shows that when a carboxylic acid is dissolved in water, it dissociates by donating its proton to water.

\(\ce{CH3COOH + H2O <=> CH3COO^{-} + H3O^{+}}\)

The reaction is written as an equilibrium because it goes in both directions, forward and reverse. Acetic acid \(\ce{CH3COOH}\) is the acid in reactants and hydronium ion \(\ce{H3O^{+}}\) is the acid in products.

The general rule: an acid–base reaction favors the side with the weaker acid and the weaker base.

For acetic acid in water, the reverse reaction is favored: at equilibrium there are more acetic acid molecules than ions, because \(\ce{H3O^{+}}\) is a stronger acid than \(\ce{CH3COOH}\). A longer arrow pointing toward the weaker acid shows this, as below.

\[\ce{CH3COOH + H2O <<=> CH3COO^{-} + H3O^+}\nonumber \]

With a strong base such as \(\ce{^{-}OH}\), the reaction goes almost completely forward, because the acid that forms, \(\ce{H2O}\), is very weak:

\[\ce{CH3COOH + ^{-}OH -> CH3COO^{-} + H2O}\nonumber \]

This is an example of an acid-base neutralization reaction. The \(\ce{^{-}OH}\) ion is obtained by dissolving sodium hydroxide \(\ce{NaOH}\) in water, but \(\ce{Na^{+}}\) is a spectator ion, i.e., it does not take part in the reaction and is usually not shown in the equation. As shown below, other organic acids, like phenols and thiols, do similar acid-base neutralization reactions.

\[\ce{C6H5OH + ^{-}OH -> C6H5O^{-} + H2O}\nonumber \]

\[\ce{CH3SH + ^{-}OH -> CH3S^{-} + H2O}\nonumber \]

Alcohols (\(\ce{R-OH}\)) are about as weak as water and are not neutralized by bases like \(\ce{^{-}OH}\); only a trace reacts, as shown below.

\[\ce{R-OH + ^{-}OH <=> R-O^{-} + H2O}\nonumber \]

In water and in the body, alcohols therefore stay almost entirely as neutral R–OH molecules.

Amines are the organic bases of this course. How they react with water and with acids, and why amine drugs are sold as hydrochloride salts, is covered in Section 7.3.

Ionization of acidic and basic functional groups at physiological pH

Whether a group is ionized depends on how strong an acid (or base) it is and on the pH around it. Body fluids are close to neutral (blood is at pH 7.4), and at that pH the functional groups commonly found in cells behave as follows.

  • Carboxylic acids (\(\ce{R-\!\!{\overset{\overset{\Huge\enspace\!{\ce{O}}}|\!\!|\enspace}{\ce{C}}}\!\!-OH}\)) give up their proton at this pH. So, carboxylic acids exist in an ionized form \(\ce{R-\!\!{\overset{\overset{\Huge\enspace\!{\ce{O}}}|\!\!|\enspace}{\ce{C}}}\!\!-O^{-}}\) in a physiological medium.
  • Amines are bases, and at this pH they pick up a proton. So, amines exist in the ionized form \(\ce{R-NH3^{+}}\) in a physiological medium.
  • Amino acids \(\ce{R-\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!{\underset{\underset{\Huge\enspace\,{\ce{NH2}}}|}{\ce{C}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!H-\!\!{\overset{\overset{\Huge\enspace\!{\ce{O}}}|\!\!|\enspace}{\ce{C}}}\!\!-OH}\) have amine and carboxylic acid groups. Amino acids exist as \(\ce{R-\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!{\underset{\underset{\Huge\enspace\;\underset{\small{+}}{\ce{N}}{\ce{H3}}}|}{\ce{C}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!H-\!\!{\overset{\overset{\Huge\enspace\!{\ce{O}}}|\!\!|\enspace}{\ce{C}}}\!\!-O^{-}}\). A species with separate positive and negative charged groups on it is called a zwitterion. Amino acids exist as zwitterions.
  • Phosphodiesters \(\ce{RO-\!\!\!\!{\overset{\overset{\Huge\,{\ce{O}}}|\!\!|\enspace}{\underset{\underset{\Huge\;{\ce{OR}}}|}{\ce{P}}}}\!\!\!\!-OH}\) present in DNA and RNA molecules are even stronger acids than carboxylic acids. So, phosphodiesters also exist in an ionized form \(\ce{RO-\!\!\!{\overset{\overset{\Huge\,{\ce{O}}}|\!\!|\enspace}{\underset{\underset{\Huge\;{\ce{OR}}}|}{\ce{P}}}}\!\!\!-O^{-}}\) in a physiological medium.

Lecture Connection

This block pulls out the three ideas you will use most: how acid strength is ranked, where carboxylic acids sit relative to phenols and alcohols, and the rule that tells you whether a drug or metabolite is ionized at blood pH. That rule is the one piece of this section a nurse uses every shift.

Professor Tip

For this section, know: (1) the order of acid strength, carboxylic acid > phenol > alcohol, with strong mineral acids such as HCl stronger than all of them; (2) the bench tests that show it: carboxylic acids react with NaHCO3 and NaOH, phenols with NaOH only, alcohols with neither; and (3) the body-pH rule: at pH 7.4, carboxylic acids are carboxylate ions (RCOO−) and amines are ammonium ions (RNH3+). No calculations are asked.

How weak is weak?

Carboxylic acids are weak acids: in water only a small fraction of the molecules give up their proton at any instant, so the ionization is written with equilibrium arrows (Section 6.4). Still, they are the most acidic organic group you have met: much more acidic than phenols, which are more acidic than alcohols. HCl, by contrast, is a strong acid and ionizes completely, so carboxylic acids are far weaker than the mineral acids. Section 4.10 built the alcohol–phenol end of this ranking; this section adds the carboxylic acids at the strong end. The table shows how you see the ranking at the bench.

Ionized or not at body pH?

Blood is at pH 7.4, and at that pH a carboxylic acid has lost its proton: more than 99% of lactic acid, pyruvic acid, citric acid, aspirin and ibuprofen in the blood exist as the carboxylate ion, RCOO−. That is why these appear on lab reports as lactate, pyruvate, citrate and salicylate. In the stomach (pH about 1–2), a strongly acidic place, the same molecules keep their proton and are un-ionized, neutral acids, which is the form that crosses membranes.

Professor Tip

Two rules answer "ionized or not" for almost every molecule in this course. At body pH (about 7.4), write a carboxylic acid as the carboxylate (RCOO−) and an amine as the ammonium ion (RNH3+). In strong acid, such as stomach acid, a carboxylic acid keeps its H (RCOOH). Phenols and alcohols are too weak to lose their H at body pH and stay neutral. The same rules work for the amines of Chapter 7 and the amino acids of Chapter 10.

Acidosis, in the language of this section

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Salicylate overdose. After aspirin is hydrolyzed (Section 6.8), the circulating salicylic acid is a carboxylic acid, so at blood pH it is ionized (salicylate). In a large overdose, salicylate does two things you can now explain. First, it is an acid, and it also disrupts the cell's energy metabolism so that lactic acid and ketone acids build up; the result is metabolic acidosis, a blood pH below 7.35 caused by excess acid. Second, because a carboxylic acid is ionized at high pH, making the urine alkaline with sodium bicarbonate traps salicylate in the kidney tubule as the charged carboxylate, which cannot diffuse back into the blood, so it is excreted faster. Lactic acidosis. The same ionization logic explains lactic acidosis in sepsis and shock: poorly oxygenated tissue makes lactic acid, which gives up its proton at blood pH, and the rising lactate level is what the lab reports while the released H+ lowers the blood pH.

Summary

  • Acid strength: carboxylic acid > phenol > alcohol (alcohols are about as weak as water).
  • Carboxylic acids react with bases to form carboxylate salts; amines react with acids to form ammonium salts.
  • A carboxylic acid keeps its H in strong acid (such as stomach acid) and loses it at body pH.
  • At blood pH 7.4, carboxylic acids exist as carboxylate ions and amines as ammonium ions.
