Learning Objectives

  • Convert between molecular, expanded, condensed, and line-angle (skeletal) formulas.
  • Classify carbons as primary (1°), secondary (2°), tertiary (3°), or quaternary (4°).
  • Dissect an IUPAC name into prefix, parent, and suffix, and name and draw alkanes, haloalkanes, and cycloalkanes using IUPAC rules.
  • Tell the difference between constitutional isomers and the same molecule drawn two ways.
  • Tell skeletal isomers from positional isomers, and isomers from conformations.
  • Draw every constitutional isomer of a small molecular formula without repeating or missing any.
  • Describe the physical properties of hydrocarbons (solubility, melting point, boiling point, flammability) and rank alkanes and haloalkanes by boiling point using intermolecular forces (IMFs).
  • Recognize addition, substitution, and elimination reactions, and write balanced combustion and halogenation equations.

Professor Tip

In a line-angle drawing, every heteroatom (O, N, Cl, etc.) is written out, along with any H attached to it. Only carbon and the hydrogens on carbon are hidden. To count the hidden hydrogens at a corner, subtract the number of lines meeting there from 4. In a condensed formula for a branched molecule, the branch goes in parentheses right after the carbon it hangs from: look to the left of the parentheses to see what the branch is connected to, so CH3CH(CH3)CH2CH3 has its methyl on the second carbon. Order matters: never rearrange atoms for the sake of condensing.

2. Alkane formulas

  • Open-chain (acyclic) alkanes: CnH2n+2
  • Cycloalkanes (one ring): CnH2n. Closing the ring costs two hydrogens.

Hydrocarbons are composed only of carbon and hydrogen, and they come in three shapes: straight-chain (the "n-" or normal alkanes), branched (like trees), and cyclic (rings, drawn as polygons). Acyclic simply means "not a ring." Alkanes and cycloalkanes are saturated hydrocarbons: every carbon has four single bonds, so the molecule is saturated with hydrogen and holds the maximum number of hydrogens its skeleton allows. A hydrocarbon with a C=C or C≡C bond is unsaturated; it has fewer hydrogens than the saturated formula predicts and, as you will see in Chapter 3, it can take up more atoms by addition. A quick test: an open-chain compound whose formula fits CnH2n+2 is saturated; every pair of missing hydrogens means one ring or one double bond.

An alkyl group is a carbon chain that branches off the parent: same count names, with the ending changed to -yl. Halogen substituents: fluoro, chloro, bromo, iodo. Carbon counts of 1–4 have the special prefixes meth-, eth-, prop-, but-; from 5 on the Greek-number prefixes take over (pent-, hex-, like pentagon and hexagon).

Three- and four-carbon branches come in more than one shape, and the common names in the figure below are still used on labels and in this course. The straight versions are propyl and butyl. Isopropyl attaches through its middle carbon and looks like a Y (or a triangle). With four carbons there are three branched shapes: isobutyl (a Y attached through the stem), sec-butyl (attached through a CH that has two side chains), and tert-butyl or t-butyl (attached through a carbon that carries three methyl groups). Learn the shapes; you will see them again as the 2° and 3° carbons in Key Idea 8.

4. The naming recipe (IUPAC)

Systematic nomenclature was devised by the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) so that you can determine a structure from its name alone: memorize a few rules instead of millions of common names. Every IUPAC name has three parts. The parent gives the number of carbons in the longest continuous chain. The suffix tells which functional group is present (for now your only suffix is -ane; each later chapter adds one). The prefix gives the identity, location, and number of the substituents on the chain. Dissect 4-ethyl-2,3,6-trimethylnonane: prefix 4-ethyl-2,3,6-trimethyl, parent non (9 carbons), suffix -ane. Underline the prefix, box the parent, circle the suffix, and the structure draws itself.

  1. Find the longest continuous carbon chain. Strategy: circle every end CH3 and count the carbons between each pair of ends. The longest chain is often not drawn in a straight line.
  2. Number the chain to minimize the number of the first substituent. If there is a tie, look at the next substituent. If it is still tied, give the lower number to the group that comes first alphabetically.
  3. Name and locate each substituent. Use di-, tri-, tetra- for repeats, and give every copy its own number (2,2-dimethyl, not 2-dimethyl).
  4. Alphabetize the substituents, ignoring di-/tri-/tetra-. Use commas between numbers and hyphens between numbers and words.

Two questions worth asking yourself: can an alkyl branch ever sit on carbon 1? (No: a branch on C1 just makes the chain longer.) What is the longest branch that can sit on carbon 2? (Only a methyl, for the same reason; an ethyl on C2 would become part of a longer parent chain.)

Rings: count the carbons in the ring and add cyclo- to the parent. With one substituent, that substituent is on carbon 1 and no number is needed (methylcyclopentane, chlorocyclohexane). With two or more, start numbering at a substituent and go the direction that gives the lowest set of numbers; if two choices give the same set, the substituent that comes first alphabetically gets C1. So a cyclohexane with Cl and CH3 on carbons 1 and 3 is 1-chloro-3-methylcyclohexane (chloro gets C1), and a cyclopentane with three methyls on adjacent carbons is 1,2,3-trimethylcyclopentane. Halogens are treated exactly like alkyl groups: they are substituents, never part of the parent name.

5. Properties of alkanes

What an alkane is used for depends on its physical state, and its physical state depends on the strength of its dispersion forces, which grow with chain length.

Physical state at room temperatureChain lengthUsesExamples
GasC1–C4FuelNatural gas (methane), propane, butane
LiquidC5–C17Fuels, solventsGasoline, hexane, kerosene, mineral oil
SolidC18 and upWaxes, lubricants, barriersParaffin wax, petroleum jelly
  • Nonpolar, so they have dispersion forces only. They are insoluble in water and less dense than water, so they float, like an oil slick.
  • Melting point and boiling point rise with chain length, because a larger molecule (higher molar mass, more electrons, more surface contact) has more dispersion forces. Branching lowers them: branched molecules cannot pack as tightly together as straight chains, so there is less contact and less dispersion force between neighbors.
  • Haloalkanes have a polar C–X bond, so they add dipole–dipole attraction to dispersion, and a heavy halogen adds many electrons. A haloalkane therefore boils higher than the alkane of the same skeleton. Neither alkanes nor haloalkanes can hydrogen bond, and both are insoluble in water.
  • Mostly unreactive except for combustion (alkane + O2 → CO2 + H2O + energy) and halogenation (an H is replaced by Cl or Br with light or heat). Flammability is the one property that makes hydrocarbons dangerous to handle: gasoline, propane, and the vapor above hexane in a lab bottle all ignite easily.

The table below shows how the two trends play out in real boiling points. Read down the first column to see the chain-length effect, and across the C5H12 row to see the branching effect.

Professor Tip

To rank boiling points, ask three questions in order: (1) Can it hydrogen bond? (not in this chapter) (2) Which is bigger, with more electrons? (3) Among isomers, which is less branched? Bigger and straighter boils higher. Melting points follow the same size trend, and water solubility is the same for all of them: essentially zero.

6. Conformations and rings

  • Single bonds rotate freely, so one molecule can twist into many conformations. Different conformations of the same molecule are not isomers.
  • Cycloalkanes are not flat. Cyclohexane folds into a "chair" shape that keeps every bond angle near 109.5°.
  • Because a ring cannot rotate, two substituents on a ring can be on the same face (cis) or opposite faces (trans): cis- and trans-1,2-dimethylcyclohexane are stereoisomers.

What rotation does and does not change. Molecules are 3-D and constantly in motion. Picture ethane as two CH3 groups joined by one C–C bond: spin one CH3 like a propeller and you get a new conformation, but no bond breaks and the atoms stay connected in the same order. The same is true of butane twisting about its C2–C3 bond: a "stretched out" zig-zag and a "folded" shape are the same compound, just drawn from another view. Double and triple bonds, by contrast, cannot rotate (Chapter 3). To turn one isomer into another you must break and remake a bond; to turn one conformation into another you only rotate.

Why rings pucker. A flat hexagon has 120° corners, but a carbon with four single bonds wants 109.5°. Cyclohexane solves this by puckering: alternate carbons tip up and down, giving the chair shape in the figure below, in which every bond angle is close to 109.5°. Larger rings pucker for the same reason. Still, we draw rings as flat polygons, because the drawing is only a map of which atoms are bonded, not a photograph of the shape.

One consequence of puckering is worth knowing even though it is optional in this course: a ring has two faces. If two substituents sit on the same face of the ring they are cis; on opposite faces, trans. Because the ring bonds cannot rotate all the way around, the two arrangements cannot interconvert, so they are different compounds (stereoisomers) with different melting points and boiling points. You will meet the same cis/trans idea again with alkenes in Chapter 3.

7. Three reaction types you will see all semester

  • Addition: two reactants combine into one product; a multiple bond becomes a single bond (Chapter 3).
  • Elimination: one reactant splits off a small molecule and forms a multiple bond (Chapters 3 and 4).
  • Substitution: one atom or group is swapped for another. Alkane halogenation is a substitution: CH4 + Cl2 → CH3Cl + HCl (requires light or heat).

Almost every organic reaction in this course is one of these three types. The table compares them by what happens to the count of atoms and bonds, which is the fastest way to recognize each one.

Reaction typeCount of moleculesWhat happens to bondsExampleWhere you will see it
Addition2 reactants → 1 productA double (or triple) bond becomes a single bond; two new atoms attach, one to each carbonCH2=CH2 + H2 → CH3CH3Alkenes (Ch. 3); carbonyls (Ch. 5)
Substitution2 reactants → 2 productsOne atom or group leaves and another takes its place; the number of bonds to carbon does not changeCH3CH3 + Cl2 → CH3CH2Cl + HClAlkane halogenation (Ch. 2); benzene (Ch. 3); esters and amides (Ch. 6–7)
Elimination1 reactant → 2 productsTwo atoms or groups on adjacent carbons are removed as a small molecule (H2O, HX) and a double bond formsCH3CH2OH → CH2=CH2 + H2ODehydration of alcohols (Ch. 4)

Professor Tip

Count the arrows' two sides. Two things becoming one is addition; one thing becoming two is elimination; two in and two out, with a piece traded, is substitution. Then confirm with the multiple bond: addition uses up a double bond, elimination creates one, and substitution leaves the double-bond count alone. Combustion is its own category: it is an oxidation that breaks every bond in the fuel.

8. Classifying carbons: 1°, 2°, 3°, 4°

Every carbon in an alkane can be labeled by how many other carbons it is bonded to. A primary (1°) carbon is bonded to one other carbon, a secondary (2°) carbon to two, a tertiary (3°) carbon to three, and a quaternary (4°) carbon to four. Count carbon neighbors, not hydrogens: in an alkane a 1° carbon happens to be a CH3, a 2° carbon a CH2, a 3° carbon a CH, and a 4° carbon has no H at all, but the definition is about the carbon–carbon bonds. That matters later, because in an alcohol the carbon carrying the OH is classified by the same rule even though it has lost a hydrogen to the oxygen.

Why bother? The classification predicts behavior. For a hydrocarbon it also tells you the hydrogen count at a glance: a 1° carbon carries three H, a 2° carbon two, a 3° carbon one, and a 4° carbon none. In Chapter 4 the same labels decide whether an alcohol can be oxidized, and in Chapter 7 they describe amines and amides. Learn the counting rule now and you will use it all semester.

9. Constitutional isomers: skeletal and positional

Constitutional (structural) isomers share a molecular formula but connect their atoms in a different order. For alkanes and haloalkanes there are two kinds you must recognize:

  • Skeletal isomers differ in the carbon skeleton itself: the chain is longer or shorter, with branches in different places. Pentane, 2-methylbutane, and 2,2-dimethylpropane are the three skeletal isomers of C5H12 (part (a) of the figure below).
  • Positional isomers keep the same skeleton but put a substituent on a different carbon. 1-Chloropropane and 2-chloropropane are positional isomers of C3H7Cl (part (b) of the figure below); so are 1,1-dichloroethane and 1,2-dichloroethane.

Professor Tip

Same molecule or constitutional isomer? Find the terminal CH3 groups and count the carbons in between. If both drawings have the same longest chain with the same branches in the same places (that is, the same IUPAC name), they are the same molecule drawn from another view. To draw all the isomers of a formula, work from the longest chain down: draw the straight chain, then shorten it by one carbon and move the leftover methyl along the chain (never onto an end carbon), then shorten it again and place two methyls, and so on. Name each one as you go; a repeat name means a repeat structure.

Isomers are different compounds with different boiling points, and their names differ either in the parent or the substituent list (skeletal) or only in a locant number (positional). Two drawings that give the same IUPAC name are the same compound, however differently they are twisted on the page; that is a conformation, not an isomer. In Chapter 4 you will add a third kind, functional group isomers, such as an alcohol and an ether with the same formula.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Naming a branched alkane

Name CH3CH(CH3)CH2CH(CH2CH3)CH2CH3.

Solution

  1. Longest chain: 6 carbons, so the parent is hexane. (Running the chain through the ethyl branch also gives 6, and it leads to the same name.)
  2. Number from the left: a methyl on C2 and an ethyl on C4, giving {2, 4}. From the right: {3, 5}. Left wins.
  3. Alphabetize: ethyl comes before methyl.

Name: 4-ethyl-2-methylhexane (C9H20)

Example 2: Breaking a tie with the alphabet

Name CH3CHBrCH2CH(CH3)CH3.

Solution

The longest chain has 5 carbons (pentane). Numbering from either end puts substituents on C2 and C4, so it is a tie. Bromo comes before methyl, so bromo gets the lower number.

Name: 2-bromo-4-methylpentane

Example 3: Drawing from a name

Write the condensed formula and molecular formula for 3-ethyl-2,4-dimethylpentane.

Solution

  1. Draw a 5-carbon chain and number it 1–5.
  2. Put methyls on C2 and C4 and an ethyl on C3.
  3. Fill in H's so each carbon has 4 bonds.

Condensed: CH3CH(CH3)CH(CH2CH3)CH(CH3)CH3

Carbons: 5 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 9, so by CnH2n+2 it is C9H20.

Example 4: Classifying carbons

Classify each carbon in 2,2,4-trimethylpentane (the "isooctane" of octane ratings): (CH3)3CCH2CH(CH3)CH3.

Solution

  • C1, C5, and all three methyl branches: each is bonded to 1 other carbon → primary (1°)
  • C2: bonded to 4 carbons → quaternary (4°)
  • C3: bonded to 2 carbons → secondary (2°)
  • C4: bonded to 3 carbons → tertiary (3°)

This skill comes right back when we classify alcohols as 1°, 2°, or 3° in Chapter 4.

Example 5: Isomers or the same molecule?

Are CH3CH2CH(CH3)CH3 and CH3CH(CH3)CH2CH3 isomers?

Solution

Find the terminal CH3 groups and count the carbons between them: in both formulas the longest chain is 4 carbons with a methyl on the second carbon from one end. Name both: each is 2-methylbutane (numbered from the closer end). They are the same molecule written in opposite directions, not isomers. Naming is the fastest way to check.

Example 6: Combustion

Write a balanced equation for the complete combustion of propane, C3H8 (barbecue fuel).

Solution

Balance C first, then H, then O last:

C3H8 + 5 O2 → 3 CO2 + 4 H2O

Check: 3 C, 8 H, and 10 O on each side. ✓

Example 7: Classifying every carbon from a line structure

Problem. Using the labeled structure in Key Idea 8, classify each carbon in 3-ethyl-2,2-dimethylpentane and give the molecular formula.

Solution. Number the five-carbon chain so that the substituents get the lowest numbers (2,2,3), then count carbon neighbors at each position.

  • C1: bonded only to C2 → 1°. C5: bonded only to C4 → 1°.
  • C2: bonded to C1, C3, and two methyl carbons → four neighbors → 4°.
  • C3: bonded to C2, C4, and the ethyl CH2 → three neighbors → 3°.
  • C4: bonded to C3 and C5 → 2°. The CH2 of the ethyl group is bonded to C3 and to its own CH3 → also 2°.
  • The two methyls on C2 and the CH3 of the ethyl group each touch one carbon → 1°.

Totals: five 1°, two 2°, one 3°, one 4° carbon, nine carbons in all, so the formula is C9H20.

Check. Hydrogens: five CH3 (15 H) + two CH2 (4 H) + one CH (1 H) + a quaternary carbon (0 H) = 20 H, which matches CnH2n+2 for n = 9. ✓

Example 8: Naming a disubstituted cycloalkane

Problem. A cyclopentane ring carries a bromine on one carbon and an ethyl group on the carbon next to it. Name it and give its molecular formula.

Solution. The parent is cyclopentane. With two substituents, numbering must start at one of them. Starting at either substituent and going toward the other gives the set {1, 2}, a tie, so the alphabetical rule decides: bromo precedes ethyl and gets C1. Name: 1-bromo-2-ethylcyclopentane.

Formula: ethylcyclopentane is C7H14 (CnH2n for a ring with 7 carbons), and the bromine replaces one hydrogen: C7H13Br.

Check. Would "2-bromo-1-ethylcyclopentane" also give the locants {1, 2}? Yes, but it breaks the alphabetical tie incorrectly; only one name is right. Notice also that this compound exists as cis and trans forms, because the ring holds the two groups on the same face or on opposite faces. ✓

Example 9: Ranking boiling points with IMFs

Problem. Rank from lowest to highest boiling point: hexane, 2,2-dimethylbutane, and 1-chlorohexane.

Solution. None of the three can hydrogen bond, so compare size and polarity, then branching.

  1. Hexane and 2,2-dimethylbutane are both C6H14 (same number of electrons), so dispersion forces are the only IMF and branching decides. 2,2-Dimethylbutane is compact, so it boils lower (about 50 °C) than hexane (69 °C).
  2. 1-Chlorohexane has the hexane skeleton with a chlorine (17 electrons) in place of one hydrogen (1 electron): many more electrons for stronger dispersion forces and a polar C–Cl bond that adds dipole–dipole attraction. It boils highest (about 135 °C).

Ranking: 2,2-dimethylbutane < hexane < 1-chlorohexane.

Check. All three are insoluble in water, so solubility could not have separated them; boiling point can, and the measured values agree with the ranking. ✓

Example 10: Halogenation gives more than one product

Problem. 2-Methylpropane, (CH3)3CH, is treated with Cl2 in light. Classify the reaction, and write every product that can form by replacing one hydrogen.

Solution. This is a substitution: Cl takes the place of an H, and the H leaves with the other Cl as HCl. The molecule has two kinds of hydrogen: nine on the three equivalent CH3 groups (1° carbons) and one on the central CH (a 3° carbon). Replacing a hydrogen of each kind gives a different positional isomer:

  • (CH3)3CH + Cl2 → (CH3)2CHCH2Cl + HCl   (1-chloro-2-methylpropane)
  • (CH3)3CH + Cl2 → (CH3)3CCl + HCl   (2-chloro-2-methylpropane)

Both form, so the result is a mixture. With excess Cl2 the chloro products are chlorinated again, giving dichloro and trichloro compounds, which is why halogenation is rarely a clean way to make a single compound.

Check. Each equation has 4 C, 10 H, and 2 Cl on both sides, and the number of bonds to carbon has not changed, which is the signature of substitution. ✓

Example 11: Dissecting a name and drawing the structure

Problem. Dissect the name 3-bromo-4-ethyl-2-methylheptane into its prefix, parent, and suffix, then write the condensed structural formula and the molecular formula.

Solution. Prefix: 3-bromo-4-ethyl-2-methyl (three substituents with their locations, listed alphabetically). Parent: hept, a seven-carbon chain. Suffix: -ane, all single bonds and no other functional group.

  1. Draw seven carbons in a row and number them 1 to 7.
  2. Attach a CH3 to C2, a Br to C3, and a CH2CH3 to C4.
  3. Fill in hydrogens until every carbon has four bonds.

Condensed: CH3CH(CH3)CHBrCH(CH2CH3)CH2CH2CH3. Carbons: 7 + 1 + 2 = 10. The alkane C10H22 loses one H for the bromine: C10H21Br.

Check. Re-name your drawing. Is the seven-carbon chain really the longest? A path that turns off at C4 and runs out through the ethyl group is only 4 + 2 = 6 carbons (or 3 + 1 + 2 = 6 from the other end), so the straight seven wins. Numbering from the other end would put the first substituent on C4 instead of C2, so the original direction is correct, and the three prefixes are in alphabetical order (bromo, ethyl, methyl). ✓

Example 12: Drawing every isomer of a formula

Problem. Draw and name all the constitutional isomers of C6H14.

Solution. Start with the longest possible chain and shorten it step by step.

  1. Six-carbon chain: hexane. (1 isomer)
  2. Five-carbon chain + one methyl. The methyl cannot go on C1 or C5 (that would just be hexane again), and C4 is the same as C2 numbered from the other end. So: 2-methylpentane and 3-methylpentane. (2 isomers)
  3. Four-carbon chain + two methyls. Both on C2 (2,2-dimethylbutane) or one on C2 and one on C3 (2,3-dimethylbutane). "2,4-dimethylbutane" is impossible because C4 is an end carbon. (2 isomers)
  4. Three-carbon chain + three methyls: impossible; the middle carbon can hold only two branches, and any more would lengthen the chain.

Total: five isomers, shown below.

Check. Each drawing has six carbons and, by CnH2n+2, fourteen hydrogens. The five names are all different, so no structure has been drawn twice. "2-ethylbutane" and "3-methylpentane" would describe the same molecule, which is why you name each one before counting it. ✓

Health Career Connection

  • Nursing – petroleum jelly and mineral oil: Petrolatum (Vaseline) and mineral oil are mixtures of long-chain alkanes. Because alkanes are nonpolar and do not dissolve in water, a thin layer on skin holds moisture in. That is why they are used as protective barriers for dry skin, diaper rash, and chapped lips.
  • Dental hygiene – waxes: Paraffin wax used in dental wax-ups, bite registration, and floss coatings is made of solid alkanes with roughly 20–40 carbons. Long chains mean strong dispersion forces, which is why the wax is solid at room temperature but softens with gentle heat.
  • Safety – oxygen and combustion: Alkanes burn readily in O2. For patients on supplemental oxygen, petroleum-based products on the face are discouraged as a fire risk, and water-based products are used instead.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

The inhaled anesthetics used in surgery today, sevoflurane (C4H3F7O) and isoflurane (C3H2ClF5O), are small, heavily halogenated ethers: saturated carbon skeletons in which most hydrogens have been swapped for fluorine. Their chemistry explains how they are given. They have no O–H or N–H, so they cannot hydrogen bond, and their IMFs are weak enough that they boil near 59 °C and 49 °C. A liquid that volatile evaporates readily in the anesthesia machine's vaporizer, mixes with oxygen, and reaches the lungs as a gas; being nonpolar overall, it crosses lipid membranes into the brain and leaves the same way when the gas is turned off. The C–F bonds are strong and unreactive, which is why these drugs are not flammable and are largely exhaled unchanged. Their history is a lesson in structure: chloroform (CHCl3), introduced in 1847, caused liver damage and dangerous heart rhythms, and halothane, a bromo-chloro-fluoro haloalkane adopted in 1956, was later linked to a rare hepatitis. The fluorinated ethers replaced both.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Three alkane-based materials turn up in a dental operatory, and each works because alkanes are nonpolar. Orthodontic relief wax and the wax used for bite registration are paraffin, a blend of C20–C40 alkanes: enough dispersion force to be a solid, but weak enough that it softens with finger warmth and molds over a bracket that is irritating the cheek. Petroleum jelly (petrolatum) is painted on the lips and sometimes the gingiva (gum tissue) before in-office bleaching. The bleaching gel is a water-based solution of hydrogen peroxide; because water and a nonpolar alkane film do not mix, the gel beads up on the barrier instead of reaching tissue, protecting it from chemical irritation. PTFE (polytetrafluoroethylene) is a long carbon chain in which every hydrogen is replaced by fluorine. It is the slick coating on some dental floss and the thin tape used to isolate adjacent teeth during composite placement: chemically inert, nonstick, and nonpolar, it slides between tight contacts without shredding and does not bond to the resin.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Missing the longest chain. "2-ethylbutane" is not a correct name, because the ethyl group extends the chain. It is really 3-methylpentane.
  • A branch on carbon 1. "1-methylpropane" is just butane. An alkyl group on C1 means you did not pick the longest chain.
  • Alphabetizing by "di" or "tri." It is 4-ethyl-2,2-dimethyl..., alphabetized under e and m.
  • Condensing a ring as a chain. Write cyclohexane as a hexagon or as "cyclo-," not (CH2)6 with no indication of the ring.
  • Calling a conformation an isomer. If two drawings give the same IUPAC name, they are the same compound no matter how the chain is bent. Isomers need a different name.
  • Classifying a carbon by its hydrogens. Count the carbons attached. The rule still works when a hydrogen has been replaced by Cl or OH, which is exactly when you will need it in Chapters 2 and 4.
  • Calling halogenation an addition. Cl2 does not add to an alkane; there is no double bond to add across. One Cl replaces an H and the other leaves as HCl, so the reaction is a substitution with two products.
  • Forgetting that halogenation gives a mixture. Any alkane with more than one kind of hydrogen gives more than one monohalogenated product, and further substitution adds di- and trihalo compounds.
  • Drawing the same isomer twice. A methyl on C4 of a pentane chain is 2-methylpentane numbered backwards. Name every structure before you count it.
  • Rearranging atoms to condense a formula. CH3CH(CH3)CH2CH3 and CH3CH2CH(CH3)CH3 are fine (same molecule, read from either end), but moving a group to a different carbon makes a different compound.

Draw (condensed) and name all three constitutional isomers of C5H12.

Answer
CH3CH2CH2CH2CH3 (pentane); CH3CH(CH3)CH2CH3 (2-methylbutane); C(CH3)4 (2,2-dimethylpropane)

Exercise 4

Write the molecular formula of 1-chloro-3-methylcyclohexane.

Answer
C7H13Cl. Methylcyclohexane is C7H14 (CnH2n), and Cl replaces one H.

Exercise 5

Balance the complete combustion of butane, C4H10.

Answer
2 C4H10 + 13 O2 → 8 CO2 + 10 H2O

Exercise 6

Rank from lowest to highest boiling point: octane, propane, 2-methylheptane.

Answer
Propane < 2-methylheptane < octane. Propane is much smaller. Octane and 2-methylheptane are both C8H18, but branching lowers the boiling point.

Exercise 7

Classify each reaction as addition, substitution, or elimination: (a) CH3CH3 + Br2 → CH3CH2Br + HBr; (b) CH2=CH2 + H2 → CH3CH3.

Answer
(a) Substitution (Br replaces an H); (b) addition
