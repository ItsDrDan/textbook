Learning Objectives

  • Classify amines as primary (1°), secondary (2°), tertiary (3°), or quaternary ammonium, and amides as 1°, 2°, or 3°.
  • Name simple amines (common and IUPAC), aniline derivatives, and amides, and recognize the common heterocyclic amine rings.
  • Rank amines and amides by boiling point and water solubility using hydrogen bonding.
  • Write the reactions of amines as bases (with water and with acids) and explain why drugs are sold as ammonium salts.
  • Draw the amide formed from a carboxylic acid and an amine, and the products of amide hydrolysis in acid and in base.
  • Recognize amide and ester links in polyamides, polyesters, and peptides.

Key Ideas

1. Amines: classification and names

An amine is a derivative of ammonia, NH3, in which one, two, or three hydrogens have been replaced by carbon groups. The nitrogen keeps its lone pair, and that lone pair is the source of almost everything amines do.

Professor Tip

Amines are classified differently from alcohols. Look at the nitrogen and count the R groups (carbon groups) bonded directly to N: 1 = 1°, 2 = 2°, 3 = 3°, 4 (with a + charge) = quaternary ammonium ion. (For alcohols, you went to the carbon holding the OH and counted its C–C bonds.) So (CH3)3CNH2 is a primary amine even though its carbon is tertiary. Quaternary means four bonds to N: that is an ammonium ion, not a fourth kind of amine.

Drawing amines. In a line (skeletal) structure the hydrogen atoms on nitrogen must be shown (NH2, NH), because that is how you tell a 1° amine from a 2° or 3° one. In a condensed structure, identical groups on N can be grouped: CH3CH2NHCH2CH3 is also written (CH3CH2)2NH, and a group branching off the nitrogen of a 3° amine goes in parentheses after the N: CH3CH2CH2N(CH3)CH2CH3 has a methyl on N between a propyl and an ethyl group.

  • Common names: list the alkyl groups alphabetically + "amine", written as one word: ethylmethylamine, triethylamine.
  • IUPAC, 1° amines: find the longest carbon chain that contains the carbon holding the NH2, change the -e of the alkane to -amine, number so that N gets the lowest locant, then apply all the other nomenclature rules: 1-propanamine, 2-butanamine.
  • IUPAC, 2° and 3° amines: the longest alkyl chain bonded to N is the parent amine; every other group on the nitrogen is named as N-alkyl (the locant N says "this is on the nitrogen"), listed alphabetically if there is more than one: N-methylethanamine, N,N-dimethyl-1-propanamine, N-ethyl-N-methylcyclohexanamine.
  • C6H5NH2 is aniline, and its derivatives are named as anilines (Key Idea 5).
  • More than one functional group: an acid, aldehyde, ketone, or alcohol outranks an amine, so when one of those is present the NH2 becomes the substituent amino- and the parent keeps its own suffix: 2-aminoethanol, 4-aminobutanoic acid, 4-amino-2-pentanone. The amine is the parent only when nothing of higher priority is present; halogens and alkyl groups are always substituents (3-chloro-N-methyl-1-butanamine). Example 9 works through the choice.

2. Properties of amines

  • Shape and polarity. The nitrogen has four electron groups (three bonds and the lone pair): tetrahedral electron-group geometry, trigonal pyramidal molecular geometry, bond angles near 109.5°. The N–H bond is polar; the C–N bond is polar too, but only slightly. So every amine is a polar molecule.
  • Intermolecular forces (IMFs) between amine molecules: dispersion (every molecule has these), dipole–dipole (the molecule is polar), and hydrogen bonding, but only for 1° and 2° amines, which have an N–H. N–H hydrogen bonds are weaker than O–H hydrogen bonds because N is less electronegative than O.
  • Ranking boiling and melting points. 1° and 2° amines boil lower than alcohols of similar size (weaker hydrogen bonds), higher than ethers of similar size (ethers cannot hydrogen bond with themselves), and higher than 3° amines (no N–H, so no hydrogen bonding between 3° amine molecules; they rely on dipole–dipole and dispersion forces). Methylamines and ethylamines are gases at room temperature; most other amines are liquids.
  • Solubility. 1° and 2° amines both donate and accept hydrogen bonds with water. 3° amines cannot donate, but the lone pair on N still accepts a hydrogen bond from water, so small 3° amines dissolve too, just not as well. Amines with up to about six carbons have appreciable water solubility; beyond that the nonpolar chain takes over. Solutions are basic (Key Idea 3).
  • Many amines have foul odors: rotting fish, urine, bad breath.

Amides stand out. Between two amide molecules there are dispersion forces, dipole–dipole forces (the C=O is strongly polar, and the C–N is polar too), and, for 1° and 2° amides, hydrogen bonds. The C=O makes the N–H bonds more polar, and each 1° amide can donate two hydrogen bonds and accept at the oxygen, so amides have the highest boiling and melting points of any family in this book. Nearly all 1° amides are solids at room temperature (methanamide, HCONH2, is the liquid exception). The same comparisons you made for amines work for amides:

  • 1° amides boil and melt higher than similar 2° amides (two N–H bonds versus one), and both are higher than 3° amides, which have no N–H.
  • 1° and 2° amides are higher than esters of similar size, because esters cannot hydrogen bond with themselves.
  • Even a 3° amide is higher than an alkane of similar size, because the polar C=O gives it dipole–dipole attractions that the alkane lacks.
  • Solubility: 1° and 2° amides with about five carbons or fewer dissolve in water (they donate and accept hydrogen bonds); 3° amides dissolve too, but less well, because they can only accept. N,N-dimethylmethanamide (DMF) is the familiar example of a fully water-miscible 3° amide.

3. Amines are bases

Amines are proton (H+) acceptors, which makes them Brønsted–Lowry bases; they accept the proton by donating the lone pair on N, which also makes them Lewis bases. Either way, the lone pair on N picks up an H+:

  • With water: RNH2 + H2O ⇌ RNH3+ + OH− (a weak base; the solution is basic)
  • With an acid: RNH2 + HCl → RNH3+Cl− (an ammonium salt; this is a neutralization, and no water is formed, unlike NaOH + HCl)
  • With a carboxylic acid: RNH2 + R′COOH → RNH3+ R′COO− (an ammonium carboxylate salt; at room temperature this salt is all that forms, see Key Idea 4)

Amines are weak bases compared with inorganic bases such as NaOH: in water only a small fraction of the molecules are protonated at any moment, so the equilibrium arrow points both ways. Among organic compounds, though, amines are the strongest bases you will meet. At blood pH 7.4 most molecules of a typical amine drug exist as the ammonium ion. Aromatic amines such as aniline are much weaker bases because the ring shares the lone pair.

Naming the ions and salts. Replace -amine with -ammonium for the cation, then combine the ion names for the salt: methylamine gives the methylammonium ion and methylammonium chloride; aniline gives anilinium chloride; trimethylamine gives trimethylammonium acetate with acetic acid.

Ammonium salts are ionic, odorless solids that dissolve well in water and body fluids. A water-insoluble amine is converted to a water-soluble ammonium salt simply by treating it with acid. That is why many drug labels say "hydrochloride" (HCl): lidocaine hydrochloride in a dental cartridge and diphenhydramine hydrochloride (Benadryl) in an allergy capsule are the amine drugs converted to their ammonium chloride salts, which dissolve for injection or for absorption in the gut and are carried in the bloodstream. Quaternary ammonium salts are used as antiseptics: benzalkonium chloride is a disinfectant in surgical scrubs, instrument-storage solutions, and some mouthwashes (its bitter taste is also used to discourage nail biting). The reaction runs backward just as easily: adding a strong base to an ammonium salt removes the H+ and frees the amine.

RNH3+Cl− + NaOH → RNH2 + H2O + NaCl

4. Amides

An amide is one more carbonyl functional group: the acyl group (RCO–) is bonded to a nitrogen atom, so the C=O carbon carries an N instead of the OH of a carboxylic acid or the OR of an ester. Amides are classified as 1°, 2°, or 3° just like amines, by the number of carbon groups on the nitrogen other than the carbonyl carbon. Reading a condensed formula: CH3CH2CONHCH3 and CH3NHCOCH2CH3 are the same amide written from opposite ends (CO next to N either way), while CH3NHCH2COCH3 is not an amide: its N is bonded to a CH2, and the C=O is a ketone one carbon away.

  • Naming: amide names blend carboxylic acid and amine naming. Name the acyl group as you would the carboxylic acid, but change -oic acid (IUPAC) or -ic acid (common) to -amide: ethanamide (acetamide), butanamide, benzamide. Any R groups on the nitrogen get the N- prefix, alphabetically: N-methylethanamide, N-ethyl-N-methylethanamide, N,N-diethylbenzamide. Substituents on the carbon chain get numbers, counting from the C=O carbon as C1, and are listed in the same alphabetical string: N,3-dimethylbutanamide has one methyl on N and one on C3.
  • Amidation (formation): heating a carboxylic acid with ammonia, a 1° amine, or a 2° amine replaces the –OH of the acid with –NR2, releasing water: a condensation. The first thing an acid and an amine do at room temperature is form an ammonium carboxylate salt (Key Idea 3); the reaction must be heated to drive off water and reach the amide.

    RCOOH + H2NR′ →(heat) RCONHR′ + H2O

    3° amines have no H on the nitrogen, so they do not form amides; with a carboxylic acid they give only the ammonium salt. Chemists call amidation a nucleophilic acyl substitution: the amine nitrogen (the nucleophile, an electron-pair donor) takes the place of the –OH on the acyl carbon. Esterification and ester hydrolysis (Chapter 6) are the same reaction type; all acyl compounds (acids, esters, amides) share it.
  • Amides are not basic. The C=O pulls electron density from the N, giving the C–N bond partial double-bond character, so the lone pair is not available to pick up H+. Amides do not form salts with HCl and do not turn litmus blue. That partial double bond also means the amide C–N bond cannot rotate, which is important for the shape of proteins (Chapter 10). Like esters, amides can also be cyclic.
  • 1° and 2° amides hydrogen bond strongly, so they have high melting and boiling points and most are solids (Key Idea 2). Everyday amides include acetaminophen (Tylenol), urea (the nitrogen waste in urine), and melatonin (the hormone that sets the sleep–wake cycle).
  • Hydrolysis: amides are the least reactive of the carboxylic acid derivatives. They are stable in water, but with heat and an acid or a base (or a specific enzyme in the body) water splits the C–N bond, the reverse of amidation; amides need more vigorous conditions than esters do. The carbonyl side always becomes the acid part and the nitrogen side always becomes the amine part, but the form each product takes depends on the conditions.

Professor Tip

It works just like esters: the C=O side came from the carboxylic acid and the N side came from the amine. The amide bond is also the peptide bond that links amino acids in every protein (Chapter 10). Amides are much harder to hydrolyze than esters: in the lab you need strong acid or base and prolonged heating, which is why proteins and nylon are so durable, and why your body relies on enzymes (proteases, amidases) to do the job at 37 °C.

5. Heterocyclic amines and aniline derivatives

A heterocyclic amine is a cyclic organic compound with a five- or six-atom ring that contains one or more nitrogen atoms in the ring (hetero = an atom other than carbon in the ring). Saturated examples are pyrrolidine (five-membered) and piperidine (six-membered). The aromatic ones below are the rings you will keep meeting in nucleic acids, amino acids, and drugs.

The table lists where each ring shows up. The molecules will come back in Chapters 10–12.

Alkaloids are physiologically active nitrogen-containing compounds produced by plants. Nearly all are amines, often with a heterocyclic ring; they are used as stimulants, anesthetics, and antidepressants, and many are habit forming. Well-known alkaloids: cocaine (coca plant), caffeine (coffee beans and tea leaves; a purine), nicotine (tobacco; a pyridine and an N-methylpyrrolidine ring), and morphine and codeine (opium poppy; each has a 3° amine in a ring, and morphine also has a phenol). Morphine is very addictive, and codeine, used for milder pain, is addictive too. Because they are amines, alkaloids are extracted from plant material with dilute acid as their water-soluble ammonium salts and then freed again with base, the chemistry of Key Idea 3.

Aniline derivatives: groups on the nitrogen get N- (C6H5NHCH3 is N-methylaniline); groups on the ring get numbers, with the NH2 carbon as C1, or the o/m/p prefixes for a disubstituted ring (4-chloroaniline = p-chloroaniline).

6. Neurotransmitters

A neurotransmitter is a chemical messenger that transmits a nerve impulse from one neuron to the next. The space between the two neurons is the synapse. The neurotransmitter is stored in vesicles at the end of the presynaptic neuron, released into the synapse when an impulse arrives, and received by receptors on the postsynaptic neuron; it is then removed (by an enzyme or by reuptake into the presynaptic neuron) so the signal stops. Neurotransmitters contain nitrogen as amines and alkylammonium ions; the body makes them from amino acids in the diet, and at body pH their amino groups are usually ionized as ammonium cations (and any carboxylic acid as a carboxylate anion). Most are amines: dopamine, norepinephrine, epinephrine, serotonin, histamine, and acetylcholine (a quaternary ammonium ester). Dopamine controls movement, emotion, and pleasure, and patients with Parkinson disease have too little of it; norepinephrine is converted to epinephrine (adrenaline) under fear or stress. Many drugs work here; for example, SSRI antidepressants block serotonin reuptake. Section 7.11 draws each structure and shows you how to find the amine, phenol, ester, and heterocyclic ring in it.

7. Esters and amides in polymers

  • Polyesters (PET bottles and polyester fabric; dissolvable sutures made of polyglycolic acid) are chains joined by ester links. PET forms from a diacid (terephthalic acid) and a diol (ethylene glycol): each monomer has two reactive ends, so the chain keeps growing.
  • Polyamides (nylon, from a diacid and a diamine; Kevlar) and peptides/proteins are chains joined by amide links. In a peptide each monomer is an amino acid that carries its own acid and its own amine, so the amide (peptide) bond repeats every three chain atoms (N–C–C=O). Learning to spot these repeating links is the key to reading biomolecule structures.

To tell them apart, find each C=O and look at the atom on its other side: O means ester, N means amide. Both links are broken by hydrolysis, which is why polyglycolic acid sutures dissolve in tissue fluid and digestive enzymes can take proteins apart.

Health Career Connection

  • Dental hygiene – local anesthetics and infection: Lidocaine, articaine, and mepivacaine are amide-type anesthetics with a 3° amine group. The cartridge solution is acidic (about pH 3.5–6.5, lowest in cartridges that contain epinephrine) so the drug stays as its water-soluble hydrochloride salt, R3NH+Cl−. After injection, tissue buffers raise the pH toward 7.4 and the equilibrium R3NH+ ⇌ R3N + H+ shifts toward the neutral amine, the only form nonpolar enough to cross the nerve membrane. In an abscess or inflamed tissue the pH is lower, more drug stays as the charged ion, and less reaches the nerve, so anesthesia is slower and weaker. Cartridges with epinephrine (a catecholamine vasoconstrictor) are the most acidic, to protect its phenol groups from oxidation.
  • Dental hygiene – chlorhexidine and nicotine: Chlorhexidine, the antiseptic in prescription mouth rinse, is a bis-biguanide, a molecule rich in amine-like nitrogens that are protonated at mouth pH; the cation binds the negatively charged surfaces of bacteria, plaque biofilm, and enamel and keeps working for hours (substantivity). Nicotine, the alkaloid in tobacco cessation lozenges and patches, is the double heterocyclic amine of Key Idea 5.
  • Dental hygiene – periodontal odor: Bacteria in periodontal pockets break down proteins into amines such as putrescine and cadaverine, which contribute to the characteristic odor of periodontal disease.
  • Dental hygiene – ammonium salts in oral care: Amine fluorides (olaflur) are ammonium salts with a long nonpolar tail, so they act as surfactants that spread across the tooth surface while delivering F−. Cetylpyridinium chloride (CPC) in many mouthwashes is a quaternary ammonium salt. In both, the antibacterial effect comes from the positively charged ammonium ion, not from the fluoride or chloride.

Further reading: M. Epple and J. Enax, "The Chemistry of Dental Care," ChemistryViews (Part 3).

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Most amine drugs are supplied as hydrochloride salts (lidocaine HCl, diphenhydramine HCl, dopamine HCl): the ammonium salt dissolves in water for IV infusion and in gastric fluid for oral absorption, while the free amine often does not. The catecholamines epinephrine, norepinephrine, and dopamine, phenol-containing amines, are IV drugs for shock and cardiac arrest. Antihistamines are amines shaped to block histamine. Opioids such as morphine are alkaloids: a 3° amine plus, in morphine, a phenol. Acetaminophen is the amide of 4-aminophenol. Amide bonds in drugs survive stomach acid and are hydrolyzed by liver enzymes, which is why amide drugs can be taken by mouth. Urea, a diamide, is how the liver packages waste nitrogen; the blood urea nitrogen (BUN) test tracks kidney function, and a failing liver lets ammonia build up (hyperammonemia, Chapter 15). Sulfonamide antibiotics are aromatic amines carrying a sulfur analog of an amide, S(=O)2–NH.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Local anesthetics come in two chemical families, and the name tells you which: amide-type drugs have an "i" before the "-caine" (lidocaine, articaine, mepivacaine, prilocaine, bupivacaine); ester-type drugs do not (benzocaine, procaine). Every one of them contains a 3° amine (benzocaine is the exception, with an aromatic 1° amine) joined through a short chain to an aromatic ring by either an amide or an ester link. The link decides the metabolism: esters are hydrolyzed quickly by esterases in plasma and release p-aminobenzoic acid (PABA), a known allergen, which is why ester anesthetics are now mostly limited to topical use; amides are hydrolyzed slowly by liver enzymes, so liver disease matters in the health history.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Classify and name

Problem. Classify and name CH3CH2NHCH3.

Solution

N is bonded to two carbons (ethyl and methyl), so it is a 2° amine. Common name: ethylmethylamine. IUPAC: N-methylethanamine.

Check. Count carbons on N, not on the carbon next to N: two alkyl groups on nitrogen means 2°, and the longer group (ethyl) is the parent in the IUPAC name.

Example 2: Amine + acid

Problem. Write the reaction of trimethylamine (the fishy smell of fish) with HCl. Why does lemon juice reduce fish odor?

Solution

(CH3)3N + HCl → (CH3)3NH+Cl− (trimethylammonium chloride)

The acid in lemon juice turns the volatile amine into a nonvolatile ionic salt, so it no longer evaporates into your nose.

Check. The nitrogen gained one H and one + charge; the Cl− balances it. No N–H was needed, so a 3° amine works fine here.

Example 3: Amidation

Problem. Draw and name the amide formed from acetic acid and methylamine.

Solution

CH3COOH + H2NCH3 → CH3CONHCH3 + H2O

Name: N-methylethanamide (N-methylacetamide).

Check. The –OH of the acid and one H from the amine left as water; the N now carries one carbon group besides the carbonyl, so the product is a 2° amide.

Example 4: Amide hydrolysis

Problem. Give the products of the base hydrolysis (NaOH) of N-ethylpropanamide, CH3CH2CONHCH2CH3.

Solution

Split the C–N bond. The C=O side becomes a carboxylate salt and the N side becomes an amine:

CH3CH2COO−Na+ (sodium propanoate) + CH3CH2NH2 (ethylamine)

Check. In base the acid is the product that ends up as a salt; the amine stays free. Three carbons went to the acid side and two to the amine side, matching the five carbons of the amide.

Example 5: Name and classify an aniline and an amide

Problem. Name and classify (a) a benzene ring with NH2 on C1 and Cl on C3, and (b) C6H5CON(CH3)CH2CH3.

Solution. (a) The parent is aniline; the chlorine is on ring carbon 3, so the name is 3-chloroaniline (m-chloroaniline). The nitrogen carries one carbon (the ring), so it is a 1° aromatic amine. (b) The C=O is bonded to N, so this is an amide of benzoic acid: parent benzamide. The N carries a methyl and an ethyl group, each named with the locant N and listed alphabetically: N-ethyl-N-methylbenzamide. Two groups on N besides the carbonyl make it a 3° amide.

Check. In (a) a number on the ring, not N, because the Cl is on carbon; in (b) N for both groups because both are on nitrogen. A 3° amide has no N–H, so (b) cannot hydrogen bond to itself and is a liquid, unlike benzamide (mp 129 °C).

Example 6: Acid versus base hydrolysis of the same amide

Problem. N-ethylbenzamide is heated with (a) HCl in water and (b) NaOH in water. Give the products of each.

Solution. Cut the amide at the C(=O)–N bond. The carbonyl side is the benzoic acid part; the nitrogen side is the ethylamine part. Then ask what each product does under the conditions.

(a) Acid: benzoic acid stays as the acid; ethylamine is a base and is neutralized by HCl. Products: C6H5COOH + CH3CH2NH3+Cl− (benzoic acid + ethylammonium chloride).

(b) Base: benzoic acid is neutralized by NaOH; ethylamine stays as the free amine. Products: C6H5COO−Na+ + CH3CH2NH2 (sodium benzoate + ethylamine).

Check. In each case exactly one product is a salt: the amine in acid, the acid in base. If you wrote a free amine next to HCl, or a free carboxylic acid next to NaOH, neutralization has been missed.

Example 7: A drug as its hydrochloride salt

Problem. Diphenhydramine is a 3° amine, (C6H5)2CHOCH2CH2N(CH3)2. Write the equation that forms diphenhydramine hydrochloride, explain why the salt is used in capsules, and show how to get the free amine back.

Solution. Abbreviate the drug as R2NCH3. The lone pair on N accepts H+ from HCl:

R2NCH3 + HCl → R2NH+CH3 Cl− (diphenhydramine hydrochloride)

The free amine, with two phenyl rings, is an oily, poorly water-soluble liquid. The ammonium chloride is an ionic solid that dissolves in water and gastric fluid, so it can be weighed into a capsule and absorbed reliably. A strong base reverses the reaction: R2NH+CH3 Cl− + NaOH → R2NCH3 + H2O + NaCl.

Check. A 3° amine can be protonated (it has a lone pair) but cannot form an amide (no N–H). Only the nitrogen picks up H+; the ether oxygen is far too weak a base.

Example 8: Rank by boiling point

Problem. Rank from lowest to highest boiling point: N-methylethanamide (CH3CONHCH3), 1-butanamine (CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2), N,N-dimethylethanamine ((CH3)2NCH2CH3). All three have molar mass 73 g/mol.

Solution. With equal molar masses, compare hydrogen bonding. The 3° amine has no N–H and only dipole–dipole attractions: lowest. The 1° amine has N–H hydrogen bonds: middle. The 2° amide has an N–H made more polar by the adjacent C=O and a strongly polar carbonyl to accept: highest.

N,N-dimethylethanamine (37 °C) < 1-butanamine (78 °C) < N-methylethanamide (206 °C)

Check. The order amine (3°) < amine (1°) < amide matches the table in Key Idea 2.

Example 9: Naming when the amine is not alone

Problem. Name (a) CH3CH(NH2)CH2CH2CHO, (b) CH3CH(OH)CH2CH(NH2)CH3, and (c) CH3CHClCH2CH(NHCH3)CH2CH3.

Solution. First ask what the parent is. (a) There is an aldehyde, which outranks the amine, so the parent is pentanal (five carbons, CHO = C1) and the NH2 is a substituent: 4-aminopentanal. (b) The alcohol outranks the amine, so the parent is 2-pentanol (number from the end nearer the OH) and NH2 is a substituent on C4: 4-amino-2-pentanol. (c) Only an amine and a halogen are present, so the amine is the parent. The longest chain through the carbon holding the N has six carbons; number from the end that gives N the lower locant (C3, not C4). The chlorine is on C5 and the methyl on nitrogen gets the locant N; list substituents alphabetically: 5-chloro-N-methyl-3-hexanamine.

Check. In (a) and (b) the word amino appears as a prefix and the suffix belongs to the higher-priority group; in (c) -amine is the suffix because nothing outranks it. The locant N never gets a number: a group on nitrogen is "N-methyl," a group on carbon 2 is "2-methyl."

Example 10: Will an amide form?

Problem. Propanoic acid is heated separately with (a) ethylamine, (b) diethylamine, and (c) triethylamine. Give the organic product of each and name it.

Solution. Amidation needs an H on the nitrogen to lose as water. (a) Ethylamine (1°) has two: CH3CH2COOH + H2NCH2CH3 → CH3CH2CONHCH2CH3 + H2O, N-ethylpropanamide (a 2° amide). (b) Diethylamine (2°) has one: the product is CH3CH2CON(CH2CH3)2 + H2O, N,N-diethylpropanamide (a 3° amide). (c) Triethylamine (3°) has no N–H, so no amide can form. The only reaction is the acid–base step: CH3CH2COOH + (CH3CH2)3N → CH3CH2COO− (CH3CH2)3NH+, triethylammonium propanoate, an ammonium salt, with no water released.

Check. Count the groups on N in each product: 2° amide from a 1° amine, 3° amide from a 2° amine, and a salt (no C=O–N bond at all) from a 3° amine. The C=O side of each amide came from the carboxylic acid, the N side from the amine.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Classifying amines by the carbon (as with alcohols) instead of counting carbons on N.
  • Calling amides bases. They are essentially neutral.
  • Making an amide from a 3° amine. There is no N–H to remove.
  • Forgetting that in acid hydrolysis the amine ends up as an ammonium salt, not a neutral amine.
  • Forgetting the mirror-image rule for base hydrolysis: the acid ends up as a carboxylate salt, not a free carboxylic acid.
  • Using a ring number for a group on nitrogen. 4-Methylaniline has CH3 on the ring; N-methylaniline has CH3 on the nitrogen. They are different compounds.
  • Assuming a 3° amine is insoluble because it cannot hydrogen bond to itself. It still accepts hydrogen bonds from water, so small 3° amines dissolve; the lack of N–H lowers the boiling point, not the solubility.
  • Reading "quaternary" as a fourth level of basicity. A quaternary ammonium ion has no lone pair, so it is not a base at all; it is a permanently charged cation.
  • Leaving the H off the nitrogen in a line structure. A bare "N" at the end of a zigzag reads as a 3° amine; write NH2 or NH so the class is clear.
  • Writing water as a product of an amine + acid neutralization. The H+ goes onto the nitrogen; the only products are the ammonium ion and its counter-ion.
  • Using the amine as the parent when an acid, aldehyde, ketone, or alcohol is present. Those outrank the amine, which then becomes the prefix amino-.

Practice

Exercise 1

Classify each: (a) (CH3CH2)3N, (b) (CH3)4N+, (c) cyclohexylamine.

Answer
(a) 3° amine; (b) quaternary ammonium ion; (c) 1° amine

Exercise 2

Write the reaction of methylamine with water.

Answer
CH3NH2 + H2O ⇌ CH3NH3+ + OH−

Exercise 3

Which has the higher boiling point: propanamide (CH3CH2CONH2) or N,N-dimethylmethanamide (HCON(CH3)2)? They are isomers.

Answer
Propanamide (a solid, bp 213 °C). It has N–H bonds and hydrogen bonds with itself. The N,N-dimethyl amide (bp 153 °C) has no N–H.

Exercise 4

Give the products of the acid hydrolysis (HCl, H2O) of N-methylbenzamide.

Answer
Benzoic acid + methylammonium chloride (CH3NH3+Cl−)

Exercise 5

Name C6H5N(CH3)2 and classify it.

Answer
N,N-dimethylaniline, a tertiary (3°) amine

Exercise 6

Name the heterocyclic ring in each and state one place it occurs: (a) a six-membered aromatic ring with nitrogens at positions 1 and 3; (b) a five-membered aromatic ring with one N–H; (c) a pyrimidine fused to an imidazole.

Answer
(a) Pyrimidine: cytosine, thymine, uracil in DNA/RNA. (b) Pyrrole: the heme of hemoglobin. (c) Purine: adenine and guanine; caffeine.

Exercise 7

A dental cartridge contains lidocaine hydrochloride at pH 4.5. Write the equilibrium that occurs after injection into tissue at pH 7.4 and state which form crosses the nerve membrane.

Answer
R3NH+ ⇌ R3N + H+. At the higher tissue pH the equilibrium shifts to the right, producing more of the neutral 3° amine, which is the form that crosses the nerve membrane.
