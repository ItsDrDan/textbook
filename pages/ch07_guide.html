Learning Objectives

  • Classify amines as primary (1°), secondary (2°), tertiary (3°), or quaternary ammonium.
  • Name simple amines and amides.
  • Write the reactions of amines as bases (with water and with acids) and explain why drugs are sold as ammonium salts.
  • Draw the amide formed from a carboxylic acid and an amine, and the products of amide hydrolysis.

Key Ideas

1. Amines: classification and names

Professor Tip

Amines are classified differently from alcohols. Look at the nitrogen and count the carbons bonded directly to N: 1 = 1°, 2 = 2°, 3 = 3°, 4 (with a + charge) = quaternary ammonium ion. (For alcohols, you looked at the carbon holding the OH.)

  • Common names: list the alkyl groups alphabetically + "amine": ethylmethylamine.
  • IUPAC: the longest chain on N becomes the parent with an -amine ending: 1-propanamine. Other groups on N get an N- prefix: N-methylethanamine.
  • C6H5NH2 is aniline.

2. Properties of amines

  • 1° and 2° amines have N–H bonds and hydrogen bond with each other, but N–H hydrogen bonds are weaker than O–H. Their boiling points fall between alkanes and alcohols.
  • 3° amines have no N–H, so they cannot hydrogen bond with each other, but all amines accept hydrogen bonds from water. Small amines are water soluble.
  • Many amines smell fishy or like decay.

3. Amines are bases

The lone pair on N picks up an H+:

  • With water: RNH2 + H2O ⇌ RNH3+ + OH− (a weak base)
  • With an acid: RNH2 + HCl → RNH3+Cl− (an ammonium salt)

Ammonium salts are ionic, odorless solids that dissolve well in water and body fluids. That is why many drug labels say "hydrochloride" (HCl).

4. Amides

  • Amide = C=O bonded to N. They are formed by amidation, a condensation reaction: RCOOH + R′NH2 (heat) → RCONHR′ + H2O. Only 1° and 2° amines (and NH3) work, because the N must have an H to lose.
  • Naming: change -oic acid to -amide. Groups on N get N-: N-methylethanamide.
  • Amides are not basic. The C=O pulls on the N's lone pair.
  • 1° and 2° amides hydrogen bond strongly, so they have high boiling points and many are solids.
  • Hydrolysis: in acid → carboxylic acid + ammonium salt; in base → carboxylate salt + amine.

Professor Tip

It works just like esters: the C=O side came from the carboxylic acid and the N side came from the amine. The amide bond is also the peptide bond that links amino acids in every protein (Chapter 10).

5. Heterocyclic amines and aniline derivatives

  • In a heterocyclic amine the nitrogen is part of a ring: pyrrolidine, pyridine, imidazole, pyrimidine, and purine. They appear in nicotine, caffeine, the amino acid histidine, and the bases of DNA and RNA (Chapter 12).
  • Aniline derivatives: groups on the nitrogen get N- (C6H5NHCH3 is N-methylaniline); groups on the ring get numbers or o/m/p (4-chloroaniline, p-chloroaniline).

6. Neurotransmitters

A neurotransmitter is a chemical messenger. It is stored in vesicles at the end of a nerve cell, released into the synapse, binds receptors on the next cell, and is then removed (by enzymes or by reuptake into the first cell). Most are amines: dopamine, norepinephrine, epinephrine, serotonin, histamine, and acetylcholine (a quaternary ammonium ester). Many drugs work here; for example, SSRI antidepressants block serotonin reuptake.

7. Esters and amides in polymers

  • Amidation is a nucleophilic acyl substitution: the amine's N replaces the –OH of the carboxylic acid.
  • Polyesters (PET bottles; dissolvable sutures made of polyglycolic acid) are chains joined by ester links.
  • Polyamides (nylon) and peptides/proteins are chains joined by amide links. Learning to spot these repeating links is the key to reading biomolecule structures.

Health Career Connection

  • Dental hygiene – local anesthetics and infection: Lidocaine, articaine, and mepivacaine are amide-type anesthetics with an amine group. Cartridges hold the water-soluble ammonium salt. Only the neutral amine form crosses nerve membranes. Infected, inflamed tissue is more acidic, which pushes more drug into the charged RNH3+ form. That is a big reason anesthesia is harder to achieve near an abscess.
  • Dental hygiene – periodontal odor: Bacteria in periodontal pockets break down proteins into amines such as putrescine and cadaverine, which contribute to the characteristic odor of periodontal disease.
  • Nursing – "HCl" on drug labels: Diphenhydramine HCl (Benadryl) and many other drugs are ammonium salts so they dissolve and absorb reliably.
  • Nursing – neurotransmitters and drugs: Dopamine, epinephrine, norepinephrine, and serotonin are amines. Acetaminophen (Tylenol) and penicillin contain amide groups.
  • Dental hygiene – ammonium salts in oral care: Amine fluorides (olaflur) are ammonium salts with a long nonpolar tail, so they act as surfactants that spread across the tooth surface while delivering F−. Cetylpyridinium chloride (CPC) in many mouthwashes is a quaternary ammonium salt. In both, the antibacterial effect comes from the positively charged ammonium ion, not from the fluoride or chloride.

Further reading: M. Epple and J. Enax, "The Chemistry of Dental Care," ChemistryViews (Part 3).

Worked Examples

Example 1: Classify and name

Classify and name CH3CH2NHCH3.

Solution

N is bonded to two carbons (ethyl and methyl), so it is a 2° amine. Common name: ethylmethylamine. IUPAC: N-methylethanamine.

Example 2: Amine + acid

Write the reaction of trimethylamine (the fishy smell of fish) with HCl. Why does lemon juice reduce fish odor?

Solution

(CH3)3N + HCl → (CH3)3NH+Cl− (trimethylammonium chloride)

The acid in lemon juice turns the volatile amine into a nonvolatile ionic salt, so it no longer evaporates into your nose.

Example 3: Amidation

Draw and name the amide formed from acetic acid and methylamine.

Solution

CH3COOH + H2NCH3 → CH3CONHCH3 + H2O

Name: N-methylethanamide (N-methylacetamide).

Example 4: Amide hydrolysis

Give the products of the base hydrolysis (NaOH) of N-ethylpropanamide, CH3CH2CONHCH2CH3.

Solution

Split the C–N bond. The C=O side becomes a carboxylate salt and the N side becomes an amine:

CH3CH2COO−Na+ (sodium propanoate) + CH3CH2NH2 (ethylamine)

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Classifying amines by the carbon (as with alcohols) instead of counting carbons on N.
  • Calling amides bases. They are essentially neutral.
  • Making an amide from a 3° amine. There is no N–H to remove.
  • Forgetting that in acid hydrolysis the amine ends up as an ammonium salt, not a neutral amine.

Practice

Exercise 1

Classify each: (a) (CH3CH2)3N, (b) (CH3)4N+, (c) cyclohexylamine.

Answer
(a) 3° amine; (b) quaternary ammonium ion; (c) 1° amine

Exercise 2

Write the reaction of methylamine with water.

Answer
CH3NH2 + H2O ⇌ CH3NH3+ + OH−

Exercise 3

Which has the higher boiling point: propanamide (CH3CH2CONH2) or N,N-dimethylmethanamide (HCON(CH3)2)? They are isomers.

Answer
Propanamide (a solid, bp 213 °C). It has N–H bonds and hydrogen bonds with itself. The N,N-dimethyl amide (bp 153 °C) has no N–H.

Exercise 4

Give the products of the acid hydrolysis (HCl, H2O) of N-methylbenzamide.

Answer
Benzoic acid + methylammonium chloride (CH3NH3+Cl−)

Exercise 5

Name C6H5N(CH3)2 and classify it.

Answer
N,N-dimethylaniline, a tertiary (3°) amine
