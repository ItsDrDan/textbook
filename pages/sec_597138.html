Monosaccharides such as glucose and fructose are crystalline solids at room temperature, but they are quite soluble in water, each molecule having several OH groups that readily engage in hydrogen bonding. The chemical behavior of these monosaccharides is likewise determined by their functional groups.

An important reaction of monosaccharides is the oxidation of the aldehyde group, one of the most easily oxidized organic functional groups. Aldehyde oxidation can be accomplished with any mild oxidizing agent, such as Tollens’ reagent or Benedict’s reagent. With the latter, complexed copper(II) ions are reduced to copper(I) ions that form a brick-red precipitate [copper(I) oxide; Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\)].

Any carbohydrate capable of reducing either Tollens’ or Benedict’s reagents without first undergoing hydrolysis is said to be a reducing sugar. Because both the Tollens’ and Benedict’s reagents are basic solutions, ketoses (such as fructose) also give positive tests due to an equilibrium that exists between ketoses and aldoses (in basic solution a ketose rearranges into an aldose, which then reacts).

These reactions have been used as simple and rapid diagnostic tests for the presence of glucose in blood or urine. For example, Clinitest tablets, an older test for sugar in the urine, contained copper(II) ions and were based on Benedict’s test. A green color indicated very little sugar, whereas a brick-red color indicated sugar in excess of 2 g/100 mL of urine. Today, blood and urine glucose tests use enzymes instead (Section 8.3).

Summary

Monosaccharides are crystalline solids at room temperature and quite soluble in water. Monosaccharides are reducing sugars; they reduce mild oxidizing agents, such as Tollens’ or Benedict’s reagents.

Lecture Connection

In lecture the chemistry of a monosaccharide is just the chemistry of its functional groups: hydroxyl groups explain every physical property, and the aldehyde and the terminal alcohol explain every reaction. Oxidation at C1, at C6, or at both gives three different sugar acids; reduction at C1 gives a sugar alcohol; and "reducing sugar" is a lab result you should be able to predict from one question: is there a free hemiacetal?

Physical properties: it is all hydrogen bonding

  • Very water soluble. Five or six OH groups on a six-carbon molecule hydrogen bond strongly with water. Glucose dissolves at roughly 900 g per liter of water at room temperature; sucrose dissolves even more. Sugars are insoluble in nonpolar solvents such as hexane.
  • Crystalline solids with high melting points for molecules of their size (glucose about 146 °C, sucrose about 186 °C) because every OH group hydrogen bonds to its neighbors in the crystal. Compare hexane, a liquid that boils at 69 °C.
  • They decompose instead of boiling. Heat a sugar past its melting point and it darkens and caramelizes (the OH groups and C=O react with each other) rather than distilling. Sugars have no boiling point.
  • Sweet taste, but the relative sweetness varies greatly. Relative to sucrose (defined as 100), fructose is about 170, glucose about 75, maltose about 30, lactose about 15 (rounded from the table in Section 8.3: 173, 74, 32, 16). Sugar alcohols are sweet too (xylitol about 100, sorbitol about 60), which is why they replace sugar in "sugar-free" products. Sucralose (Splenda) is sucrose with three OH groups replaced by Cl; it tastes about 600 times sweeter and is not digested.

When an exam asks "would you expect monosaccharides to have a high melting point / be water soluble, and why?", the answer is the IMF answer: they are polar (dipole–dipole forces) and have many hydroxyl groups that both donate and accept hydrogen bonds, with each other in the solid and with water in solution.

Reducing sugars: oxidation at C1

  • Reducing sugars have a free hemiacetal (an anomeric OH) that can open to an aldehyde. They give a positive Benedict's test. All monosaccharides are reducing sugars, and so are most disaccharides except sucrose.
  • Oxidation of the aldehyde end by a weak oxidizing agent (Tollens' or Benedict's) gives a sugar acid (glucose → gluconic acid). Stronger oxidants and enzymes can also oxidize the CH2OH end (below).
  • Reduction of the C=O (NaBH4, or H2 with a metal catalyst) gives a sugar alcohol (glucose → sorbitol, xylose → xylitol).

Here is why the word "reducing" is used. A sugar whose ring can open exposes an aldehyde, and aldehydes are easily oxidized to carboxylic acids (Chapter 5). When the sugar is oxidized, something else must be reduced: in Benedict's reagent, blue Cu2+ is reduced to brick-red Cu2O; in Tollens' reagent, Ag+ is reduced to a silver mirror. The sugar is the reducing agent, so we call it a reducing sugar. Ketoses count too: in the basic test solution, fructose rearranges into an aldose, and the aldose then reacts. A sugar whose anomeric carbon is locked in an acetal (a glycosidic bond, Section 8.6) cannot open, so it is non-reducing.

Example 1: Reducing sugar?

Problem. Which give a positive Benedict's test: glucose, fructose, maltose, sucrose?

Solution

Glucose, fructose, and maltose: positive. (Fructose is a ketose, but under the basic test conditions it rearranges to an aldose, so it still reacts.) Sucrose: negative. Its α1–β2 link ties up both anomeric carbons, so no ring can open.

Check. One question decides it: is there a free hemiacetal (anomeric OH)? Yes for the two monosaccharides (glucose and fructose) and the right-hand unit of maltose; no for sucrose.

The three kinds of sugar acids

An aldose has two carbons that can be oxidized to a carboxylic acid: the aldehyde at C1 (easy, as for any aldehyde) and the primary alcohol at C6 (harder; needs a strong oxidant or, in the body, an enzyme). The secondary alcohols in between are never oxidized to acids. Which end gets oxidized decides the name, and the oxidizing agent decides which end: weak oxidizing agents (Tollens', Benedict's) take C1 only; strong oxidizing agents take both ends at once; enzymes can take the C6 alcohol while leaving the aldehyde alone.

Professor Tip

Take the names apart. Ald-on-ic: the aldehyde was oxidized to the acid and the alcohols were left alone. Ald-ar-ic: the aldehyde and the end alcohol are both acids. Ald-uron-ic: the aldehyde is still there; uron marks the alcohol end that became the acid. Ald-it-ol: the aldehyde turned into an alcohol (-ol). Swap in the sugar's stem (gluc-, galact-, mann-) and you can name every product.

Professor Tip

Know all four products of a sugar’s redox chemistry and their name endings: -onic (C1 oxidized), -uronic (C6 oxidized), -aric (both ends), -itol (reduced). Example 5 in the Chapter Guide runs galactose through all four.

Reduction to sugar alcohols

Reducing the C=O of a sugar (hydrogen gas with a metal catalyst, or an enzyme in the body) turns the aldehyde or ketone into one more CH2OH or CHOH group. The product is a sugar alcohol (alditol), named by changing -ose to -itol ("ald-it-ol": the aldehyde turned into an alcohol): glucose → sorbitol (also called glucitol), mannose → mannitol, xylose → xylitol, galactose → galactitol. Sugar alcohols taste sweet (sorbitol is about 60% as sweet as sucrose and is found in berries, cherries, plums, and pears), keep the OH groups and the water solubility of sugars, and are used as sugar substitutes in candy and chewing gum and as moisturizing agents in foods and cosmetics. But they have no carbonyl to open and close: no ring, no mutarotation, no positive Benedict's test. The figure puts all of the oxidation and reduction products in one place.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Above the kidney's threshold (about 180 mg/dL) glucose spills into the urine (glucosuria). Dipsticks use glucose oxidase and detect only glucose; the older copper-reduction tablet test (the older Clinitest) reacts with any reducing sugar, which is why it was used to screen infants' urine for galactose (galactosemia). The liver attaches glucuronic acid (the alduronic acid of glucose) to bilirubin and to drugs such as acetaminophen to make them water soluble (glucuronidation); newborns do this slowly, one cause of neonatal jaundice. Two sugar alcohols are drugs: IV mannitol pulls water from tissue by osmosis (an osmotic diuretic); oral sorbitol holds water in the gut (an osmotic laxative).

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Xylitol and sorbitol, the sugar alcohols in sugar-free gum and mints, taste sweet but have no carbonyl, so cariogenic bacteria ferment them to acid poorly (sorbitol slowly, xylitol essentially not at all) (the caries chemistry itself is in Section 8.6); xylitol also enters Streptococcus mutans and inhibits its growth, and chewing raises salivary flow, which buffers plaque acid. Reduction of one carbon is the whole difference between a sugar that feeds plaque and one that does not.

In the Lab

  • Experiment 8a – Types of Carbohydrates and 8b – Tests for Carbohydrates
  • Benedict’s test: reducing sugars turn blue Cu2+ into a green, gold, or brick-red Cu2O precipitate (more sugar = redder). Ketoses also react because fructose rearranges to an aldose in base. Sucrose is negative.
  • Seliwanoff’s test: ketoses (fructose) give a deep red color quickly; aldoses give only a light pink slowly.
  • Fermentation: yeast ferments most mono- and disaccharides (CO2 bubbles), but not lactose (galactose ferments slowly, if at all).
  • You will hydrolyze di- and polysaccharides, test foods, and identify an unknown sugar; the iodine test for starch is in Section 8.7.
