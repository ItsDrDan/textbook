Learning Objectives

  • Define neurotransmitter and describe how one carries a signal across a synapse and is then removed.
  • Identify the functional groups (amine, phenol, ester, carboxylic acid, heterocyclic ring) in acetylcholine, the catecholamines, serotonin, histamine, GABA, glutamate, and glycine.
  • Classify the nitrogen in each neurotransmitter as 1°, 2°, 3°, or quaternary.
  • Explain, using polarity and charge, why L-DOPA enters the brain while dopamine cannot.

Epinephrine in an EpiPen or an anesthetic cartridge, antihistamines, antidepressants: all of them act on the chemistry of this section. Neurotransmitters are small molecules, nearly all amines, that carry messages between nerve cells. Spot the amine, phenol, ester, or heterocyclic ring in each one and you can read how it behaves in the body.

What a neurotransmitter is

A neurotransmitter is a chemical messenger that one nerve cell (a neuron) releases to signal the next cell. Neurons do not touch: between them is a tiny gap, the synapse (synaptic cleft), that an electrical impulse cannot jump. The neuron converts the signal to a chemical one in three steps:

  1. Release. The neurotransmitter is stored in membrane bubbles called vesicles at the tip of the sending (presynaptic) neuron. When an impulse arrives, vesicles fuse with the membrane and empty into the cleft.
  2. Binding. The molecules diffuse across and fit into receptors, proteins on the receiving (postsynaptic) cell, which then responds: it fires, contracts (a muscle cell), or secretes.
  3. Removal. A signal must stop, so the neurotransmitter is cleared quickly: a transporter pumps it back into the presynaptic neuron (reuptake), or an enzyme in the cleft breaks it apart.

Structural families

Most neurotransmitters contain nitrogen as amines or alkylammonium ions, and the body builds them from amino acids obtained in the diet. At body pH the amino group is usually ionized to an ammonium cation (and any carboxylic acid to a carboxylate anion), which keeps the molecule water-soluble. Look for the nitrogen first and count its carbons (1°, 2°, 3°, or quaternary), then look for the other functional groups around it.

Acetylcholine: a quaternary ammonium ester

Acetylcholine is the messenger between motor nerves and skeletal muscle and in the parasympathetic ("rest and digest") nervous system. Its nitrogen has four carbons and a permanent positive charge: a quaternary ammonium ion, so acetylcholine is always charged. The other end is an ester, the acetate ester of the alcohol choline.

That ester is the off switch. The enzyme acetylcholinesterase in the cleft hydrolyzes it, the ester hydrolysis of Chapter 6, to acetate and choline. The pieces no longer fit the receptor, and the muscle relaxes within milliseconds.

Catecholamines: dopamine, norepinephrine, and epinephrine

A catechol is a benzene ring with two OH groups on neighboring carbons; each OH on an aromatic ring is a phenol. Add a two-carbon chain ending in an amine and you have a catecholamine. The three differ by one change each: norepinephrine adds a secondary alcohol to dopamine's side chain, and epinephrine adds a methyl to norepinephrine's nitrogen, turning a primary amine into a secondary amine.

The body builds them in that order from the amino acid tyrosine: tyrosine → L-DOPA → dopamine → norepinephrine → epinephrine. Each arrow is one enzyme step: add a ring OH, remove CO2, add a side-chain OH, add an N-methyl.

Dopamine governs movement, motivation, and reward. Norepinephrine and epinephrine are the "fight or flight" messengers: they raise heart rate and blood pressure and constrict skin blood vessels. Epinephrine is also an adrenal hormone. Catecholamines are removed by reuptake and by the enzyme monoamine oxidase (MAO).

Serotonin and histamine: heterocyclic amines

Serotonin contains an indole, the fused benzene-plus-pyrrole ring from Section 7.8, with a phenol OH and a two-carbon chain ending in a primary amine. It is made from tryptophan by adding the OH and removing CO2. Serotonin affects mood, sleep, appetite, and gut motility; most of the body's serotonin is in the intestine. The indole N–H is not basic; the side-chain amine is.

Histamine contains an imidazole ring (five atoms, two nitrogens) and a side-chain primary amine; it comes from histidine by loss of CO2. Mast cells release it in allergic reactions, where it dilates blood vessels and makes them leaky (swelling, redness, itching), and it also triggers stomach acid secretion.

Amino acids as neurotransmitters: GABA, glutamate, glycine

Some neurotransmitters are simply amino acids, molecules with both a carboxylic acid and an amine. Glutamate is the brain's main excitatory ("go") messenger, the same glutamate found in proteins and in MSG. Glycine, the smallest amino acid, is inhibitory ("stop") in the spinal cord. GABA (gamma-aminobutyric acid) is the brain's main inhibitory messenger. Its IUPAC name, 4-aminobutanoic acid, gives its structure: a four-carbon carboxylic acid with NH2 on carbon 4. It is made from glutamate by removing CO2. Many sedatives and anti-seizure drugs strengthen GABA's signal.

Finally, the endorphins are short chains of amino acids joined by amide (peptide) bonds, the body's own pain relievers; opioids such as morphine act at the same receptors. You will read peptide structures in Chapter 10.

Professor Tip

Run the same scan on every new neurotransmitter: (1) find each nitrogen and count its carbons (1°, 2°, 3°, or quaternary); (2) find each OH and decide whether it is on an aromatic ring (phenol) or a chain (alcohol); (3) find any C=O and ask what is attached (acid, ester, or amide).

Worked examples

Example 1: Functional-group scan of epinephrine

Problem. List every functional group in epinephrine and classify its amine.

Solution. Start with the nitrogen: it is bonded to a CH3 and to the CH2 of the side chain, two carbons, so it is a secondary (2°) amine. The ring carries two OH groups on adjacent carbons; each is a phenol, and together they make a catechol. The side chain carries an OH on a carbon that is bonded to two other carbons, a secondary alcohol. There is no C=O, so no acid, ester, or amide.

Check. Norepinephrine differs only by lacking the N-methyl group, which is why it is a primary amine.

Example 2: Acetylcholine and its hydrolysis

Problem. Identify the two functional groups in acetylcholine, classify the nitrogen, and write the products when acetylcholinesterase hydrolyzes it.

Solution. The nitrogen carries three CH3 groups and one CH2, four carbons and a positive charge: a quaternary ammonium ion. The CH3C(=O)O– portion is an ester of acetic acid. Hydrolysis splits the ester at the C–O bond to the alcohol oxygen, giving the acid and the alcohol:

CH3COOCH2CH2N+(CH3)3 + H2O → CH3COOH + HOCH2CH2N+(CH3)3 (choline)

At body pH the acetic acid is present as acetate, CH3COO−.

Check. Ester + water → carboxylic acid + alcohol, the Chapter 6 rule. The quaternary nitrogen is untouched.

Example 3: Why L-DOPA gets into the brain and dopamine does not

Problem. Patients with Parkinson disease lack dopamine in part of the brain, but dopamine given by IV does not help. L-DOPA does. Explain using structure.

Solution. The blood–brain barrier is a layer of tightly joined cells that admits only small, fairly nonpolar molecules unless a transporter carries them. At blood pH dopamine's amine is protonated to –NH3+, a charged, very polar ion with no transporter, so it stays in the blood. L-DOPA has the same amine but is also an amino acid, and the barrier has a transporter for amino acids such as tyrosine. L-DOPA rides it in, and brain enzymes remove CO2 to make dopamine where it is needed.

Check. The two molecules differ by one –COOH group. It does not make L-DOPA less polar; it makes it look like an amino acid, which the transporter recognizes.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Epinephrine is the drug in an EpiPen for anaphylaxis (a severe, body-wide allergic reaction); it reverses the vessel-leaking, airway-narrowing effects of histamine and is supplied as a water-soluble salt of its secondary amine. Antihistamines such as diphenhydramine are amines shaped to occupy histamine receptors; they do not remove histamine. SSRIs (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors) block the serotonin transporter, so serotonin stays in the cleft longer; the full effect builds over weeks as receptors adjust. Organophosphate insecticides and nerve agents inhibit acetylcholinesterase; acetylcholine piles up, causing drooling, tearing, and muscle twitching (cholinergic crisis). Note that local anesthetics such as lidocaine do not act on neurotransmitters at all: they block the sodium channels that carry the impulse along the nerve, so the signal never reaches the synapse.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Most anesthetic cartridges contain two amines: the anesthetic (an amide-type drug with a tertiary amine) and epinephrine, typically 1:100,000. The epinephrine does not numb; it constricts the small blood vessels in the gingiva, so the anesthetic stays near the nerve longer and bleeding in the field is reduced. Because epinephrine is a catecholamine whose phenol groups oxidize in air, the solution also contains a sulfite preservative, and that preservative, not the anesthetic, is what a sulfite-sensitive patient reacts to. Because epinephrine raises heart rate and blood pressure, the health history asks about cardiovascular disease before a cartridge that contains it is chosen.

Summary

  • A neurotransmitter is released from vesicles, crosses the synapse, binds a receptor, and is removed by reuptake or by an enzyme.
  • Acetylcholine is a quaternary ammonium ester; acetylcholinesterase ends its signal by ester hydrolysis.
  • Dopamine, norepinephrine, and epinephrine are catecholamines (two phenol OH groups plus a side-chain amine), made from tyrosine through L-DOPA.
  • Serotonin (indole) and histamine (imidazole) are heterocyclic amines made from tryptophan and histidine.
  • Glutamate, glycine, and GABA are amino acid neurotransmitters; endorphins are peptides.
  • Charged ammonium ions cannot cross the blood–brain barrier; L-DOPA enters on an amino acid transporter and becomes dopamine in the brain.

Exercises

Exercise 1

Put the three removal and signaling events in order and name the structure involved in each: receptor binding, reuptake, vesicle release.

Answer
Vesicle release (vesicles in the presynaptic terminal fuse with the membrane) → receptor binding (receptor proteins on the postsynaptic cell) → reuptake (a transporter protein in the presynaptic membrane). Enzyme breakdown is the alternative to reuptake.

Exercise 2

Classify the amine in each as 1°, 2°, 3°, or quaternary: (a) dopamine, (b) epinephrine, (c) acetylcholine, (d) GABA.

Answer
(a) 1°; (b) 2° (the N carries a methyl and the side chain); (c) quaternary ammonium ion (four carbons on N, positive charge); (d) 1°.

Exercise 3

Which neurotransmitter contains an ester? Name the enzyme that hydrolyzes it and the two products.

Answer
Acetylcholine. Acetylcholinesterase hydrolyzes the ester to acetic acid (acetate at body pH) and choline.

Exercise 4

What do dopamine, norepinephrine, and epinephrine have in common structurally, and what single change converts norepinephrine into epinephrine?

Answer
All three have a catechol ring (benzene with two adjacent phenol OH groups) and a two-carbon side chain ending in an amine. Adding a methyl group to the nitrogen of norepinephrine (making the 1° amine a 2° amine) gives epinephrine.

Exercise 5

Name the heterocyclic ring in (a) serotonin and (b) histamine, and name the amino acid each is made from.

Answer
(a) Indole, from tryptophan; (b) imidazole, from histidine.

Exercise 6

Write the IUPAC name of GABA and draw its condensed structure. What reaction converts glutamate into GABA?

Answer
4-Aminobutanoic acid, H2NCH2CH2CH2COOH. Removal of CO2 (decarboxylation) from the carboxylic acid on glutamate's alpha carbon leaves GABA.

Exercise 7

A student says, "Dopamine is too polar to enter the brain, so L-DOPA must be less polar." Correct the statement.

Answer
L-DOPA is not less polar; it has the same protonated amine plus a carboxylic acid group. It enters because the blood–brain barrier has a transporter for amino acids, and L-DOPA is an amino acid. Dopamine has no carboxylic acid, so the transporter does not recognize it, and its charged ammonium group keeps it from diffusing across on its own.

Exercise 8

An SSRI and an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor both increase the amount of neurotransmitter in the cleft. Explain how each does it and which removal route each one blocks.

Answer
An SSRI blocks the reuptake transporter, so serotonin is not pumped back into the presynaptic neuron. An acetylcholinesterase inhibitor blocks the enzyme that hydrolyzes acetylcholine's ester, so acetylcholine is not broken down. One blocks reuptake; the other blocks enzymatic breakdown.
