Learning Objectives

  • To describe how to prepare alcohols from alkenes

Methanol is prepared by combining hydrogen gas and carbon monoxide at high temperatures and pressures in the presence of a catalyst composed of zinc oxide (ZnO) and chromium oxide (Cr2O3):

\[\mathrm{2H_2 + CO \underset{ZnO,\: Cr_2O_3}{\xrightarrow{200\: atm,\: 350^\circ C}} CH_3OH} \nonumber \]

Methanol is an important solvent and is used as an automotive fuel, either as the pure liquid—as in some racing cars—or as an additive in gasoline.

Nearly 2 billion gallons of methanol are produced each year in the United States by the catalytic reduction of carbon monoxide with hydrogen gas.

Many simple alcohols are made by the hydration of alkenes. Ethanol is made by the hydration of ethylene in the presence of a catalyst such as sulfuric acid (H2SO4).

In a similar manner, isopropyl alcohol is produced by the addition of water to propene (propylene).

A generalization called Markovnikov’s rule (Section 3.4) predicts the results when the addition of water to an alkene has two possible products.

To Your Health: The Physiological Effects of Alcohols

Methanol is quite poisonous to humans. Ingestion of as little as 15 mL of methanol can cause blindness, and 30 mL (1 oz) can cause death. However, the usual fatal dose is 100 to 150 mL. The main reason for methanol’s toxicity is that we have liver enzymes that catalyze its oxidation to formaldehyde, the simplest member of the aldehyde family:

Formaldehyde reacts rapidly with the components of cells, coagulating proteins in much the same way that cooking coagulates an egg. This property of formaldehyde accounts for much of the toxicity of methanol.

Organic and biochemical equations are frequently written showing only the organic reactants and products. In this way, we focus attention on the organic starting material and product, rather than on balancing complicated equations.

Ethanol is oxidized in the liver to acetaldehyde:

The acetaldehyde is in turn oxidized to acetic acid (HC2H3O2), a normal constituent of cells, which is then oxidized to carbon dioxide and water. Even so, ethanol is potentially toxic to humans. The rapid ingestion of 1 pt (about 500 mL) of pure ethanol would kill most people, and acute ethanol poisoning kills several hundred people each year—often those engaged in some sort of drinking contest. Ethanol freely crosses into the brain, where it depresses the respiratory control center, resulting in failure of the respiratory muscles and hence suffocation. Ethanol is believed to act on nerve cell membranes, causing a diminution in speech, thought, cognition, and judgment.

Rubbing alcohol is usually a 70% aqueous solution of isopropyl alcohol. It has a high vapor pressure, and its rapid evaporation from the skin produces a cooling effect. It is toxic when ingested but, compared to methanol, is less readily absorbed through the skin.

Lecture Connection

The lecture treats hydration (this section) and dehydration (Section 4.5) as one equilibrium read in two directions. You already ran the hydration of an alkene in lab; the skill here is to recognize the conditions that push the reaction toward the alcohol, and to use Markovnikov's rule when two alcohols are possible.

Hydration and dehydration are reversible

For propene and 2-propanol the equilibrium is:

CH3CH=CH2 + H2O \(\rightleftharpoons\) CH3CH(OH)CH3   (H+ catalyst; the same acid catalyzes both directions)

By Le Chatelier's principle, a system at equilibrium shifts to relieve whatever stress you apply:

  • Toward the alcohol (hydration): run the reaction in excess water with dilute acid at moderate temperature. Adding a reactant (water) pushes the equilibrium to the right.
  • Toward the alkene (dehydration): use concentrated acid (which contains very little water), heat strongly, and distill off the low-boiling alkene or absorb the water as it forms. Removing a product pulls the equilibrium to the left, and because dehydration absorbs heat (it is endothermic), heating favors it as well.

So "H+, heat, concentrated H2SO4" on an exam signals dehydration, while "H+, H2O" signals hydration. Hydration is an addition reaction; dehydration is an elimination.

Markovnikov's rule decides which alcohol forms

When the alkene is unsymmetrical, water can add two ways. Markovnikov's rule from Chapter 3 still applies: the H goes to the carbon of the C=C that already has more hydrogens, and the OH goes to the carbon with fewer. Propene therefore hydrates to 2-propanol (isopropyl alcohol), not 1-propanol, which is exactly the industrial route the section describes. Keep this rule separate from Zaitsev's rule for dehydration (Section 4.5), which is the opposite idea: there you remove the H from the neighboring carbon with fewer hydrogens.

Example: Predict the alcohol

Problem. What alcohol forms when 1-butene, CH2=CHCH2CH3, is hydrated with H2O and an H+ catalyst?

Solution. C1 of the double bond has two H atoms; C2 has one. Markovnikov: H adds to C1 (the carbon with more H), OH adds to C2. Product: CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3, 2-butanol, a 2° alcohol.

Check. The product has the formula of the alkene plus H2O (C4H10O). The other possibility, 1-butanol, would need the OH on the carbon that had more hydrogens, which Markovnikov's rule rules out as the major product.

Professor Tip

The industrial synthesis of methanol and the fermentation of sugars to ethanol are background. Exam questions on this section ask you to predict the alcohol formed by hydrating a given alkene (with Markovnikov’s rule) and to recognize hydration and dehydration as a reversible pair.

Alcohols in the clinic: strength labels

Clinical Connection: Nursing

The section's note on rubbing alcohol is worth a closer look. Isopropyl alcohol swabs are usually 70% because that water content helps the alcohol enter bacterial cells and denature (unfold) their proteins; pure isopropyl alcohol evaporates before it can do the job. For beverages, "proof" is twice the percent alcohol by volume: 80-proof spirits are 40% ethanol. Both numbers are concentrations, not doses. The toxicity the section describes for methanol and ethanol comes from the oxidation chemistry of Section 4.5, where the body's enzyme alcohol dehydrogenase treats every small 1° alcohol the same way.
