Problem 9.11

Answer

Problem 9.12

Why does an unsaturated oil turn rancid faster than a saturated fat?

Answer
Its C=C double bonds react with O2, breaking into short-chain aldehydes and acids with unpleasant odors. Saturated chains lack these reactive sites.

Problem 9.13

Explain why phospholipids form a bilayer in water.

Answer
They are amphipathic: polar heads face water on both surfaces while the nonpolar tails cluster inside, away from water.

Problem 9.48

A margarine label says "partially hydrogenated soybean oil." Which statement best describes what partial hydrogenation does to the fatty acid chains?

  1. It adds H2 to every C=C, giving a fully saturated fat.
  2. It hydrolyzes the ester bonds, releasing free fatty acids.
  3. It adds H2 to some C=C and flips some of the remaining cis bonds to trans, so the product is semi-solid.
  4. It oxidizes the C=C to aldehydes, making the fat rancid.
Answer
C. "Partial" means only some double bonds are hydrogenated. The nickel catalyst also isomerizes a fraction of the surviving cis bonds to trans. Both changes straighten chains, so the oil becomes semi-solid; the trans chains are the health concern. A describes complete hydrogenation; B is hydrolysis; D is rancidity, which hydrogenation actually slows.

Problem 9.49

Complete hydrolysis of an unknown lipid gives one molecule of a 16-carbon carboxylic acid and one molecule of a 30-carbon alcohol, and nothing else. The lipid is a

  1. triacylglycerol
  2. wax
  3. glycerophospholipid
  4. sphingolipid
Answer
B. A wax is a single ester of a long-chain fatty acid and a long-chain alcohol, so hydrolysis gives exactly one of each. A triacylglycerol would give glycerol plus three fatty acids; a glycerophospholipid would also release phosphate and an amino alcohol; a sphingolipid would release sphingosine (an amino alcohol) and a polar head group.

Problem 9.50

Which process requires a membrane protein but does not consume ATP?

  1. O2 entering a red blood cell
  2. glucose entering a red blood cell through a GLUT carrier, from high to low concentration
  3. Na+ being pumped out of a neuron from low to high concentration
  4. a steroid hormone entering its target cell
Answer
B. Glucose is polar, so it needs a carrier, but it is moving down its gradient, so no energy is spent: facilitated diffusion. A and D are simple diffusion of small or nonpolar molecules (no protein). C goes against the gradient and needs the ATP-driven Na+/K+-ATPase: active transport.

Problem 9.51

A patient with asthma is told that ibuprofen will relieve a sore knee but will not help the wheezing. Which explanation is chemically correct?

  1. Ibuprofen blocks lipoxygenase, so it lowers leukotrienes but not prostaglandins.
  2. Ibuprofen blocks the COX enzymes, lowering prostaglandins and thromboxanes, but leukotrienes (made by lipoxygenase) are unaffected.
  3. Ibuprofen is a steroid and cannot reach the lungs.
  4. Ibuprofen blocks the release of arachidonic acid, so all eicosanoids fall.
Answer
B. NSAIDs inhibit cyclooxygenase (COX), the enzyme that turns arachidonic acid into prostaglandins (pain, inflammation) and thromboxanes. Leukotrienes, the bronchoconstrictors in asthma, come from a different enzyme, lipoxygenase, which NSAIDs do not block. Choice D describes how corticosteroids act, one step earlier; ibuprofen is not a steroid (C).

Problem 9.52

Compare and contrast. Phosphatidylcholine (lecithin) and sphingomyelin both carry a phosphate-choline head and both are found in nerve cell membranes. List two structural differences between them, and explain why, despite those differences, both form the same kind of bilayer.

Answer
Differences: (1) the backbone is glycerol in phosphatidylcholine but sphingosine (an 18-carbon amino alcohol) in sphingomyelin; (2) phosphatidylcholine holds two fatty acids by ester bonds, while sphingomyelin holds one fatty acid by an amide bond (its second nonpolar tail is the hydrocarbon chain of sphingosine itself). Both still have the same overall shape: a charged, polar phosphocholine head and two long nonpolar tails. That amphipathic shape, not the backbone, is what drives bilayer formation: heads face the water on each side and tails hide inside. Both are hydrolyzable (ester or amide links).

Problem 9.53

Predict. Triacylglycerol A contains palmitic (16:0), stearic (18:0), and oleic (18:1) chains. Triacylglycerol B contains oleic (18:1), linoleic (18:2), and α-linolenic (18:3) chains. Predict (a) which is more likely a liquid at room temperature, (b) how many moles of H2 each needs for complete hydrogenation and what product each gives, and (c) which turns rancid faster when left open to air.

Answer
(a) B. It has six cis double bonds across its three chains (1 + 2 + 3), so its chains cannot pack; A has only one. B is an oil, A a soft fat. (b) One H2 per C=C: A needs 1 mol H2 and becomes a mixed saturated fat (palmitic, stearic, stearic); B needs 6 mol H2 and becomes glyceryl tristearate, since every 18-carbon chain ends up as stearic acid. (c) B. Oxygen attacks most easily at the CH2 between two double bonds, and linoleic and linolenic chains have such positions; A has none. More C=C means faster oxidative rancidity.

Problem 9.54

Analyze the table. The table lists four fatty acids with their shorthand notation, the positions of their double bonds counted from the carboxyl carbon, and their melting points.

Fatty acidNotationC=C positions (from C1)Melting point
Myristic acid14:0none54 °C
Palmitoleic acid16:1C90 °C
Erucic acid22:1C1333 °C

(a) Give the omega class of each unsaturated acid. (b) Palmitoleic and erucic acids each have one cis double bond, yet erucic acid melts 33 °C higher. Explain. (c) Explain why EPA has the lowest melting point even though it is longer than palmitoleic acid. (d) Write the condensed structural formula of palmitoleic acid.

Answer
(a) Omega number = total carbons − position of the last double bond: palmitoleic 16 − 9 = 7 (omega-7); erucic 22 − 13 = 9 (omega-9); EPA 20 − 17 = 3 (omega-3). Problem 9.55

Analyze the table. A fasting lipid panel for a 54-year-old patient is shown with the laboratory's reference targets (all values in mg/dL).

TestPatient resultTarget
Total cholesterol232below 200
HDL cholesterol3840 or higher (60 or higher is protective)
LDL cholesterol (calculated)152below 100
Triglycerides210below 150

(a) Which results are outside the target range, and in which direction? (b) The lab calculates LDL as total cholesterol − HDL − triglycerides/5. Show that the reported LDL is consistent with the other three values. (c) "Triglycerides" is the clinical name for which lipid class, and what kind of bond does lipase break in them? (d) Why must the patient fast for 9–12 hours before this blood draw? (e) The provider starts a statin. Which enzyme does it inhibit, and why does that lower LDL?

Answer
(a) Total cholesterol (232) and LDL (152) are high; triglycerides (210) are high; HDL (38) is low. All four point the wrong way, a pattern called hyperlipidemia. (b) 232 − 38 − 210/5 = 232 − 38 − 42 = 152 mg/dL, which matches. (c) Triacylglycerols: glycerol esterified to three fatty acids. Lipase hydrolyzes the ester bonds, releasing fatty acids and glycerol. (d) After a meal, dietary triacylglycerols enter the blood in lipoprotein particles for several hours, so a non-fasting triglyceride value is inflated and the LDL calculation (which depends on it) becomes unreliable. (e) HMG-CoA reductase, the enzyme that controls cholesterol synthesis in the liver. Making less cholesterol makes the liver pull more LDL particles out of the blood, so circulating LDL falls.

More Practice: Clinical and Integrative

Problem 9.56

Nursing. A patient on warfarin (an anticoagulant) starts a "green smoothie" habit: two cups of kale and spinach every morning. Two weeks later the clinic finds that the blood takes less time to clot than it should. (a) Which fat-soluble vitamin is involved and what does it do chemically? (b) Why did the diet change matter? (c) A second patient asks whether she can "flush out" an accidental double dose of vitamin D the way she could with vitamin C. Explain the difference.

Answer
(a) Vitamin K. It is required to activate the clotting factors (II, VII, IX, X) made in the liver; warfarin works by blocking the recycling of vitamin K, so the factors stay inactive and clotting slows. (b) Leafy greens are rich in vitamin K1. A sudden large intake supplies enough vitamin K to overcome the warfarin block, so clotting speeds back up and the drug's effect is lost; the lesson is consistent intake, not avoidance. (c) Vitamin C is water-soluble: excess is filtered by the kidneys and leaves in urine within hours. Vitamin D is a nonpolar, fat-soluble steroid derivative: it is stored in fat and liver and leaves slowly, so repeated excess accumulates and can cause hypercalcemia (high blood calcium). Drinking water does not remove it.

Problem 9.57

Dental hygiene. Lidocaine is an amine local anesthetic. In solution it exists as an equilibrium between an uncharged base form and a protonated (positively charged) form. (a) Which form can cross the nonpolar nerve-cell membrane, and by what transport process? (b) An abscessed tooth sits in acidic, inflamed tissue. Use the equilibrium of amines from Chapter 7 to explain why anesthesia there is often incomplete. (c) If a toxic amount of anesthetic reached the bloodstream, an IV lipid emulsion could be given. In one sentence, what property of the drug makes this work?

Answer
(a) The uncharged base form. It is lipid-soluble and passes through the phospholipid bilayer by simple diffusion (no protein, no ATP). Once inside, the protonated form blocks the sodium channel. (b) In acid, the amine equilibrium R3N + H+ ↔ R3NH+ shifts toward the charged ammonium form. Charged ions cannot cross the nonpolar membrane, so less drug reaches the inside of the nerve and the block is weaker or slower. (c) The anesthetic is far more soluble in oil than in water, so it partitions into the emulsion's soybean-oil droplets and is drawn out of heart and brain tissue (a "lipid sink").

Problem 9.58

Nursing. A patient whose bile duct is blocked by a gallstone reports bulky, pale, foul-smelling stools that float. (a) Name this finding and explain, in terms of emulsification, why the fat is not being absorbed. (b) What is the gallstone most likely made of, and what normally keeps that substance dissolved in bile? (c) Which vitamins will the patient become deficient in if the blockage persists, and why those?

Answer
(a) Steatorrhea (fat in the stool). Bile salts are amphipathic steroid derivatives that break dietary fat into tiny droplets (emulsification) so that water-soluble pancreatic lipase can reach the ester bonds. Without bile, the fat stays in large globules, is barely hydrolyzed, and passes through unabsorbed. (b) Cholesterol. Bile is a solution of cholesterol kept dissolved by bile salts and lecithin (phosphatidylcholine), which surround it in micelles; when cholesterol exceeds what they can hold, it crystallizes into stones. (c) The fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, and K. They are nonpolar, dissolve in dietary fat, and are absorbed along with it; when fat is not absorbed, neither are they. Vitamin K loss shows up first as easy bruising and bleeding.

Problem 9.59

Integrative (Chapter 6 esters). Cetyl palmitate, CH3(CH2)14COO(CH2)15CH3, is the main ester in spermaceti, a wax once used in cosmetics and still mimicked in hand lotions. (a) Classify it as a lipid and name the functional group. (b) Write the products of its hydrolysis with aqueous acid and heat. (c) Write the products of its saponification with NaOH. (d) Name the reaction that would make cetyl palmitate from its two components, and state what small molecule is released.

Answer
(a) A wax (hydrolyzable lipid): one ester group joining a 16-carbon fatty acid to a 16-carbon alcohol. (b) Acid hydrolysis gives palmitic acid, CH3(CH2)14COOH, and cetyl alcohol (1-hexadecanol), CH3(CH2)15OH. (c) Saponification gives sodium palmitate, CH3(CH2)14COO− Na+ (a soap), plus the same cetyl alcohol; the base converts the acid to its salt. (d) Fischer esterification: palmitic acid + cetyl alcohol, with an acid catalyst and heat, gives the ester and releases one molecule of water (a condensation). Hydrolysis is the reverse reaction.

Problem 9.60

Integrative (Chapter 8 carbohydrates). Galactocerebroside, a lipid abundant in myelin, is built from sphingosine, a 24-carbon fatty acid, and one D-galactose attached to the terminal OH of sphingosine. (a) Classify this lipid (family and class). (b) Name the two kinds of bond that hold the three pieces together, using terms from Chapters 7 and 8. (c) What products form on complete hydrolysis? (d) Is galactocerebroside a reducing sugar? Explain.

Answer
(a) A hydrolyzable lipid; a sphingolipid, specifically a glycolipid (cerebroside), because its polar head is a sugar rather than a phosphate. (b) The fatty acid is joined to the amine of sphingosine by an amide bond (Chapter 7); the galactose is joined to the sphingosine OH by a glycosidic bond, which is an acetal (Chapter 8). (c) Sphingosine, the free 24-carbon fatty acid, and D-galactose. (d) No. Galactose's anomeric carbon is locked in the acetal (glycosidic) bond, so the ring cannot open to expose the aldehyde; a sugar must have a free hemiacetal carbon to reduce Benedict's reagent.

Problem 9.61

Which molecule is a polyunsaturated fatty acid?

  1. CH3(CH2)12COOH
  2. CH3(CH2)5CH=CH(CH2)7COOH
  3. CH3CH2CH=CHCH2CH=CHCH2CH=CH(CH2)7COOH
  4. CH3(CH2)16COO(CH2)29CH3
Answer
C. It has three C=C bonds (polyunsaturated) and a COOH; it is α-linolenic acid, 18:3. A is saturated (myristic acid), B has one C=C (monounsaturated, palmitoleic acid), and D is an ester with no COOH, a wax, not a fatty acid at all.

Problem 9.62

For the fatty acid CH3(CH2)4CH=CHCH2CH=CH(CH2)7COOH: (a) give the shorthand notation; (b) identify the α, β, and ω carbons; (c) give the omega class; (d) if the chain is drawn with the H atoms on each C=C on opposite sides, is it cis or trans, and would it be a natural fatty acid?

Answer
(a) 1 + 4 + 2 + 1 + 2 + 7 + 1 = 18 carbons, two C=C: 18:2 (linoleic acid). (b) α = C2 and β = C3, counting the COOH carbon as C1; ω = C18, the terminal CH3. (c) The last C=C from the COOH end is at C12, so 18 − 12 = 6: omega-6. (d) H atoms on opposite sides is trans. Natural unsaturated fatty acids are almost always cis, so a trans version would be a trans fat, formed during partial hydrogenation, with a straighter chain and a higher melting point.

Problem 9.63

Write the condensed structural formula of the wax formed from myristic acid, CH3(CH2)12COOH, and the alcohol CH3(CH2)22CH2OH. What kind of reaction is this, and what else is produced?

Answer
CH3(CH2)12COO(CH2)23CH3 + H2O. A condensation (esterification): the acid's OH and the alcohol's H leave as water and the ester forms. The acid supplies the C=O side; the 24-carbon alcohol supplies the O–CH2 side.

Problem 9.64

Draw (as condensed structures) the triacylglycerol made from glycerol and three molecules of lauric acid, CH3(CH2)10COOH. How many molecules of water are released?

Answer
Glycerol's three carbons each carry an ester: CH2–O–C(=O)(CH2)10CH3, CH–O–C(=O)(CH2)10CH3, CH2–O–C(=O)(CH2)10CH3 (glyceryl trilaurate, trilaurin). Three ester bonds form, so three H2O are released. It is saturated, so it is a solid fat (the main fat of coconut oil, which melts just below room temperature because the chains are short).

Problem 9.65

A triacylglycerol carries myristic acid (14:0) on C1, palmitoleic acid (16:1) on C2, and palmitic acid (16:0) on C3 of glycerol. (a) Write the products when it is hydrolyzed by lipase in the intestine. (b) Write the products when it is heated with excess NaOH. (c) Name the reaction type in each case.

Answer
(a) Glycerol + myristic acid, CH3(CH2)12COOH + palmitoleic acid, CH3(CH2)5CH=CH(CH2)7COOH + palmitic acid, CH3(CH2)14COOH. (b) Glycerol + sodium myristate, CH3(CH2)12COO−Na+ + sodium palmitoleate + sodium palmitate. (c) (a) is hydrolysis (water splits the three esters; the enzyme catalyzes it at body pH). (b) is saponification: base hydrolysis, which is hydrolysis followed by acid–base neutralization of each fatty acid to its salt (a soap). The C=C in the palmitoleate chain survives both reactions.

Problem 9.66

The reaction of a triacylglycerol with KOH and heat to give glycerol and potassium salts of fatty acids is best described as

  1. hydrogenation
  2. condensation
  3. hydrolysis under basic conditions (saponification)
  4. oxidation
Answer
C. Water (with base) splits the three ester bonds, so it is hydrolysis; because the conditions are basic, each fatty acid is neutralized to its potassium salt, which is why the products are soap rather than free acids. Hydrogenation adds H2 to C=C, condensation would build the ester, and oxidation (rancidity) attacks the C=C with O2.

Problem 9.68

A lipid is drawn with a three-carbon backbone bonded to two long-chain esters and, on the third carbon, a phosphate that is also bonded to –OCH2CH2NH3+. (a) Classify it. (b) Is it hydrolyzable? (c) Identify its polar head and nonpolar tails, and explain what it does in water.

Answer
(a) A glycerophospholipid: glycerol backbone, two fatty acid esters, a phosphodiester, and the amino alcohol ethanolamine (so it is phosphatidylethanolamine, cephalin). (b) Yes: it has ester links (and the phosphate esters), so acid, base, or enzymes split it into glycerol, two fatty acids, phosphate, and ethanolamine. (c) The charged phosphate–ammonium end is the polar head; the two fatty acid chains are the nonpolar tails. In water the molecules line up into a bilayer, heads facing the water on both sides and tails inside, the basic structure of a cell membrane.

Problem 9.69

A 20-carbon molecule ends in COOH, contains several C=C bonds, and has five of its carbons closed into a ring that carries a C=O and an OH. (a) What family and class of lipid is it? (b) Is it hydrolyzable? (c) Which fatty acid is it made from, and which enzyme makes it? (d) Name a drug class that blocks that enzyme.

Answer
(a) An eicosanoid, specifically a prostaglandin (the five-membered carbon ring is the giveaway; a thromboxane would have a six-membered ring containing an O, and a leukotriene would have no ring). (b) No. It has no ester or amide bond, only a carboxylic acid, alkenes, a ketone, and an alcohol. (c) Arachidonic acid (20:4), by the cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes. (d) NSAIDs such as aspirin and ibuprofen inhibit COX.

Problem 9.70

Rank from lowest to highest melting point and explain: glyceryl tristearate (three 18:0 chains), glyceryl trilinoleate (three 18:2 chains), glyceryl trioleate (three 18:1 chains).

Answer
Trilinoleate < trioleate < tristearate. All three have 18-carbon chains, so only the number of cis double bonds matters: six kinks (trilinoleate) pack worst and melt lowest; three kinks next; the fully saturated tristearate packs tightly and is a solid fat. The same rule you used for the free fatty acids carries over to the triacylglycerols made from them.

Problem 9.71

True or false? (If false, correct it.) Sphingomyelin is a non-hydrolyzable lipid because it contains no ester groups.

Answer
False. Sphingomyelin has no ester, but its fatty acid is attached to sphingosine by an amide bond, and amides are hydrolyzed by water (with acid, base, or an enzyme). It is a hydrolyzable lipid; the non-hydrolyzable lipids are the steroids, eicosanoids, and fat-soluble vitamins.

Problem 9.72

Classify each process as diffusion, facilitated transport, or active transport, and state whether cellular energy is required: (a) Cl− ions moving through a protein channel from high to low concentration; (b) a steroid hormone entering a cell; (c) Ca2+ being moved out of a muscle cell against its concentration gradient.

Answer
(a) Facilitated transport: a charged ion cannot cross the nonpolar bilayer on its own, so it uses a channel, but it moves down its gradient, so no energy is needed. (b) Diffusion (passive): the nonpolar steroid dissolves through the bilayer with no protein and no energy. (c) Active transport: moving against the gradient requires a pump and energy from ATP.

Cumulative Problem 9.1 (Chapters 4, 6 and 9)

A triacylglycerol (fat) is made from one glycerol and three fatty acids. Which statement is correct?

  1. It is a triester; saponification gives glycerol and three fatty-acid salts (soaps)
  2. It is a triether; hydrolysis gives glycerol and three alcohols
  3. It is a triamide; hydrolysis gives glycerol and three amines
  4. It is an acetal; hydrolysis gives three sugars
Answer
A. Glycerol is a triol (three alcohol groups, Chapter 4) and each fatty acid is a carboxylic acid, so joining them by three esterifications (Chapter 6) gives a triester and three waters. Heating the fat with NaOH hydrolyzes all three ester bonds (saponification) and gives glycerol plus three sodium carboxylates, which are soaps. The enzyme lipase does the same hydrolysis in your small intestine, releasing fatty acids and monoglycerides.

Cumulative Problem 9.2 (Chapters 1, 3 and 9)

Stearic acid (18:0) melts at 70 °C, oleic acid (18:1, one cis double bond) at 13 °C and linoleic acid (18:2) at −5 °C. (a) Explain the trend. (b) What reaction converts oleic acid to stearic acid? (c) Would a trans-18:1 acid melt closer to 70 °C or to 13 °C?

Answer
(a) Each cis double bond puts a rigid kink in the chain (no rotation around C=C, Chapter 3), so the molecules cannot pack closely and the dispersion forces between them (Chapter 1) are weaker; less energy is needed to pull them apart, so the melting point drops with every additional cis double bond. (b) Hydrogenation (H2, Ni catalyst) adds hydrogen across the double bond. (c) Closer to 70 °C. A trans double bond leaves the chain nearly straight, so trans fatty acids pack almost like saturated ones (elaidic acid, the trans isomer of oleic acid, melts at 45 °C). This is why partially hydrogenated oils are semi-solid and why trans fats behave like saturated fats in the body.

Cumulative Problem 9.3 (Chapters 1, 6, 7 and 9)

Phosphatidylcholine (lecithin) has two fatty-acid chains, a glycerol, a phosphate and a choline group, (CH3)3N+CH2CH2O–. (a) Which parts are hydrophilic and which hydrophobic, and what word describes a molecule with both? (b) What class of nitrogen compound is choline? (c) Explain how these molecules arrange themselves in water to form a cell membrane.

Answer
(a) The phosphate (negatively charged) and choline (positively charged) form a polar, ionic hydrophilic head; the two long hydrocarbon chains are the nonpolar hydrophobic tails. A molecule with both is amphipathic. (b) Choline is a quaternary ammonium ion: a nitrogen bonded to four carbons that carries a permanent positive charge (Chapter 7). (c) The heads hydrogen bond and interact ionically with water while the tails avoid it, so the molecules line up tail-to-tail in a double layer, the lipid bilayer, with heads facing the water on both sides. The nonpolar middle is why ions and glucose need protein channels to cross while small nonpolar molecules (O2, CO2, steroids, anesthetics) pass through directly.

Cumulative Problem 9.4 (Chapters 2, 3, 4 and 9)

Cholesterol has four fused rings, one C=C double bond, one –OH group and a branched eight-carbon tail. (a) Name the functional groups. (b) Is cholesterol a fat? (c) In blood, most cholesterol travels as cholesterol esters. What reacts with the –OH to make them, and does the ester become more or less polar than cholesterol itself?

Answer
(a) A 2° alcohol and an alkene; the fused rings are cycloalkanes (the steroid nucleus). (b) No. A fat is a triacylglycerol (an ester of glycerol). Cholesterol is a steroid, a different lipid class; it contains no ester and no fatty acid. (c) A fatty acid esterifies the alcohol (Chapter 6), giving a cholesterol ester. The product is less polar: the only polar group, the OH, has been capped, so the ester must be carried inside lipoprotein particles such as LDL and HDL rather than dissolving in plasma.

Cumulative Problem 9.5 (Chapters 2, 3 and 9)

α-Linolenic acid is written 18:3 (ω-3) and its double bonds are at carbons 9, 12 and 15 by IUPAC numbering. (a) From which end does the omega count start, and from which end does IUPAC numbering start? (b) Show that the IUPAC numbers and the ω-3 label agree. (c) Are these double bonds cis or trans in the natural acid?

Answer
(a) The ω count starts at the methyl (CH3) end, the far end of the hydrocarbon chain; IUPAC numbering starts at the carboxyl carbon (C1), as for any carboxylic acid (Chapter 6). (b) With 18 carbons, the double bond that begins at C15 by IUPAC numbering involves C15 and C16; counting from C18 backwards, C16 is the third carbon, so the first double bond is three carbons from the methyl end: ω-3. (c) All cis, as in nearly all natural fatty acids. The three cis kinks make α-linolenic acid a liquid well below freezing, which is why fish and flaxseed oils stay fluid in cold water and why fish-oil capsules can go rancid: the C=C bonds are easily oxidized by air.

Cumulative Problem 9.6 (Chapters 1, 4, 6 and 9)

In the clinic. Bile salts such as glycocholate are steroids with several –OH groups on one face of the ring system and a carboxylate-containing side chain. (a) Why are they amphipathic even though they are not phospholipids? (b) Explain how they let a water-based digestive enzyme (lipase) work on dietary fat. (c) A patient whose bile duct is blocked has pale, greasy stools and can become deficient in vitamins A, D, E and K. Explain both observations.

Answer
(a) The OH groups and the carboxylate give one polar face, while the other face of the rings and the hydrocarbon are nonpolar: a flat amphipathic molecule. (b) Bile salts coat fat droplets with their nonpolar faces inward and polar faces outward, breaking large droplets into tiny emulsified ones. That multiplies the surface area where water-soluble lipase can reach the ester bonds it hydrolyzes (Chapter 6). (c) Without bile, fat is not emulsified or absorbed, so it passes through undigested (greasy, pale stools). Vitamins A, D, E and K are nonpolar, fat-soluble molecules (Chapter 1: like dissolves like) that are absorbed only along with dietary fat, so they are lost too. Vitamin K deficiency then shows up as easy bleeding.
