Problem 13.42

Which statement about the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex is correct?

  1. It is the last enzyme of glycolysis and works in the cytosol.
  2. It reduces pyruvate to lactate using NADH.
  3. It removes CO2 from pyruvate, reduces NAD+, and attaches the two-carbon product to coenzyme A.
  4. It phosphorylates pyruvate using ATP.
Answer
C. Pyruvate dehydrogenase carries out an oxidative decarboxylation in the mitochondrion: one carbon leaves as CO2, NAD+ is reduced to NADH, and the acetyl group is linked to coenzyme A as a thioester. A is wrong because glycolysis ends at pyruvate in the cytosol; B describes lactate dehydrogenase; D describes no step in the pathway.

Problem 13.43

Which enzyme of glycolysis is a lyase?

  1. hexokinase
  2. phosphoglucose isomerase
  3. aldolase
  4. glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase
Answer
C. Aldolase breaks a C–C bond without using water and without oxidation, the definition of a lyase. Hexokinase is a transferase (kinase), phosphoglucose isomerase is an isomerase, and the dehydrogenase is an oxidoreductase. Enolase (step 9) is the other lyase in glycolysis.

Problem 13.44

Which statement about galactose and fructose from a meal is correct?

  1. They cannot be used for energy because glycolysis accepts only glucose.
  2. They are converted in the liver to intermediates of glycolysis and end up as pyruvate.
  3. They must first be stored as glycogen.
  4. They are excreted unchanged in the urine.
Answer
B. Glycolysis is written for glucose, but a few extra reactions in the liver (phosphorylation, isomerization, cleavage) turn galactose into glucose-6-phosphate and fructose into three-carbon intermediates, so all the common hexoses are metabolized to pyruvate. A is wrong for that reason; C and D do not happen in a healthy person.

Problem 13.45

Problem 13.47

Analyze the data. A biochemist measures the rate of three glycolysis enzymes in muscle extracts and reports each as a percentage of its maximum rate.

Problem 13.48

Compare and contrast the fate of pyruvate in a red blood cell and in a liver cell with normal oxygen supply: name the enzyme, the product, what happens to the NADH from glycolysis, and the net ATP per glucose at the end of the step.

Answer
Red blood cell: no mitochondria, so lactate dehydrogenase reduces pyruvate to lactate, consuming the 2 NADH from glycolysis and regenerating NAD+; net 2 ATP per glucose, 0 NADH left. Liver cell: pyruvate enters the mitochondrion, pyruvate dehydrogenase makes acetyl CoA + CO2 + NADH, and the glycolytic NADH is later re-oxidized by the electron transport chain for ATP; at the end of this step the cell has 2 ATP, 2 acetyl CoA, and 4 NADH (2 from glycolysis, 2 from pyruvate dehydrogenase) waiting to be cashed in (Chapter 14).

Problem 13.49

Predict and explain: a patient is given a drug that inhibits glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase. Which pathway is blocked, which two products fall, and which cell type is affected first? Would glycolysis and ATP production be affected directly?

Answer
The oxidative phase of the pentose phosphate pathway is blocked, so NADPH production falls (and, with it, ribose-5-phosphate from the oxidative route). Red blood cells are hit first because NADPH is their only defense against oxidants: without it glutathione stays oxidized and the cells hemolyze. Glycolysis is not directly affected, since G6PD is not a glycolysis enzyme and glucose-6-phosphate can still go down steps 2–10; ATP production continues.

More Practice: Clinical and Integrative

Problem 13.50

Clinical lab scenario. A 70-year-old arrives from a nursing home with fever, low blood pressure, and confusion. Labs: glucose 212 mg/dL, lactate 5.1 mmol/L (reference < 2), bicarbonate 16 mEq/L (reference 22–28), HbA1c 5.3%. (a) Explain the high lactate and the low bicarbonate with the pathway involved. (b) Explain the high glucose and name the hormones responsible. (c) Does this patient have diabetes? Justify from the data.

Answer
(a) Sepsis with low blood pressure means tissues are poorly perfused and short of O2. Pyruvate cannot be oxidized in the mitochondria, so lactate dehydrogenase reduces it to lactate to regenerate NAD+ for glycolysis. Lactic acid is ionized at blood pH, so each lactate arrives with an H+, which is neutralized by bicarbonate; bicarbonate falls (lactic acidosis). (b) Stress hyperglycemia: epinephrine triggers liver glycogenolysis and suppresses insulin, cortisol drives gluconeogenesis from amino acids, and inflammatory cytokines cause insulin resistance so the glucose stays in the blood. (c) No evidence of diabetes: the HbA1c of 5.3% shows a normal average glucose over the past 2–3 months. The high glucose is acute and should be rechecked after recovery.

Problem 13.51

Dental hygiene scenario. A patient sips a 20-ounce soda over three hours every afternoon at work. Sketch in words what the plaque pH does during those three hours, name the chemical responsible, and state the critical pH for enamel. Then explain why switching to a xylitol-sweetened drink would change the picture.

Answer
Each sip supplies sucrose/fructose/glucose to plaque bacteria, which run glycolysis and ferment pyruvate to lactic acid. Plaque pH drops within minutes and would normally recover in 20–40 minutes as saliva buffers it, but repeated sipping keeps the pH below the critical value of about 5.5 for most of the three hours, so hydroxyapatite dissolves (demineralization) with little time for remineralization. Xylitol is a five-carbon sugar alcohol that plaque bacteria cannot ferment, so no lactic acid is produced and the pH stays above 5.5; chewing or sipping also stimulates saliva, which adds buffering and mineral.

Problem 13.52

Nursing scenario. A patient with alcohol use disorder is admitted confused and malnourished. The nurse is told to give thiamine before the IV dextrose. Explain, using the pyruvate dehydrogenase step, why the order matters.

Answer
Thiamine (vitamin B1) is a required cofactor of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, the step that converts pyruvate to acetyl CoA for the citric acid cycle. A thiamine-deficient patient already struggles to oxidize pyruvate. A dextrose load pushes a surge of glucose through glycolysis to pyruvate, which, with the step blocked, is diverted to lactate and starves the brain of aerobic ATP; this can precipitate or worsen Wernicke encephalopathy. Giving thiamine first restores the cofactor so the glucose can be used safely.

Problem 13.53

Integrative (Chapters 8 and 13). Lactose is a disaccharide of galactose and glucose joined by a β(1→4) glycosidic bond. (a) Name the enzyme and reaction type that releases the two monosaccharides, and where in the body it happens. (b) In one or two sentences, say where the galactose goes next and which glycolysis intermediate it becomes; name the enzyme class of a step that keeps the formula the same but changes the arrangement of atoms. (c) A newborn screening test is positive for galactosemia. Which compound accumulates and what dietary change follows?

Answer
(a) Lactase, a hydrolase in the brush border of the small intestine, hydrolyzes the glycosidic bond (hydrolysis). (b) Galactose travels to the liver, where it is phosphorylated (kinase, transferase) and rearranged into glucose-6-phosphate, which enters glycolysis at step 2; the rearrangement steps that keep the same formula are carried out by isomerases. (c) Galactose-1-phosphate accumulates behind the missing liver enzyme; the infant is switched to a lactose-free formula and galactose is restricted in the diet lifelong (details in Section 13.6).

Problem 13.54

Integrative (Chapters 11 and 13). Glycogen phosphorylase and glycogen synthase are both regulated by phosphorylation. (a) Which type of enzyme regulation is this, and which hormones trigger the phosphorylation? (b) What does the same phosphate do to each enzyme, and why is that arrangement efficient? (c) Which hormone removes the phosphates, and what is the cell doing then?

Answer
(a) Covalent modification (Section 11.7); glucagon in the liver and epinephrine in muscle and liver trigger it through a protein kinase. (b) Phosphorylation turns glycogen phosphorylase on and glycogen synthase off, so one signal starts breakdown and stops synthesis at the same time; the cell never wastes ATP running both directions at once. (c) Insulin activates phosphatases that remove the phosphates; the cell is then in the fed state, storing glucose as glycogen and not breaking it down.

Problem 13.56

Fill in the blanks. Oxidation is a ______ of electrons, a ______ of oxygen atoms, or a ______ of hydrogen atoms. A coenzyme that acts as an oxidizing agent is itself ______ during the reaction.

Answer

Problem 13.57

Rank ATP, ADP, and AMP from highest to lowest energy. When ATP is hydrolyzed to ADP, is energy released or absorbed? Which kind of bond is broken, a phosphoester bond or a phosphoanhydride bond?

Answer
ATP > ADP > AMP. Energy is released (the process is exothermic; energy is a product), which is why ATP hydrolysis is coupled to steps that need energy. The bond broken is a phosphate–phosphate phosphoanhydride bond; the phosphate–ribose link is the phosphoester bond and is not the one hydrolyzed here.

Problem 13.58

In glycogenolysis, a glucose unit is removed from glycogen as glucose-1-phosphate using free phosphate (Pi). Set up the table: reaction type, how you can tell, enzyme class, coenzyme or nucleotide. Then explain why this is not the reverse of the last step of glycogenesis.

Answer
Reaction type: phosphorolysis, the removal of a glucose by phosphorylation with Pi. How you can tell: a glycosidic bond is broken but the reagent is phosphate, not water and not ATP, and the product sugar is already phosphorylated. Enzyme: glycogen phosphorylase, a transferase. Coenzyme/nucleotide: none (Pi is the reagent). The last step of glycogenesis adds glucose from UDP-glucose using glycogen synthase and releases UDP; glycogenolysis involves no UDP or UTP at all, so it is a different reaction with a different enzyme.

Problem 13.59

In glycogenesis, glucose-1-phosphate reacts with UTP to form UDP-glucose and pyrophosphate. (a) What is the purpose of this step? (b) What type of reaction is it, and what tells you? (c) The UDP released later is turned back into UTP; write that reaction and say what it costs the cell.

Answer
(a) Activation: the glucose is attached to a nucleotide so glycogen synthase can add it to the chain in the next step. (b) A nucleotide-transfer reaction (a transferase); you can tell because UTP is consumed, PPi is released, and the sugar now carries UDP. (c) UDP + ATP → UTP + ADP; it costs one ATP, so storing a glucose in glycogen costs about 2 ATP equivalents in all (one at hexokinase, one to remake UTP).

Problem 13.60

True or false; correct any false statement. (a) In gluconeogenesis, the reverse of glycolysis step 3 requires ATP. (b) Seven of the ten glycolysis reactions run in reverse during gluconeogenesis using the same enzymes. (c) The three glycolysis steps that are not reversible are all catalyzed by kinases. (d) Gluconeogenesis is a catabolic pathway.

Answer
(a) False: the reverse of step 3 is a hydrolysis by fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase and requires no ATP (and recovers none). (b) True. (c) True: hexokinase, phosphofructokinase, and pyruvate kinase. (d) False: gluconeogenesis builds glucose from smaller molecules and consumes ATP, so it is anabolic; glycolysis is the catabolic partner.

Problem 13.61

Fill in the blanks. Gluconeogenesis uses ATP or GTP at ____ steps, each occurring twice per glucose, for a net expenditure of ____ ATP equivalents. Glycolysis produces a net ____ ATP per glucose. The two new enzymes that gluconeogenesis uses in place of phosphofructokinase and hexokinase are ______ and ______, and both belong to the enzyme class ______.

Problem 13.62

The first step of gluconeogenesis converts three-carbon pyruvate into four-carbon oxaloacetate while ATP is converted to ADP. What type of reaction is this, how can you tell, and why is this detour needed instead of simply running pyruvate kinase backward?

Answer
A carboxylation: the carbon count rises from 3 to 4 because CO2 is added, and ATP → ADP shows that energy was needed. (The enzyme that joins two molecules using ATP is a ligase.) The reverse of pyruvate kinase cannot be done in one step because the energy difference between pyruvate and phosphoenolpyruvate is too large; going through oxaloacetate, and then spending GTP to make PEP, breaks the climb into two affordable steps.

Problem 13.63

Multiple choice. Why does carbohydrate digestion not take place in the stomach?

  1. The stomach contains no water for hydrolysis.
  2. The low pH of the stomach destroys the activity of salivary amylase.
  3. Starch cannot enter the stomach.
  4. The stomach secretes an amylase inhibitor.

B. Salivary amylase is a protein whose shape, and therefore activity, is destroyed by the acid of the stomach (denaturation, Chapter 10). Digestion of starch resumes in the small intestine, where pancreatic amylase works at the higher pH there.

Problem 13.64

Multiple choice. Starch is broken down into maltose and glucose during digestion. This reaction is best described as

  1. a hydrolysis that releases energy for the body to capture as ATP.
  2. a hydrolysis that does not produce ATP.
  3. a phosphorylation that requires ATP.
  4. a combustion.
Answer
B. Digestion (Stage 1) is hydrolysis of glycosidic bonds by amylase and maltase, which are hydrolases. It is exothermic, but the small amount of energy released is lost as heat; no ATP is captured until Stage 2.

Problem 13.65

Use the Latin roots. Each root has a meaning: -lysis, -neo-, -gen-, -esis. (a) Give the meaning of each root. (b) Using the roots, define glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis in plain words and give the initial reactant and final product of each.

Problem 13.67

A step of a pathway is described in words: “Water is removed from a three-carbon phosphate ester, creating a carbon–carbon double bond.” What type of reaction is this: hydration, dehydration, dehydrogenation, hydrolysis, or condensation? Cumulative Problem 13.1 (Chapters 5, 6, 8 and 13)

Glycolysis converts one glucose to two pyruvate. Which statement is correct?

  1. Net 2 ATP and 2 NADH are produced, and pyruvate contains a ketone and a carboxylate
  2. Net 4 ATP and no NADH are produced, and pyruvate contains an aldehyde
  3. Net 2 ATP and 2 FADH2 are produced, and pyruvate contains two alcohols
  4. Net 36 ATP are produced, and pyruvate is CO2
Answer
A. Glycolysis spends 2 ATP in the first half and makes 4 in the second, for a net of 2 ATP, plus 2 NADH from the one oxidation step. Pyruvate, CH3COCOO−, has a ketone (Chapter 5) and a carboxylate (the ionized form of the acid at body pH, Chapter 6). Each six-carbon glucose (Chapter 8) has been split into two three-carbon pieces, and no oxygen was needed.

Cumulative Problem 13.2 (Chapters 4, 5 and 13)

When muscle works without enough oxygen, pyruvate is converted to lactate. (a) Write the reaction with its coenzyme and classify it as an oxidation or a reduction of pyruvate. (b) Which functional group changes, and into what? (c) Why does the cell bother doing this when it produces no ATP? (d) Is lactate chiral?

Answer
(a) Pyruvate + NADH + H+ → lactate + NAD+, catalyzed by lactate dehydrogenase. Pyruvate gains hydrogen, so it is reduced. (b) The ketone becomes a 2° alcohol (Chapter 5 reduction of a carbonyl). (c) Glycolysis needs NAD+ at its oxidation step. Without oxygen the mitochondria cannot recycle NADH, so the cell uses pyruvate as the hydrogen acceptor to regenerate NAD+ and keep glycolysis, and its 2 ATP per glucose, running. (d) Yes: the middle carbon carries H, OH, CH3 and COO−; the enzyme makes only L-lactate.

Cumulative Problem 13.3 (Chapters 4, 5 and 13)

Yeast turn pyruvate into ethanol in two steps. Name the two steps, give the intermediate, and identify the organic reaction type of each step. Which step releases the gas that makes bread rise?

Answer
Step 1: pyruvate loses CO2 (a decarboxylation) to give acetaldehyde (ethanal), CH3CHO. Step 2: acetaldehyde is reduced by NADH to ethanol, an aldehyde-to-1°-alcohol reduction (the reverse of the Chapter 4 oxidation). Step 1 releases the CO2 that leavens bread; in beer and wine both the gas and the ethanol are kept. Like lactate formation, the point of step 2 is to regenerate NAD+.

Cumulative Problem 13.4 (Chapters 6, 10, 11 and 13)

The first step of glycolysis is glucose + ATP → glucose 6-phosphate + ADP, catalyzed by hexokinase. (a) What kind of bond is formed on the glucose, and to which enzyme class does hexokinase belong? (b) Why does this step trap glucose inside the cell? (c) Insulin and glucagon control whether cells store or release glucose. What kind of molecules are they, and what does each signal?

Answer
(a) The phosphate is joined to the C6 –OH as a phosphate ester (Chapter 6); the enzyme moves a phosphate group from ATP to glucose, so it is a transferase (a kinase, Chapter 11). (b) Glucose 6-phosphate carries two negative charges, so it cannot pass back through the nonpolar membrane or through the glucose transporter; the cell has committed it. (c) Both are peptide hormones (proteins, Chapter 10). Insulin, released after a meal, signals cells to take up glucose and store it as glycogen and fat; glucagon, released during fasting, signals the liver to break down glycogen and make new glucose.

Cumulative Problem 13.5 (Chapters 5, 8 and 13)

Starch and cellulose are both polymers of glucose, yet we digest one and not the other. (a) Which enzymes digest starch, where, and what bond do they break? (b) Why can we not digest cellulose? (c) What enzyme is missing in lactose intolerance, and what sugar then reaches the colon?

Answer
(a) Salivary α-amylase in the mouth and pancreatic α-amylase in the small intestine hydrolyze the α-1,4 glycosidic (acetal) bonds (Chapters 5 and 8) to maltose and smaller fragments; maltase on the intestinal wall finishes the job to glucose. (b) Cellulose is linked by β-1,4 bonds. Our amylases are shaped for the α link and cannot fit the β one, so cellulose passes through as fiber; cows and termites rely on microbes that make cellulase. (c) Lactase; undigested lactose reaches the colon, where bacteria ferment it.

Cumulative Problem 13.6 (Chapters 4, 6, 8, 11 and 13)

In the dental office. Plaque bacteria run glycolysis on the sugar in a sip of soda and release lactic acid for about 20 minutes afterward (the “Stephan curve”). (a) Why do the bacteria make lactate instead of sending pyruvate to the citric acid cycle? (b) Explain why sipping a soda all afternoon damages teeth more than drinking it in five minutes. (c) Xylitol gum and fluoride toothpaste each interrupt this chemistry in a different place. Where?

Answer
(a) Deep in plaque there is little oxygen, so the bacteria ferment: pyruvate is reduced to lactate to regenerate NAD+, and the acid is dumped outside the cell. (b) Each exposure to sugar drops the plaque pH below 5.5 for about 20 minutes, the time it takes saliva to buffer and wash the acid away. Sipping all afternoon keeps the pH low continuously, so enamel is dissolving the whole time; five minutes of exposure costs one 20-minute acid attack. (c) Xylitol (a sugar alcohol, Chapter 4) is taken up by the bacteria but cannot enter glycolysis, so no acid is made, and it crowds out real sugar. Fluoride inhibits the glycolytic enzyme enolase (Chapter 11), slowing acid production, and it hardens enamel into fluorapatite, which resists acid better.
