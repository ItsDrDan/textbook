DRAWING LEWIS STRUCTURES

For very simple molecules and molecular ions, we can write the Lewis structures by merely pairing up the unpaired electrons on the constituent atoms. See these examples:

For more complicated molecules and molecular ions, it is helpful to follow the step-by-step procedure outlined here:

  1. Determine the total number of valence (outer shell) electrons among all the atoms. For cations, subtract one electron for each positive charge. For anions, add one electron for each negative charge.
  2. Draw a skeleton structure of the molecule or ion, arranging the atoms around a central atom. (Generally, the least electronegative element should be placed in the center.) Connect each atom to the central atom with a single bond (one electron pair).
  3. Distribute the remaining electrons as lone pairs on the terminal atoms (except hydrogen), completing an octet around each atom.
  4. Place all remaining electrons on the central atom.
  5. Rearrange the electrons of the outer atoms to make multiple bonds with the central atom in order to obtain octets wherever possible.

Let us determine the Lewis structures of OF2 and HCN as examples in following this procedure:

1. Determine the total number of valence (outer shell) electrons in the molecule or ion. For a molecule, we add the number of valence electrons (use the main group number) on each atom in the molecule. This is the total number of electrons that must be used in the Lewis structure.

O + 2 (F) = OF2

6e- + (2 x 7e-) = 20e-

H + C + N = HCN

1e-+ 4e-+ 5e-= 10e-

2. Draw a skeleton structure of the molecule or ion, arranging the atoms around a central atom and connecting each atom to the central atom with a single (one electron pair) bond. Note that H and F can only form one bond, and are always on the periphery rather than the central atom.

3. Distribute the remaining electrons as lone pairs on the terminal atoms (except hydrogen) to complete their valence shells with an octet of electrons.

  • In OF2, six electrons are placed on each F.
  • In HCN, six electrons placed on N

4. Place all remaining electrons on the central atom.

  • In OF2, 4 electrons are placed on O.
  • In HCN: no electrons remain (the total valence of 10e-is reached) so nothing changes.

5. Rearrange the electrons of the outer atoms to make multiple bonds with the central atom in order to obtain octets wherever possible.

  • In OF2, each atom has an octet as drawn, so nothing changes.
  • In HCN, form two more C–N bonds

Finally, check to see if the total number of valence electrons are present in the Lewis structure. And then, inspect if the H atom has 2 electrons surrounding it and if each of the main group atoms is surrounded by 8 electrons.

Example \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Which is the correct Lewis structure for N2H2?

A.

B.

C.

Solution

Lewis structure A is the correct answer. It has a total of (2 x 5e-) + (2 x 1e-) = 12e-. Each of the N atoms satisfies the octet requirement and the H atoms follow the duet rule.

Structure B is electron deficient. It has only 10e- instead of 12.

Structure C has 14 (2 extra) electrons. The N atoms do not satisfy the octet.

Exercise \(\PageIndex{2}\)

Which is the correct Lewis structure for NOCl?

A.

B.

C.

Answer

Structure A violates the octet rule; N is surrounded by only 6e-.

Structure B violates the octet rule; Cl has 10e- around it. Furthermore, there are a total of 20e- instead of 18e-.

Structure C is the correct structure. It has a total of 6e- + 5e- + 7e- = 18e-. Each atom is surrounded by 8 electrons (octet rule).

Key Takeaways

  • A Lewis structure shows the bonding and nonbonding electrons around individual atoms in a molecule.
  • Some molecules must have multiple covalent bonds between atoms to satisfy the octet rule.
  • A double bond contains four electrons and a triple bond contains six electrons.

Lecture Connection

The lecture rule for Lewis structures is count first, draw second. A structure that uses the wrong number of electrons is wrong no matter how tidy it looks, and most exam errors are caught by a ten-second count.

Count first, draw second

To check a finished Lewis structure, count the total valence electrons first (for CH3CHO: 2(4) + 4(1) + 1(6) = 18 e−) and make sure the drawing uses exactly that many. The three errors to hunt for: too many or too few electrons, an atom without its octet, and a hydrogen with two bonds.

Professor Tip

The source text's procedure places the least electronegative atom in the center, which is the right rule for inorganic molecules such as OF2. For organic molecules you rarely have to guess: the condensed formula is the skeleton. Connect the atoms in the order the formula reads. CH3NH2 means a carbon bonded to three hydrogens and to a nitrogen that carries two hydrogens; CH3CHO means two carbons, with the second carbon bonded to one H and one O. Hydrogen and the halogens make one bond, so they are always on the outside, never in the middle. Once the skeleton is down, give every atom its cheat-sheet number of bonds and lone pairs (Section 1.1) and the electron count takes care of itself.

Example 1: Finding the error in a Lewis structure

Problem

Each description below is a Lewis structure a student drew for a neutral molecule. Decide whether each is correct, and if not, say what the error is. (a) Methanol: C with three H and one O; the O has one H and three lone pairs. (b) Formaldehyde, H2C=O: C with two H and a double bond to O; the O has two lone pairs. (c) Ethanethiol, CH3CH2SH: the S has two bonds and one lone pair. (d) Methylamine with the N bonded to the carbon and to three hydrogens, no lone pair.

Solution

  • (a) Too many electrons. Methanol has 4 + 4(1) + 6 = 14 valence electrons, but five bonds plus three lone pairs is 16. An oxygen with 2 bonds and 3 lone pairs would also have 10 electrons around it, breaking the octet. Oxygen needs 2 bonds and 2 lone pairs.
  • (b) Correct. 4 + 2(1) + 6 = 12 electrons; three bonds (one of them double) use 8 and the two lone pairs use 4. C has four bonds, O has two bonds and two lone pairs.
  • (c) Octet not satisfied. Sulfur is in oxygen's group, so it needs 2 bonds and 2 lone pairs for eight electrons. With only one lone pair it has six.
  • (d) Wrong bond count on N. Four bonds and no lone pair is the pattern for a positive nitrogen (CH3NH3+). Neutral N has 3 bonds and 1 lone pair, so the molecule should be CH3NH2.

Check

Three questions catch nearly every error: Does the electron count match the total valence electrons? Does every atom other than H have an octet? Does every atom have its usual number of bonds (C 4, N 3, O and S 2, H and halogens 1)? Any "no" means either a mistake or a charge. ✓
