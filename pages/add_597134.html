Lecture Connection

In lecture, carbohydrates open with a promise: you will meet only a handful of molecules (glucose, galactose, fructose, three disaccharides, four polysaccharides), but all the chemistry is chemistry you already own. If you can draw a hemiacetal from Chapter 5, assign D/L from Chapter 5, and oxidize an aldehyde or an alcohol from Chapters 4 and 5, you can do everything in this chapter.

What a carbohydrate is, and why we care

Carbohydrates are made of C, H, and O; they are also called saccharides ("sugars"). Chemically they are polyhydroxy aldehydes or polyhydroxy ketones: one C=O plus an OH on almost every other carbon. The general formula of a simple sugar is CnH2nOn (usually n = 3 to 6), which looks like carbon plus water, the "hydrate of carbon" misreading the section text describes. Why they matter, in the order we say it in class:

  • Carbohydrates are about 65% of the diet and a major source of energy: they release energy when oxidized (Chapter 13).
  • Plants make them by photosynthesis to store energy; animals store glucose short-term as glycogen (Section 8.7).
  • They supply carbons for other biomolecules (proteins, lipids, nucleic acids).
  • Ribose and deoxyribose form the sugar backbone of RNA and DNA (Chapter 12).

Professor Tip

Use the section's own test to decide whether a drawing is a carbohydrate: is there an aldehyde or ketone, and does every other carbon carry an OH? A molecule with a C=O but a bare CH2 or CH3 somewhere is not a monosaccharide. The prefix tells you the size of the chain (mono-, di-, poly-), and the rule to remember for later is that di- and polysaccharides can be hydrolyzed back to monosaccharides, while a monosaccharide cannot be split any further (Section 8.6).
