Lecture Connection

Quaternary structure exists only when a protein is made of two or more separate chains. Same forces as tertiary structure, just acting between chains instead of within one. If a protein is a single chain, it has no quaternary structure, no matter how big it is.

Hemoglobin, insulin, myoglobin

  • Hemoglobin is the classic case: two α subunits (141 residues each) and two β subunits (146 residues each), each cradling a heme group whose Fe2+ binds one O2 and carries it through the blood. Carbon monoxide is poisonous because it binds that same Fe2+ about 200 times more tightly than O2 does, so a small amount of CO takes a large share of the oxygen-carrying sites out of service; the patient looks pink and the pulse oximeter can read normal while the tissues starve.
  • Insulin, with two chains (A and B) linked by disulfide bonds, is another protein with quaternary structure; here the chains are held by covalent –S–S– links as well as the weak interactions.
  • A single-chain protein such as myoglobin, the oxygen store in muscle, has primary, secondary and tertiary structure but no quaternary structure. Its fold looks like one hemoglobin subunit.

Because the subunits are held by the same weak R-group forces as tertiary structure, anything that denatures a protein (Section 10.10) also pulls its subunits apart.
