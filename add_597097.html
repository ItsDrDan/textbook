Lecture Connection

Ethers get two kinds of names in this course, and you need both: the common name ("alkyl alkyl ether") that the section text teaches, and the IUPAC name, which treats the shorter group as an alkoxy substituent on the longer chain. For properties, remember the lecture's line: ethers cannot hydrogen bond to each other, but they can accept a hydrogen bond from water or from an R–OH.

Naming ethers two ways

  • Common name: name both R groups, arrange them alphabetically, and follow with the separate word "ether": CH3OCH2CH3 is ethyl methyl ether. If both groups are the same, use "di": diethyl ether.
  • IUPAC name: the longer R group is the parent chain and the shorter R group, together with its oxygen, is a substituent on that chain called an alkoxy group. Instead of the -yl ending, use -oxy: methoxy (CH3O–), ethoxy (CH3CH2O–). CH3OCH2CH3 is methoxyethane; CH3CH2OCH2CH2CH3 is 1-ethoxypropane; CH3OCH(CH3)CH2CH3 is 2-methoxybutane. Number the chain so the alkoxy group gets the lowest number.

Practice (lecture style). Draw 2-methoxybutane: a four-carbon chain with CH3O– on C2, CH3CH(OCH3)CH2CH3. Name CH3–O–CH2CH2CH2CH2CH3: the longer group is pentane, the methoxy group is on C1, so 1-methoxypentane (common name: methyl pentyl ether).

Example: Naming an ether both ways

Problem. Give a common name and an IUPAC name for CH3OCH2CH2CH3.

Solution. The ether has a methyl group and a propyl group on oxygen. Common name: list them alphabetically, then "ether": methyl propyl ether. IUPAC: the longer chain (propane) is the parent and CH3O– is a methoxy substituent on C1: 1-methoxypropane.

Check. Ether common names end in a separate word "ether"; IUPAC ether names contain "-oxy" and no "ether" at all. The alkoxy group is always the shorter group: 1-methoxypropane, never "propoxymethane."

Boiling point: like an alkane. Solubility: like an alcohol.

An ether has no O–H, so there is no hydrogen bonding between ether molecules, and its boiling point is close to that of an alkane of the same mass. But the ether oxygen's lone pairs can accept hydrogen bonds from water, so an ether dissolves in water about as well as its isomeric alcohol. The section's table makes both points; the worked example below is the comparison the lecture uses.

Example: Comparing boiling points and solubility

Problem. Compare the boiling points and water solubility of 1-butanol, diethyl ether and pentane (all about 72–74 g/mol).

Solution. Boiling point: Pentane (36 °C) and diethyl ether (35 °C) boil at nearly the same temperature. Neither can hydrogen bond with itself, and the ether's weak dipole adds very little. 1-Butanol (118 °C) is far higher because its O–H groups hydrogen bond with each other. Solubility: Diethyl ether and 1-butanol are both moderately soluble (about 7–8 g/100 mL) because the oxygen in each can accept hydrogen bonds from water. Pentane has no polar group and is insoluble.

Check. Hydrogen bonding with itself controls boiling point. Hydrogen bonding with water controls solubility.

In this course

The section text says ethers boil at "about the same" temperature as alkanes of comparable molar mass. The lecture is a little more precise: an ether is polar (bent C–O–C, dipole–dipole forces), so at equal mass and shape it boils slightly above the alkane. In the lecture's example, 1-methoxypropane boils at 39 °C and pentane at 36 °C. When an exam asks you to rank, use alkane < ether < alcohol; the gap between the first two is small, the gap to the alcohol is large.

Functional group isomers

Every ether has an alcohol isomer with the same formula: dimethyl ether and ethanol (C2H6O), diethyl ether and 1-butanol (C4H10O). The lecture calls these functional group isomers, the third kind of constitutional isomer after skeletal and positional. Section 4.2's Lecture Connection draws all five C4H10O isomers side by side. The quick test for a pair: "same functional group?" If not, they are functional group isomers, and you can expect very different boiling points and reactivity (ethers do not oxidize or dehydrate).

Professor Tip

The anesthetic story in the section text is a reminder that "ether" is also a drug name. Diethyl ether's replacements (halothane, isoflurane) are halogenated ethers, and the eucalyptol in mouthrinses is a cyclic ether. When you see a C–O–C with no O–H, you have found the ether, whatever the rest of the molecule looks like.
