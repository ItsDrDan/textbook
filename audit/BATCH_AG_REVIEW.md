## 15.6 Biosynthesis of Fatty Acids

**8. 15.6 conflict: two routes out of the mitochondrion (citrate and acetylcarnitine) vs. one in the Lecture Connection**

Why: The Lecture Connection teaches only the citrate route (the citrate–malate shuttle), which is the route that supplies fatty acid synthesis; the acetylcarnitine sentence is cut so the page tells one story.

- Before: Two molecules can play roles in moving it to the cytosol – citrate and acetylcarnitine. Joining of oxaloacetate with acetyl-CoA in the mitochondrion creates citrate which moves across the membrane, followed by action of citrate lyase in the cytosol of the cell to release acetyl-CoA and oxaloacetate. Additionally, when free acetyl-CoA accumulates in the mitochondrion, it may combine with carnitine and be transported out to the cytosol.
- After: It travels as citrate: oxaloacetate and acetyl-CoA join in the mitochondrion to make citrate, which crosses the membrane, and citrate lyase in the cytosol splits it back into acetyl-CoA and oxaloacetate.

**9. 15.6 cut: the start-up and the four elongation reactions are told twice**

Why: The Lecture Connection ("Getting started" and the "Chain elongation" table, with the figure) gives every step with more detail; the section text becomes a short overview that points to it.

- Before: The fatty acid chain is built two carbons at a time (opposite of beta-oxidation), beginning with two acetyl-CoA molecules. One is converted to malonyl-CoA by carboxylation, catalyzed by the enzyme acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC). Next, both acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA have their CoA portions replaced by a carrier protein known as ACP (acyl carrier protein) to form acetyl-ACP and malonyl-ACP. Joining acetyl-ACP with malonyl-ACP releases the CO2 that was added and gives a four-carbon chain, acetoacetyl-ACP (see the figure in the Lecture Connection below). From this point forward, the reaction types mirror those of beta-oxidation in reverse order, although the pathway is not beta-oxidation run backward (see the Lecture Connection). First, the ketone is reduced to a hydroxyl using NADPH. Next, water is removed from carbons 2 and 3 of the hydroxyl intermediate to produce a trans double-bonded mol
- After: The fatty acid chain is built two carbons at a time on an acyl carrier protein (ACP), using malonyl-CoA as the donor and NADPH as the reducing agent. Four reactions repeat (condensation, reduction, dehydration, reduction) until a 16-carbon chain is released as palmitate. The Lecture Connection below shows how the starting complexes are made and classifies each reaction.

**15. 10.5 wording: how tannic acid denatures**

Why: Tannins bind proteins mainly through hydrogen bonds from their phenol –OH groups (and hydrophobic contacts), not only through ionic bonds; the cell now says both.

- Before: These reagents combine with positively charged amino groups in proteins to disrupt ionic bonds.
- After: Their many –OH groups hydrogen bond to the protein, and they also bind positively charged amino groups, disrupting the protein’s own hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds so it clumps and precipitates.
