Lecture Connection

The source gives the three IUPAC rules; lecture adds the ring rules, the tie-breaker, and two drawing conventions that cost students points every semester. The one rule to tattoo on your arm: the multiple bond gets the lowest number, even if a branch ends up higher.

Naming alkenes, lecture style

An unsaturated hydrocarbon contains at least one carbon–carbon double or triple bond, so it holds fewer hydrogens than the alkane with the same number of carbons. Alkenes (C=C) have the general formula CnH2n and alkynes (C≡C) have CnH2n−2. The two carbons of a double bond are trigonal planar (120° angles); the two carbons of a triple bond are linear (180°).

  • Pick the longest chain that contains the double or triple bond.
  • Number from the end that gives the multiple bond the lowest number, even if a branch ends up with a higher number.
  • Change the ending: -ene (C=C) or -yne (C≡C). Put the number of the first carbon of the multiple bond in front of the parent: 2-pentene (also written pent-2-ene). Two double bonds: -diene, as in 1,3-butadiene.
  • Rings: cyclohexene. C1 and C2 are the double-bond carbons, and no number is needed for the double bond itself. Number through the double bond toward the first substituent: a methyl group on the carbon next to the double bond gives 3-methylcyclohexene, never 6-methylcyclohexene. A methyl group on a double-bond carbon makes it C1: 1-methylcyclopentene.
  • Alkynes follow exactly the same rules with -yne: HC≡CCH2CH3 is 1-butyne; CH3C≡CCH3 is 2-butyne.
  • Tie-breaker: if the multiple bond is the same distance from both ends, begin numbering at the end closest to the first branch.

Two drawing conventions: in a condensed formula, always write the multiple bond in (CH3CH=CH2, never CH3CHCH2), and in a line structure draw the two carbons of a triple bond and their neighbors in a straight line, because the alkyne carbons are linear. You will also hear common names: ethylene (ethene), propylene (propene), and acetylene (ethyne). They are not IUPAC names, so treat them as slang you can recognize but should not write on an exam.

In this course

The source text writes the locant in front (2-pentene). Newer IUPAC style puts it next to the suffix (pent-2-ene). Both are accepted on exams and in the games; use whichever you read more easily, but be consistent inside one name. Common names (ethylene, propylene, acetylene) are for recognizing, not writing: if a question says "name," give the IUPAC name.

Example 1: Numbering for the double bond, not the branch

Problem

Name CH3CH(CH3)CH2CH=CH2, and explain why "2-methyl-4-pentene" is wrong.

Solution

The longest chain containing the C=C has five carbons: a pentene. Numbering from the right puts the double bond at C1; from the left it would be C4. The double bond wins, so number from the right: the double bond is C1–C2 and the methyl lands on C4. Name: 4-methyl-1-pentene (4-methylpent-1-ene).

Check

"2-Methyl-4-pentene" gives the branch the low number and the double bond the high one, which breaks the first rule of alkene naming. Formula check: C6H12 fits CnH2n for an alkene with six carbons (five in the chain plus the methyl). ✓
