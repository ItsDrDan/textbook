Lecture Connection

The lecture introduces carboxylic acids, esters and (next chapter) amides as one family built on the acyl group, R–C(=O)–. Naming an acid is the easiest nomenclature in the course, because the –COOH carbon is always C1 and always wins the suffix; the work is in the prefixes, which now include hydroxy-, alkoxy- and oxo-.

The acyl group family

Every compound in this chapter, and the amides of Chapter 7, is built on the same two-atom unit: a carbon double-bonded to an oxygen with one more bond available. This unit, R–C(=O)–, is called an acyl group. What is attached to the acyl carbon decides the family. An –OH gives a carboxylic acid, an –OR′ gives an ester, and an –NH2 (or –NHR, –NR2) gives an amide. (An acyl group bonded to a chlorine is an acid chloride, a reactive lab reagent you will not need to name.) The acid, ester and amide are often called carboxylic acid derivatives because an acid can be converted into each of the others by swapping the group on the acyl carbon.

Condensed formulas. A carboxylic acid's carboxyl group is abbreviated RCOOH or RCO2H (CH3CH2COOH is propanoic acid). In the abbreviation, the first O after the C is the C=O and the second is single-bonded. A carboxylic acid is always terminal, at the end of a chain, so it cannot be cyclic and never needs a position number. It follows that carboxylic acids have skeletal isomers (butanoic acid and 2-methylpropanoic acid) but no positional isomers; an acid and an ester with the same formula are functional group isomers (Section 6.5).

Two more words you will hear in the metabolism chapters: the carbon next to the –COOH group is the α (alpha) carbon, and the carbon bonded to the α carbon is the β (beta) carbon (β-oxidation, β-hydroxybutyrate).

In this course

The section text names substituted acids two ways: common names with Greek letters (γ-chlorobutyric acid) and IUPAC names with numbers (4-chlorobutanoic acid). On exams, use the IUPAC name with numbers. Keep the Greek letters only as the labels α and β for the first two carbons after the carboxyl, which is how the biochemistry chapters use them.

Naming carboxylic acids

  1. Find the longest chain containing the –COOH group.
  2. –COOH is the highest-priority functional group of all, and it is always C1, so omit the 1 from the name.
  3. Change the -e of the parent alkane to -oic acid.
  4. Apply the other nomenclature rules to build the prefix: CH3CH(CH3)CH(CH3)CH2CH2COOH is 4,5-dimethylhexanoic acid.

Common names to know: formic acid (methanoic; the source's ant acid), acetic acid (ethanoic; vinegar) and benzoic acid. A carboxyl group on a benzene ring is benzoic acid; the ring carbon attached to the carboxyl carbon is C1, and the other aromatic nomenclature rules apply: 2-bromobenzoic acid.

Substituents and benzoic acid derivatives

Because carboxylic acids have the highest naming priority, every other group appears as a prefix, with the same prefixes you used for aldehydes and ketones: a ketone C=O becomes oxo- and an –OH becomes hydroxy-. CH3COCH2COOH is 3-oxobutanoic acid (acetoacetic acid, a ketone body you will meet in Chapter 15). CH3CH(OH)COOH is 2-hydroxypropanoic acid (lactic acid). A short checklist for naming a substituted acid:

  1. Find the longest chain that contains the COOH carbon; that carbon is always C1, so no position number is written for the acid itself.
  2. Name each substituent with its prefix and locant: chloro-, bromo-, fluoro- (halogens); methyl-, ethyl- (alkyl); hydroxy- (–OH); methoxy-, ethoxy- (–OR, an alkoxy group); oxo- (a C=O inside the chain).
  3. List the prefixes alphabetically (ignore di-, tri-), then the chain name ending in -oic acid.

Examples: CH3CH(OCH3)COOH is 2-methoxypropanoic acid; CH3CHBrCH2CH2COOH is 4-bromopentanoic acid; CH3COCH(CH3)COOH is 2-methyl-3-oxobutanoic acid. Ring acids are named as derivatives of benzoic acid, with the ring carbon carrying the COOH as C1 and the lowest possible numbers for the other groups; ortho/meta/para are still used in common names. 2-Hydroxybenzoic acid is salicylic acid (the parent of aspirin), and 4-aminobenzoic acid is PABA (released when benzocaine is hydrolyzed, Section 6.8).

Acids you will meet by their common names

Several small carboxylic acids have common names that you will hear far more often than their IUPAC names, in the kitchen, the clinic and the metabolism chapters. The table lists the ones to know and where each turns up in the body. At body pH (about 7.4) each exists mainly as its carboxylate ion (Section 6.10), which is why clinical reports say lactate, citrate and pyruvate rather than the acid names.
