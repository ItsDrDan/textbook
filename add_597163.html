Lecture Connection

Denaturation and hydrolysis are the two ways to ruin a protein, and the exam always asks you to tell them apart: denaturation unfolds (secondary, tertiary, quaternary lost; peptide bonds intact); hydrolysis breaks the peptide bonds (primary structure lost). To predict what an agent does, ask which interactions it touches.

Match the agent to the interaction it breaks

Protein function depends on shape, and shape depends on a web of weak interactions. When the chain unfolds, buried hydrophobic groups come to the surface, and unfolded chains tangle and precipitate: a cooked egg white turns from a clear solution of albumin into a white solid. Denaturation can be reversible for small proteins if the agent is removed gently, but for most proteins (and for anything cooked) it is permanent. In this course

The section text describes heavy metals as attacking –SH groups and "making salt bridges" with them, and lists heating "above 50 °C." On exams, say that heavy-metal ions disrupt disulfide bonds and salt bridges (they bind sulfur and carboxylate groups), that heat and alcohol disrupt hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, that acids and bases disrupt salt bridges and hydrogen bonds, and that reducing agents break only disulfide bonds. No denaturing agent breaks a peptide bond.

Professor Tip

To predict what a denaturing agent does, ask one question: which interactions does it touch? Then list the levels those interactions hold. Reducing agents touch only disulfide bonds, so they unfold keratin (lots of Cys) but barely affect a protein with no cysteine. Nothing on the denaturation list breaks peptide bonds, so the amino acid sequence is always intact afterward.

Hydrolysis is different

Hydrolysis is the reverse of peptide-bond formation: water is added across the amide link, with strong acid and hours of heating in the lab or with protease enzymes in the body (pepsin in the stomach, trypsin and chymotrypsin from the pancreas). Hydrolysis destroys the primary structure and releases free amino acids, which is exactly what digestion is for: dietary protein is hydrolyzed to amino acids, absorbed, and reassembled into the body's own proteins. It is also why a protein drug taken by mouth stops being a drug.

Misfolding

A protein can also lose function by folding into the wrong stable shape. The section text describes amyloid plaques and prions; the chemistry to remember is that the misfolded form is rich in sticky β-sheet, it converts normal copies into the same shape, and the aggregates survive the heat of ordinary sterilization. That last point is why instruments used on a patient with Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease need special handling: a standard autoclave denatures ordinary proteins but does not reliably destroy prions.

Worked example

Example 1: Predict the effect of a denaturing agent

Problem. A 70% isopropyl alcohol swab is wiped across skin carrying bacteria. (a) Which interactions in the bacterial enzymes does the alcohol disrupt, and which levels of structure are lost? (b) Are the enzymes' amino acid sequences changed? (c) Would the swab have the same effect on a protein with no cysteine as a hair-relaxer reducing agent would?

Solution.

(a) Alcohol is a small polar molecule that hydrogen-bonds and also dissolves nonpolar groups. It disrupts the hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions that hold the fold, so secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure are lost. The enzymes unfold and stop working, and the cell dies.

(b) No. Alcohol does not hydrolyze amide bonds, so the primary structure is intact; the protein is denatured, not digested.

(c) Not at all. A reducing agent such as thioglycolate attacks only disulfide bonds. On a cysteine-free protein it would do almost nothing, whereas alcohol attacks hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions that every protein has.

Check. Match agent to interaction: heat, alcohol and agitation hit hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic packing; acids, bases and heavy metals hit salt bridges (and metals hit disulfides); reducing agents hit disulfides only. Nothing on that list touches peptide bonds.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Denaturation is the chemistry behind infection control. An autoclave (steam at 121 °C) and a 70% alcohol swab kill microbes by unfolding their enzymes, and heavy-metal antiseptics such as silver nitrate do the same by binding sulfur. The same vulnerability is why a high fever is dangerous: human enzymes begin to unfold above about 41 °C, and Chapter 11 shows how temperature and pH change enzyme activity. Insulin is a protein drug: it is injected, not swallowed, because stomach acid denatures it and pepsin then hydrolyzes its peptide bonds. Vials of protein drugs are rolled, never shaken, because agitation at the air–liquid surface denatures them.
