Lecture Connection

This chapter introduces four new functional groups at once: alcohols, phenols, ethers and thiols. In lecture they are grouped together for one reason: in all four, the oxygen or sulfur is single-bonded to carbon. If you see a C=O, you are looking at a different family (Chapters 5 and 6).

The four groups and their name endings

The table lists the four groups of this chapter with the general form, the name ending you will use, and one example. Learn the endings as vocabulary; they are how you recognize the family in a name.

GroupGeneral formName endingExample
AlcoholR–OH (OH on an sp3 carbon)-olCH3CH2OH, ethanol
PhenolOH directly on a benzene ringphenol (the parent name)C6H5OH
EtherR–O–R′"alkyl alkyl ether" (common); -oxy prefix (IUPAC)CH3CH2OCH2CH3, diethyl ether
ThiolR–SH-thiolCH3SH, methanethiol

The lecture's one-line summary: -ol = alcohol suffix, -thiol = thiol suffix, phenol = the parent name for an OH on a benzene ring, and -oxy = the ether prefix. The source table above also lists the aldehyde (-al), ketone (-one) and carboxylic acid (-oic acid) endings; those three all contain a C=O and are the subject of the next two chapters.

Sorting a molecule into the right family

Before you name anything, decide which family it belongs to. Ask two questions about the oxygen or sulfur:

  1. Is it single-bonded to carbon? If it is double-bonded (C=O), the molecule is not in this chapter.
  2. What is on the other side of the O or S? An H on an O that sits on a chain carbon makes an alcohol; an H on an O that sits directly on a benzene ring makes a phenol; a second carbon on the O makes an ether; an H on an S makes a thiol.

Practice (lecture style): a molecule contains a benzene ring with a CH2OH group hanging off it. Is it an alcohol, an ether, a hydroxide or a phenol? It is an alcohol: the OH is on a CH2 carbon, not on a ring carbon. "Hydroxide" is never the answer for an organic molecule; OH− is an ion, while the OH in an alcohol is a covalently bonded hydroxyl group.

Professor Tip

Many real molecules carry more than one of these groups. Menthol (an alcohol on a cyclohexane ring), thymol (a phenol) and the triclosan once used in toothpaste (a phenol and an ether) are all built from this chapter's groups. When a molecule has several groups, name each one you see; Sections 4.2, 4.7, 4.8 and 4.9 give the rules for each.
