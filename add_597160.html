Lecture Connection

Secondary structure is about the backbone, not the side chains. Both the α-helix and the β-pleated sheet are held by hydrogen bonds between a backbone C=O and a backbone N–H; the R groups just point out of the way. If a question mentions R groups, it is asking about tertiary structure, not secondary.

Helix, sheet, and what holds them

  • In an α-helix the chain coils like a spiral staircase, with each backbone C=O hydrogen-bonded to the N–H four residues further along; the R groups point outward. In a β-pleated sheet stretches of chain lie side by side, zig-zagging, with hydrogen bonds between the C=O of one strand and the N–H of the neighbor.
  • Both are held by backbone hydrogen bonds. When the N–H and C=O are on the same chain these are intramolecular hydrogen bonds (intra = within one molecule; inter would mean between different molecules). The R groups are not involved; which arrangement forms depends on which one lets the backbone hydrogen-bond best around the side chains it carries.
  • Keratin in hair is mostly α-helix; silk is mostly β-sheet; most globular proteins contain both, joined by the "random coil" stretches the section text describes.
  • The triple helix of collagen is the third kind of secondary structure you need: three chains wind around each other, each a repeat of Gly-X-Y with lots of proline and hydroxyproline.

In this course

The section text describes α-helix hydrogen bonds as "intrachain" and β-sheet hydrogen bonds as "interchain." On exams, the answer to "what holds secondary structure together" is always hydrogen bonds between backbone C=O and N–H groups. Call them intramolecular when the two strands belong to the same chain (a helix, or a sheet that folds back on itself), intermolecular only when two separate chains line up, as in silk.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Collagen, a triple-helix protein, makes up the periodontal ligament, gingival connective tissue, and the organic matrix of dentin and bone. Vitamin C is needed by the enzyme that converts proline residues in collagen to hydroxyproline, and the extra –OH groups are what let the three chains hydrogen-bond into a stable triple helix. Without vitamin C the helix is weak and the tissue falls apart: deficiency (scurvy) shows up first in the mouth as bleeding, swollen gums and loose teeth, because gingival collagen turns over quickly. Hair gets its shape from a different secondary structure: the α-helices of keratin, cross-linked by disulfide bonds (Section 10.8).
