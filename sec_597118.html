Lecture Connection

This section is a general treatment of acid–base chemistry written for organic chemistry students. The lecture uses only a slice of it: what pKa means, where carboxylic acids sit relative to phenols and alcohols, and the rule that tells you whether a drug or metabolite is ionized at blood pH. That rule is the one piece of this section a nurse uses every shift.

In this course

You are not responsible for Ka, Kb or pKb calculations, the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, or the electronegativity, size, resonance and induction arguments the section uses to rank acids. You are responsible for: (1) the definition of pKa as a measure of acid strength, lower = stronger; (2) the approximate pKa order carboxylic acid (about 4–5) < phenol (about 10) < alcohol (about 16), with strong mineral acids such as HCl below 0; and (3) the rule that at a pH well above its pKa an acid exists as its conjugate base. The source's pKa table (acetic acid 4.76, ethanol 15.9) matches the values used in lecture.

pKa: how weak is weak?

Carboxylic acids are weak acids: in water only a small fraction of the molecules give up their proton at any instant, so the ionization is written with equilibrium arrows (Section 6.4). Their strength is described by the acid dissociation constant, usually quoted as the pKa (the lower the pKa, the stronger the acid). Most simple carboxylic acids have pKa values of about 4–5 (acetic acid 4.8, benzoic acid 4.2, lactic acid 3.9). For comparison, phenol has a pKa near 10 and an alcohol near 16, while HCl is a strong acid and ionizes completely. So carboxylic acids are the most acidic organic group you have met, but still far weaker than the mineral acids. Section 4.10 built the alcohol–phenol end of this scale; this section adds the carboxylic acids at the strong end.

Ionized or not? Compare pH with pKa

The pKa tells you the pH at which an acid is half ionized. At any pH more than about two units above the pKa, essentially all of the molecules have lost their proton. Blood is at pH 7.4, roughly three units above the pKa of a typical carboxylic acid, so more than 99% of lactic acid, pyruvic acid, citric acid, aspirin and ibuprofen in the blood exist as the carboxylate ion, RCOO−. That is why these appear on lab reports as lactate, pyruvate, citrate and salicylate. Run the comparison the other way in the stomach (pH about 1–2, well below pKa 4–5) and the same molecules are un-ionized, neutral acids, which is the form that crosses membranes.

Professor Tip

You do not need the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation to answer "ionized or not." Subtract: pH minus pKa. Positive by two or more means the acid has lost its proton (carboxylate, RCOO−); negative by two or more means it has kept it (RCOOH); close to zero means a roughly even mixture. The same arithmetic works for the amines of Chapter 7 and the amino acids of Chapter 10.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Salicylate overdose. After aspirin is hydrolyzed (Section 6.8), the circulating salicylate is a carboxylic acid at a pH three units above its pKa, so it is ionized. In a large overdose, salicylate does two things you can now explain. First, it is an acid, and it also disrupts the cell's energy metabolism so that lactic acid and ketone acids build up; the result is metabolic acidosis, a blood pH below 7.35 caused by excess acid. Second, because a carboxylic acid is ionized at high pH, making the urine alkaline with sodium bicarbonate traps salicylate in the kidney tubule as the charged carboxylate, which cannot diffuse back into the blood, so it is excreted faster. Lactic acidosis. The same ionization logic explains lactic acidosis in sepsis and shock: poorly oxygenated tissue makes lactic acid (pKa 3.9), which gives up its proton at blood pH, and the rising lactate level is what the lab reports while the released H+ lowers the blood pH.
