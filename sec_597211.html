Lecture Connection

The lecture mentions lipoproteins only where the digestion story needs them: chylomicrons carry the triacylglycerol rebuilt in the intestinal wall, and a lipase at the tissues takes it apart again. The five classes above are background for the lipid panel you will read as a health professional, not a list to memorize for the exam.

In this course

Know three things: chylomicrons carry dietary fat from the intestine through the lymph to the blood; VLDL carries fat made in the liver (lipogenesis, Section 15.8) out to adipose tissue; and lipoprotein lipase, a hydrolase on the capillary walls of muscle and adipose tissue, hydrolyzes the ester bonds of the triacylglycerol in both so that free fatty acids can enter the cells to be burned or stored. The density ranges, IDL, and the detailed cholesterol traffic of LDL and HDL are not assessed.

Delivery and storage

At capillaries in muscle and adipose tissue, lipoprotein lipase hydrolyzes chylomicron and VLDL triacylglycerol, the same ester hydrolysis pancreatic lipase performed in the gut (Section 15.1), only now the products go into cells instead of across the intestinal wall. Muscle burns the fatty acids by β-oxidation (Section 15.3); adipocytes re-esterify them with glycerol 3-phosphate and park the triacylglycerol in their single large droplet (Section 15.6). Between meals the traffic reverses: hormone-sensitive lipase inside the adipocyte hydrolyzes the stored fat, and the fatty acids leave bound to albumin, not in a lipoprotein, because free fatty acids are too insoluble to travel alone.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Reading a lipid panel. A fasting lipid panel reports total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, and triglycerides (the clinical word for triacylglycerols). The “fasting” matters because of this section’s chemistry: after a meal the blood is full of chylomicrons, so the triglyceride number would reflect breakfast rather than the patient. High LDL and low HDL together are the pattern associated with hyperlipidemia and atherosclerosis (cholesterol-rich plaque in artery walls); high triglycerides are often a sign of excess carbohydrate being turned into fat by lipogenesis, or of poorly controlled diabetes, in which insulin is not switching lipoprotein lipase on. Statins lower LDL by inhibiting the liver’s cholesterol synthesis, a pathway that, like fatty acid synthesis, starts from acetyl CoA (Section 15.4); omega-3 fatty acids (EPA and DHA, Chapter 9) mainly lower triglycerides. A nurse who understands which particle carries which lipid can explain why the diet advice for high triglycerides (less sugar and alcohol) differs from the advice for high LDL (less saturated and trans fat).
