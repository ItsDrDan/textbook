Protein misfolding and associated diseases

Newly synthesized proteins fold in a specific way, i.e., into secondary, tertiary, and quaternary structures, to be able to perform their function, as illustrated in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\). Some proteins can fold in only one way, but others can fold in multiple ways. There are proteins in the cell, called chaperones, that help newly formed proteins to fold in the way needed for their function.

Sometimes normal proteins misfold and become pathological. Often, these proteins are in soluble \(\alpha\) helix forms that reassemble into \(\beta\)-pleated sheet forms that are sticky and aggregate into plaques or amyloid structures, as illustrated in Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\), with the example of plaque formation in Alzheimer's affected brain.

Prions are small proteins found in nerve tissue. Their exact functions are unknown, but when they misfold, they can cause more normal proteins to misfold. This protein misfolding is related to diseases such as mad cow disease in cows, Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease in humans, Alzheimer's disease, and familial amyloid cardiomyopathy or polyneuropathy, as well as intracellular aggregation diseases such as Huntington's and Parkinson's disease.

Denaturation of proteins

Proteins usually keep their primary, secondary, tertiary, and quaternary structures under physiological conditions. However, some physical processes and chemical agents can break the interactions, i.e., hydrogen bonding, disulfide linkages, salt bridges, and hydrophobic interactions.

Denaturation

Loss of the secondary, tertiary, and quaternary structures of proteins by a physical process or a chemical agent while maintaining the primary structure almost intact is called denaturation of proteins.

Proteins unfold and become almost linear polypeptide chains upon denaturation. Denatured proteins cannot perform their functions. Denaturation can be caused by heat, acids or bases, organic compounds and solvents, heavy metal ions, and agitation, as explained below.

  • Heating above 50 oC disrupts hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, causing protein denaturation. For example, cooking food and sterilizing surgical instruments by autoclave treatment. In laser surgery, laser, i.e., the light of a single wavelength, is focused on a spot, causing heat that denatures proteins. Heating by laser cauterizes incisions, i.e., burns the site or the wound. It helps prevent blood loss.
  • Acids and bases disrupt hydrogen bonding and salt bridges, e.g., by neutralizing some ions involved in the salt bridges.
  • Organic compounds like urea disrupt hydrogen bonding by replacing them with stronger hydrogen bonding with the compound, and organic solvents disrupt hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions. For example, 70% alcohol disinfects skin by denaturing and coagulating the proteins of microbes (it kills most microbes but does not sterilize).
  • Heavy-metal ions like \(\ce{Pb^{2+}}\) and \(\ce{Hg^{2+}}\) bind to –SH groups, breaking disulfide bonds, and to carboxylate groups, disrupting salt bridges (for example –S− Pb2+ −S–). Egg white or milk was once given after heavy-metal poisoning because its proteins bind the metal ions in the stomach. Today the advice for any suspected poisoning is to call Poison Control (1-800-222-1222) or 911; vomiting should not be induced.
  • Agitation physically disrupts hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions. Whipped cream and whipped egg white are prepared based on the agitation process.

Valley Connection: When Summer Heat Unfolds Proteins

Valley summers are long and hot, and many families work outside picking crops, packing fruit, or building roads. A CDC study found that from 1992 to 2006, U.S. crop workers died from heat at a rate nearly 20 times higher than all U.S. civilian workers (0.39 vs. 0.02 deaths per 100,000 workers).4

The chemistry. A protein’s folded shape is held together mostly by weak forces: hydrogen bonds, ionic attractions (salt bridges), and hydrophobic interactions. Heat gives molecules enough energy to break these forces, so the protein unfolds, or denatures, even though its peptide bonds stay intact. A denatured enzyme can no longer do its job. Heat stroke is defined by a core body temperature above 40 °C (104 °F) along with brain dysfunction, and one of the earliest effects inside cells is protein denaturation and clumping (aggregation).3 Cells fight back with heat shock proteins such as HSP70, chaperones that help refold damaged proteins. If heat overwhelms them, misfolded proteins pile up, cell membranes become leaky, and organs such as the liver, kidneys, and brain can fail.3

The law. California’s outdoor heat illness standard (Title 8, §3395) requires employers to provide free, fresh water—one quart per worker per hour for the whole shift—and shade whenever it is hotter than 80 °F. Workers may take a cool-down rest in the shade whenever they feel they need one. At 95 °F or above, employers must start high-heat procedures: keeping workers in contact with a supervisor, watching for signs of heat illness, holding pre-shift meetings, and reminding workers to drink water. Farmworkers also get a 10-minute cool-down rest every two hours. New workers in high heat must be watched closely for their first 14 days, because the body needs about 4 to 14 days to adjust to heat.1

In the field and the ER. EMTs and nurses must spot heat stroke fast: confusion, slurred speech, hot dry skin or heavy sweating, and seizures. Body temperature can reach 106 °F or higher within 10 to 15 minutes. Call 911 and cool the person quickly with a cold-water or ice bath, cold wet cloths, or ice packs on the head, neck, armpits, and groin.2 Every minute counts, because proteins keep denaturing as long as the body stays that hot.

Think about it: When you cook an egg, the clear egg white turns solid white and never goes back. How is this like what happens in cells during heat stroke, and how are living cells different from the egg?

Answer
In both cases heat breaks the hydrogen bonds and other weak interactions holding proteins in their folded shape, so the proteins denature and clump together (aggregate). The egg has no way to repair its proteins, so the change is permanent. Living cells make heat shock proteins (chaperones) that can help refold some damaged proteins, which is why fast cooling can save a heat stroke patient. If the heat lasts too long, the damage overwhelms the chaperones and cells die.
