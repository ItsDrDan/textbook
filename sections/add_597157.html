Lecture Connection

A peptide bond is an amide bond with a new name. Everything you learned about amides in Chapter 7 carries over: it forms by condensation (lose water), it breaks by hydrolysis (add water), and it is planar and stiff. The lecture skills are to draw a peptide from its name, name it from its structure, and count its peptide bonds.

Condensation builds it, hydrolysis breaks it

The carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amino group of the next lose a molecule of water, a condensation reaction, and the C(=O)–NH link that results is an amide bond. Between amino acids it gets a special name: the peptide bond. Because the peptide bond is an amide, it is planar, it does not rotate freely, and it is stable in water at body temperature; breaking it requires hydrolysis (adding the water back) with strong acid and heat in the lab, or a digestive enzyme such as pepsin or trypsin in the body.

Notice what is left over at the two ends of a peptide. One end still has a free –NH3+: the N-terminus. The other still has a free –COO−: the C-terminus. Everything in between has had both groups used up in peptide bonds, so a peptide with n amino acids contains n − 1 peptide bonds. Each amino acid unit inside a chain is called a residue.

Naming and writing peptides

By convention, peptides are always written and named starting from the N-terminus on the left and ending at the C-terminus on the right. The rules:

  • Every residue except the last (C-terminal) one is named with its ending changed to -yl, the same ending used for acyl groups: glycine → glycyl, alanine → alanyl, serine → seryl, cysteine → cysteinyl, aspartate → aspartyl, glutamine → glutaminyl. Tryptophan becomes tryptophyl; the C-terminal residue keeps its full name.
  • Run the names together as one word: Gly-Ala is glycylalanine; Ala-Gly is alanylglycine, a different compound (different N-terminus, different C-terminus).
  • The three-letter form uses hyphens (Ser-Gly-Val); the one-letter form just strings the letters (SGV). Ser-Gly-Val is seryl-glycyl-valine, written as one word: serylglycylvaline.
  • Dashes mean the order is known: Ser-Gly-Val is one specific tripeptide, and the dashes stand for the peptide bonds. Commas mean only the composition is known: "Ser, Gly, Val" is a tripeptide made of those three residues in some unknown order.

Order matters because the two ends are chemically different, so Gly-Ala and Ala-Gly are constitutional isomers. With three different amino acids there are 3 × 2 × 1 = 6 possible tripeptides, which is why the reversed bradykinin in the section text is a different molecule with none of the activity.

Professor Tip

To draw any peptide, draw the backbone first and add the side chains last. Write N–C–C(=O) once for every residue, N-terminus on the left: N–C–CO–N–C–CO–N–C–CO for a tripeptide. Put –NH3+ on the first N, –O− on the last carbonyl carbon, and an H on each middle N. Then go back to each α-carbon and hang the correct R group on it (alternating above and below the chain keeps the drawing tidy). Finish by checking the side-chain charges for the pH: Asp and Glu carry –COO− and Lys and Arg carry a positive N at pH 7.4. Example 1 below does this step by step; the Peptide Builder game lets you practice it with instant feedback.

Worked example

Example 1: Draw a tripeptide at pH 7

Problem. Draw the tripeptide Gly-Asp-Ala as it exists at pH 7.4, name it, give its one-letter form, and state its net charge.

Solution. Follow the Professor Tip above.

  1. Backbone. Three residues, so write N–C–CO three times, N-terminus on the left: N–C–CO–N–C–CO–N–C–CO. Give the first N three hydrogens and a positive charge (–NH3+), the last carbonyl carbon an –O−, and each middle N one H. The two C(=O)–NH links are the peptide bonds.
  2. Side chains. Hang the R group on each α-carbon in order: glycine gets –H, aspartate gets –CH2COO−, alanine gets –CH3.
  3. Charges for the pH. At 7.4 the N-terminus is –NH3+ (+1), the C-terminus is –COO− (−1), and the aspartate side chain, an acidic R group with pI near 3, is also –COO− (−1).

Name: change every residue but the last to -yl: glycylaspartylalanine. One-letter form: GDA. Net charge: +1 − 1 − 1 = −1.

Check. Three residues, two peptide bonds, and one free amine plus one free carboxylate at the ends. If the middle residue had been lysine instead of aspartate, the side chain would carry –NH3+ and the net charge would be +1; a middle serine would leave it at 0.
