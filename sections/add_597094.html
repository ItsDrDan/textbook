Lecture Connection

Alcohols do three things in this course: they burn (combustion), they lose water (dehydration) and they lose hydrogen (oxidation). For dehydration you need Zaitsev's rule; for oxidation you need the 1°/2°/3° classification from Section 4.2. Classify first, predict second.

Combustion

Like hydrocarbons, alcohols burn in oxygen to give carbon dioxide and water: C2H5OH + 3 O2 → 2 CO2 + 3 H2O. Balance it the same way you balanced hydrocarbon combustion in Chapter 2, remembering that the alcohol already contributes one oxygen atom. Ethanol burns with a clean, nearly invisible flame, which is why it is used in fondue burners and lab alcohol lamps.

Example: Combustion of an alcohol

Problem. Write and balance the equation for the complete combustion of 2-propanol, C3H7OH.

Solution. Products are always CO2 and H2O. Balance C first (3 CO2), then H (8 H → 4 H2O), then O. The right side has 6 + 4 = 10 O atoms; the alcohol supplies 1, so O2 must supply 9, which is 4½ O2. Double everything to clear the fraction:

2 C3H7OH + 9 O2 → 6 CO2 + 8 H2O

Check. C: 6 = 6. H: 16 = 16. O: 2 + 18 = 20 on the left; 12 + 8 = 20 on the right. The one difference from hydrocarbon combustion is the oxygen atom already in the alcohol; forgetting it is the most common error.

Dehydration: an elimination, with Zaitsev's rule

With a strong acid catalyst and heat, water is eliminated from an alcohol to form an alkene. The OH leaves from one carbon and an H leaves from a neighboring carbon; the double bond forms between them. You already know the reverse reaction: in hydration (Section 4.4 and the lab), water adds to an alkene to form an alcohol. Dehydration is an elimination, hydration is an addition, and the two are related the same way oxidation and reduction are.

Symmetric vs. asymmetric alcohols. If the two carbons next to the OH carbon are identical (3-pentanol, cyclohexanol), only one alkene is possible. If they are different, two alkenes can form, and you need Zaitsev's rule (also spelled Saytzeff): the alkene formed in the greatest amount is the one made by removing the hydrogen from the neighboring carbon that has the fewest hydrogens. Put another way, the major product has the most alkyl groups bonded to the C=C: the more substituted alkene wins. Notice that this is the opposite of Markovnikov's rule for addition, where the H goes to the carbon that already has more hydrogens. Count the alkyl groups on each possible C=C to decide.

Example: Zaitsev's rule with two possible alkenes

Problem. Predict the major and minor products when 2-butanol, CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3, is dehydrated with H2SO4 and heat.

Solution. The OH is on C2. Its neighbors are C1 (a CH3, three H atoms) and C3 (a CH2, two H atoms). Removing an H from C3 gives CH3CH=CHCH3, 2-butene, whose double bond carries two alkyl groups. Removing an H from C1 gives CH2=CHCH2CH3, 1-butene, whose double bond carries only one alkyl group. Zaitsev's rule: 2-butene is major, 1-butene is minor.

Check. Both products are C4H8, and each is 2-butanol (C4H10O) minus H2O. Notice that C3, the neighbor with fewer hydrogens, gives the major product, exactly as Zaitsev's rule predicts; counting the alkyl groups on each possible C=C gives the same answer.

Professor Tip

When an exam says "dehydration of an alcohol," write the alkene (plus water) and apply Zaitsev’s rule when two alkenes are possible. The enzyme-catalyzed dehydration of 2-phosphoglycerate to phosphoenolpyruvate returns in Chapter 13 as a step of glycolysis.

Oxidation: the class of the alcohol decides

In organic chemistry an oxidation is an increase in the number of C–O bonds and/or a decrease in the number of C–H bonds. The symbol [O] over the arrow stands for any oxidizing agent, such as potassium dichromate, K2Cr2O7. The alcohol loses the H from its O–H and an H from the OH carbon, and a C=O forms, so the class of the alcohol decides the outcome:

  • Primary alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid. The aldehyde still has an H on its C=O carbon, so with excess [O] it is oxidized a second time.
  • Secondary alcohol → ketone. The ketone carbon has no H left, so the reaction stops there.
  • Tertiary alcohol → no reaction. There is no H bonded to the C–OH carbon to remove.

Reduction, written [H], is the reverse: an aldehyde goes back to a 1° alcohol and a ketone back to a 2° alcohol (Chapter 5). Oxidation/reduction and elimination/addition are the two reversible pairs you should be able to run in either direction.

In the Lab

Chromate oxidation test (alcohols and phenols experiment): orange chromium(VI) (dichromate, Cr2O72−, in the acidic reagent) turns green (Cr3+) when it oxidizes a 1° or 2° alcohol. A 3° alcohol cannot be oxidized, so the solution stays orange. The balanced equation in the section text is this same color change written out: dichromate is the orange reactant, Cr3+ the green product.

Professor Tip

Read the product first, then pick the reagent. A new C=O in place of a C–OH means [O]. A new C=C and a lost water means H+ and heat. CO2 and H2O mean O2 and a flame. The lecture's "what reactants lead to each product?" practice is exactly this matching exercise.

The body runs the same oxidation

Clinical Connection: Nursing

One enzyme, three poisons. Ethanol is absorbed in the stomach and small intestine, and in the liver the enzymes alcohol dehydrogenase and aldehyde dehydrogenase act as the oxidizing agents. (Older breath-alcohol testers ran the same oxidation chemically, using the orange-to-green color change of an acidic dichromate reagent. Most devices used today measure breath alcohol with a fuel cell, which also oxidizes the ethanol, or with infrared light.) Alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) treats ethanol, methanol and ethylene glycol as the same kind of molecule: a 1° alcohol to be oxidized to an aldehyde and then an acid. Ethanol's products (acetaldehyde, acetic acid) are handled routinely. Methanol's (formaldehyde, formic acid) damage the optic nerve, which is why Section 4.4 warns that as little as 10 mL can cause blindness. Ethylene glycol's (ending in oxalic acid) cause metabolic acidosis (blood pH below normal) and calcium oxalate crystals in the kidneys (Section 4.6). The antidote concept follows from the chemistry: give ADH something else to work on. Fomepizole blocks the enzyme, and ethanol itself is a competitive substrate that keeps ADH busy while the unchanged methanol or glycol is cleared. (Concept only; dosing belongs to pharmacology.) Rubbing alcohol is different. Isopropyl alcohol is a 2° alcohol, so ADH oxidizes it only as far as a ketone, acetone, and the oxidation stops there. A person who swallows rubbing alcohol can have acetone on the breath and ketones in the blood, but usually without the acidosis seen with methanol or ethylene glycol, because no acid is formed.

Answers

“Think about it: Methanol (CH3OH) is also a primary…”

Methanol is oxidized to the aldehyde formaldehyde (HCHO, methanal) and then to the carboxylic acid formic acid (HCOOH, methanoic acid). Each step is an oxidation (more C–O bonds, fewer C–H bonds), just as ethanol goes to acetaldehyde and then acetic acid/acetate.
