Lecture Connection

In lecture you must know three hexoses cold: D-glucose, D-galactose, and D-fructose, each as a Fischer projection you can draw from a four-word pattern. The rest of this section is the nursing and dental context that makes those three sugars worth memorizing.

The three monosaccharides to know

D-Glucose (blood sugar, dextrose) is the most abundant monosaccharide; the body keeps its blood level in a narrow range (below), and excess is stored as glycogen or converted to fat. D-Galactose is half of the milk sugar lactose; it is part of brain and nerve tissue (so it is sometimes called brain sugar) and of the carbohydrates that define blood types. In galactosemia an enzyme needed to use galactose is missing, galactose accumulates, and untreated infants develop cataracts and liver damage. D-Fructose (fruit sugar) is the ketohexose in honey and fruit and the other half of sucrose; it is the sweetest common sugar, almost twice as sweet as table sugar with the same calories per gram. High-fructose corn syrup is made from corn starch: the starch is hydrolyzed to glucose and an enzyme then converts part of the glucose to fructose.

Professor Tip

Say the glucose pattern out loud as "right, left, right, right" for the OH groups on C2, C3, C4, C5. Galactose is "right, left, left, right" (C4 flipped). Mannose is "left, left, right, right" (C2 flipped). Fructose has the same C3–C5 pattern as glucose ("left, right, right") but with a ketone at C2 instead of an aldehyde at C1. In Sweet Structures (Practice Games, Chapter 8) you can build each one from these patterns.

Glucose in the blood: the numbers nurses use

Blood glucose is reported in the United States in milligrams per deciliter (mg/dL); 1 dL = 100 mL. A normal fasting level is about 70–99 mg/dL. Below about 70 mg/dL is hypoglycemia (low blood sugar); a fasting value of 126 mg/dL or higher on repeated tests is one of the criteria for diabetes mellitus, and a persistently high level is hyperglycemia. (Many other countries use mmol/L; 18 mg/dL = 1 mmol/L, so 90 mg/dL is 5.0 mmol/L. Check the unit before you interpret a value.) Glucose meters and test strips use the enzyme glucose oxidase, which oxidizes C1 of glucose to gluconic acid, the aldonic acid of Section 8.5; the strip measures the electrons released. It is reducing-sugar chemistry made specific for glucose.

A glucose tolerance test measures blood glucose for several hours after a measured dose of glucose. Insulin regulates the level by moving glucose into tissues and promoting glycogen formation; in diabetes too little insulin is made or it works poorly, so patients monitor their diet and glucose, and some must inject insulin.

Professor Tip

Any of the common "normal" ranges (70–99, 70–105, 70–110 mg/dL) is acceptable on an exam. What you must know is the unit (mg/dL), that about 70 mg/dL is the floor below which a patient is hypoglycemic, and that 126 mg/dL fasting is the diabetes criterion.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

"Dextrose" on an IV bag is D-glucose. D5W is 5% dextrose in water: 5 g of D-glucose per 100 mL (about 170 kcal/L), isotonic in the bag and a source of free water once the glucose is metabolized. When you read a point-of-care glucose, you are reading an enzyme-based oxidation of C1, an updated version of the reducing-sugar idea; the value is a snapshot, which is why it is paired with HbA1c (Section 8.4) for the long-term picture.

Carbohydrates on cell surfaces: blood types

Short chains of monosaccharides attached to membrane proteins and lipids act as cell-identity tags. The ABO blood types are the best-known example. Every red blood cell carries a short core chain of monosaccharides (the O antigen). Type A cells add one more sugar, an amino sugar (N-acetylgalactosamine); type B cells add a D-galactose instead; type AB cells carry both chains; type O carries only the core. The immune system makes antibodies against the sugar it lacks, so a transfusion of the wrong type causes the cells to clump (agglutinate), a potentially fatal reaction. In the United States type O is the most common blood type and type A the second. Only the last sugar on a short chain differs, which is why blood typing is, at bottom, carbohydrate chemistry.
