Lecture Connection

Ask the same three questions you asked for every family so far: can the molecules hydrogen bond with each other, can they hydrogen bond with water, and how long is the carbon chain? Carboxylic acids answer "yes, yes, and it depends," which puts them at the top of the boiling-point ranking and makes the small ones miscible with water.

Forces between carboxylic acid molecules

Can carboxylic acids hydrogen bond with other carboxylic acids? Yes: they have an H bonded to an O. They also have dipole–dipole forces (the C=O, C–O and O–H bonds are polar and do not cancel) and, like every molecule, dispersion forces. The carbon of the acyl group has three electron groups, so it is trigonal planar, like the carbonyl carbon of Chapter 5.

Why do acids beat alcohols? An alcohol has one O–H to donate. A carboxylic acid has an O–H and a C=O oxygen a short distance away, so two acid molecules can line up head-to-head and form two hydrogen bonds at once, making a dimer that behaves like a molecule of double the mass. That is the "strong hydrogen bonding" the section text refers to, and it gives carboxylic acids the highest boiling points of the groups studied so far: higher than alcohols of similar size. The section's table shows the other half of the trend: within the family, boiling point rises with molar mass (formic 100 °C, acetic 118 °C, propanoic 141 °C) because dispersion forces grow with the chain.

Boiling-point trend for similar molar masses: alkane < ester < aldehyde/ketone < alcohol < carboxylic acid (esters boil a little lower than aldehydes and ketones of similar size).

Example: Boiling point ranking

Problem. Acetic acid, 1-propanol and methyl formate all have a molar mass of 60 g/mol. Rank their boiling points.

Solution. Methyl formate (ester, no self hydrogen bonding, 32 °C) < 1-propanol (hydrogen bonding, 97 °C) < acetic acid (hydrogen-bonded dimers, 118 °C).

Check. Same mass, so only the IMFs differ: no O–H < one O–H < O–H plus C=O (dimer).

Solubility in water

Carboxylic acids can both donate hydrogen bonds to and accept them from water, so they are more soluble in water than alcohols, ethers, aldehydes or ketones of comparable mass. The chain still matters: about 5 carbons or fewer means water soluble (the section's table: four-carbon butyric acid is miscible, five-carbon valeric acid dissolves 5 g/100 g, six-carbon caproic acid only 1 g/100 g). Fatty acids, the long-chain carboxylic acids from animal fats and vegetable oils (Chapter 9), are at the insoluble extreme. Section 6.4 shows the way around that limit: convert the acid to its salt.

Professor Tip

The "goaty" odor of the C5–C10 acids in the section is a property you can predict: they are volatile enough to reach your nose (medium chain, modest dispersion forces) but too long to dissolve in the water of saliva, so they linger on surfaces and skin. Butanoic acid in rancid butter and the acids in sweat are the same chemistry. Esters, which cannot hydrogen bond to each other, are far more volatile and smell of fruit instead (Section 6.6).
