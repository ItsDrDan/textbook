Lecture Connection

Glycols and glycerol are the lecture's proof that intermolecular forces scale: each extra OH adds more hydrogen bonding, so these small molecules are thick, syrupy and completely water-soluble. They also carry a safety lesson that returns in nursing: it is the oxidation products of ethylene glycol, not the glycol itself, that do the damage.

More OH groups, stronger hydrogen bonding

A glycol is a diol with OH groups on adjacent carbons. The two you will meet most often are ethylene glycol (1,2-ethanediol, HOCH2CH2OH) and propylene glycol (1,2-propanediol). Glycerol (glycerin; 1,2,3-propanetriol, HOCH2CH(OH)CH2OH) is a triol. Compare the boiling points in a series of about the same size: ethanol 78 °C, ethylene glycol 198 °C, glycerol 290 °C. Each added O–H group raises the boiling point by roughly 90–120 °C, far more than the few grams per mole of added mass could explain; that jump is the signature of hydrogen bonding. The same forces make all three mix with water in any proportion (they are miscible) and make them viscous.

Those properties explain the uses in the section text: ethylene glycol as antifreeze and coolant (high boiling point, miscible with water), propylene glycol and glycerol as moisture-holding ingredients in foods, cosmetics and medicines. Glycerol is also the backbone of fats and oils, which you will meet in Chapter 9 as esters of glycerol.

Professor Tip

When a ranking question includes a diol or triol, put it at the top for boiling point and solubility, even if a competitor has a slightly larger molar mass. Count the O–H groups first, then the carbons.

Ethylene glycol toxicity is 1° alcohol oxidation

Ethylene glycol tastes sweet and is not very toxic by itself; the danger is what the body makes from it. The same liver enzyme that oxidizes ethanol, alcohol dehydrogenase (Section 4.5), oxidizes each 1° OH group of ethylene glycol through aldehydes to acids, ending in oxalic acid. Oxalate precipitates with calcium as the calcium oxalate crystals the section describes, and the acids cause metabolic acidosis (blood pH below normal). Propylene glycol, by contrast, is oxidized to lactic and pyruvic acids, both normal metabolites, which is why it is the glycol allowed in foods and medicines.

Polyols in the mouth and the pharmacy

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Sugar alcohols. Xylitol and sorbitol are polyols, molecules with an OH on nearly every carbon; they are glycerol's bigger relatives. They taste sweet, yet plaque bacteria cannot ferment them to acid, so they are noncariogenic (do not cause dental caries, tooth decay). That is why sugar-free gum is sweetened with them. Glycerin in toothpaste is a humectant, a polyol that holds water by hydrogen bonding so the paste does not dry out.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Glycerin (glycerol) is the water-holding base of suppositories and lubricating gels for the same reason it works in toothpaste: three O–H groups hold water by hydrogen bonding and keep the product soft. Propylene glycol is a common solvent for drugs that do not dissolve well in water alone, including some injectable and oral liquid medications; its low toxicity, explained above, is what makes that use acceptable.
