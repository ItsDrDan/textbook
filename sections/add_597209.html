Lecture Connection

This is stage 3 of fatty acid breakdown, the fatty acid spiral. The lecture wants two things from it: the four-question table for the four reactions (they follow the citric acid cycle pattern, oxidize, hydrate, oxidize, then a cleavage), and the arithmetic. For a chain with n carbons, slice it every two carbons: acetyl CoA = n/2, repetitions = n/2 − 1, and ATP = (acetyl CoA × 10) + (NADH × 2.5) + (FADH2 × 1.5) − 2 for activation. Glycerol, the other half of a fat, gets its own two steps at the end of this page.

Professor Tip

Count ATP from β-oxidation step by step on exams (2.5 per NADH, 1.5 per FADH2, 10 per acetyl CoA, minus 2 for activation); the formula 7n − 6 is a check, and both give 106 for palmitate and 120 for stearate. You are responsible for the reaction type, enzyme class and coenzyme of each step, not the mechanisms. The obesity, BMI and leptin material is background; ketone bodies are in Section 15.7.

β-Oxidation: the four reactions

β-Oxidation is a sequence of four reactions that repeatedly cleaves two-carbon units from the carboxyl end of the acyl CoA. The name comes from the β carbon, the second carbon from the carboxyl end (the α carbon is the first): it is the carbon that gets oxidized, from CH2 to C=O, before the bond between the α and β carbons is broken. “Pathway” is a better word than “cycle” here: it is a linear set of four reactions that repeats, and each repetition gives a different (shorter) product rather than regenerating the starting material. The first three reactions are the pattern you saw in the citric acid cycle (succinate → fumarate → malate → oxaloacetate): dehydrogenate to a C=C, hydrate it, dehydrogenate the new alcohol to a ketone; the fourth, thiolysis, is unique to β-oxidation. Practice: At which step of β-oxidation does each event occur? (a) A C–C single bond is converted to a C=C. (b) NAD+ is reduced to NADH. (c) A hydration reaction occurs. (d) An acetyl CoA molecule is produced.

Answer
(a) Step 1. (b) Step 3. (c) Step 2. (d) Step 4.

Counting repetitions, acetyl CoA, and ATP

  • Activation (Section 15.1) costs the equivalent of 2 ATP, once per fatty acid.
  • Each repetition removes 2 carbons as acetyl CoA and produces 1 FADH2 + 1 NADH, leaving an acyl CoA two carbons shorter.
  • For a fatty acid with n carbons (even): repetitions = n/2 − 1, acetyl CoA = n/2. The last repetition splits a 4-carbon piece into two acetyl CoA (the “extra” acetyl CoA), which is why there is one fewer repetition than acetyl CoA. A C18 fatty acid needs 8 repetitions to give 9 acetyl CoA; a C14 fatty acid needs 6 to give 7.
  • ATP total = (acetyl CoA × 10) + (NADH × 2.5) + (FADH2 × 1.5) − 2. Each acetyl CoA yields about 10 ATP in the citric acid cycle (Chapter 14); each repetition’s FADH2 and NADH go straight to electron transport. For stearic acid (18:0): 9 acetyl CoA (90) + 8 NADH (20) + 8 FADH2 (12) = 122, minus 2 for activation = 120 ATP.

Professor Tip

Draw the chain and slice it every 2 carbons from the carboxyl end. Count the cuts (repetitions of β-oxidation) and the pieces (acetyl CoA). A 10-carbon chain gets 4 cuts and makes 5 pieces. Do the counting before you touch any ATP numbers, and remember that the final cut gives two pieces at once, which is why pieces = cuts + 1.

Example 1: The full ATP tally for palmitic acid (16 C)

Problem. Palmitic acid is the most common saturated fatty acid in body fat. Build a complete table of its β-oxidation products and the ATP from each, then find the net ATP. Then, without redoing the table, state the net ATP for stearic acid (18 C).

Solution. Count first: 16 carbons → 16/2 = 8 acetyl CoA and 8 − 1 = 7 cycles, so 7 FADH2 and 7 NADH.

Stearic acid has one more two-carbon unit, which adds one acetyl CoA (10 ATP) and one more cycle (1.5 + 2.5 = 4 ATP): 106 + 14 = 120 ATP. In general, each extra pair of carbons on a saturated chain is worth 14 ATP.

Check. Per carbon, palmitate gives 106/16 ≈ 6.6 ATP while glucose gives 32/6 ≈ 5.3 ATP (32 per glucose with the malate–aspartate shuttle), consistent with fat being the more reduced, more energy-dense fuel. (Older textbooks that use 3 ATP per NADH and 2 per FADH2 report 129 ATP for palmitate; this book uses the modern values.)

Fats versus carbs, carbon for carbon

Example 2: Carbs versus fat, carbon for carbon

Problem. How many glucose molecules contain the same number of carbons as one stearic acid (18:0)? Compare the ATP from those glucose molecules with the ATP from the stearic acid, and explain the result in terms of oxidation state.

Solution. Glucose has 6 carbons, so 3 glucose = 18 carbons. ATP from 3 glucose = 3 × 32 = 96 ATP (using 32 per glucose, the malate–aspartate-shuttle value; with 30 per glucose it is 90, and the conclusion is the same). ATP from stearic acid = 9 acetyl CoA (90) + 8 NADH (20) + 8 FADH2 (12) − 2 (activation) = 120 ATP. The fatty acid gives more ATP per carbon because its carbons are mostly –CH2– groups with no oxygen, so each one has more hydrogens (electrons) to give to NAD+ and FAD; glucose carbons each already carry an oxygen and are partly oxidized before metabolism begins.

Check. Per carbon: 120/18 ≈ 6.7 ATP for stearic acid versus 96/18 ≈ 5.3 for glucose. On a mass basis the gap widens to about 2.4 times, which is the 9 kcal/g versus 4 kcal/g you see on nutrition labels (Chapter 14).

Unsaturated fatty acids

A double bond that is already present means the FAD step is skipped for that repetition: the pathway starts at step 2, so each C=C gives one fewer FADH2 (1.5 fewer ATP). Less energy is released from an unsaturated fatty acid than from the saturated one of the same length. There is a wrinkle: the double bonds in natural fatty acids are cis, and the hydration step adds water only to a trans double bond between the α and β carbons. A cis–trans isomerase (an isomerase) converts the cis bond, which is usually one carbon too far down the chain, into the trans-(2,3) bond that step 2 needs. At the level of this course, count one fewer FADH2 per pre-existing double bond; oleic acid (18 C, one C=C) gives 120 − 1.5 = 118.5 ATP. The number of acetyl CoA and the number of repetitions do not change.

Glycerol: the other half of a fat

Glycerol is worth a closer look because it is the only part of a fat that can become glucose. Released from adipose tissue by hormone-sensitive lipase (Section 15.6), it dissolves in blood and travels to the liver (fat cells lack the first enzyme), where it is converted to dihydroxyacetone phosphate in two steps:

  1. Phosphorylation of the 1° hydroxyl group. Glycerol kinase (a kinase, so a transferase; ATP → ADP) phosphorylates an end carbon to make glycerol 3-phosphate. How can you tell? A phosphate was added and ATP became ADP.
  2. Oxidation of the 2° alcohol to a ketone. Glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (an oxidoreductase; NAD+ → NADH) oxidizes the middle carbon, giving dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP). How can you tell? A C–OH became a C=O, and NAD+, the oxidizing agent, was reduced.

DHAP is a glycolysis intermediate, so it can go “downhill” to pyruvate when energy is needed or “uphill” to glucose during fasting (Section 13.9). Two glycerols make one glucose. The amount is small (glycerol is about one-tenth of a fat by mass and about 5% of its energy), but during a long fast it is a steady contribution to blood glucose.

Practice: True or false, and correct any false statement. (a) Glycerol 3-phosphate is reduced to dihydroxyacetone phosphate. (b) NAD+ is the oxidizing agent. (c) NAD+ is being reduced. (d) A dehydrogenase enzyme is likely involved.

Answer
(a) False. A C–OH becoming a C=O is an oxidation; glycerol 3-phosphate is oxidized. (b) True. (c) True: the oxidizing agent is itself reduced, to NADH. (d) True: an oxidation that reduces NAD+ is run by a dehydrogenase, an oxidoreductase.

Summary

  • β-Oxidation removes two carbons at a time as acetyl CoA in four repeated reactions: oxidation (FAD), hydration, oxidation (NAD+) and thiolysis.
  • A fatty acid with n carbons gives n/2 acetyl CoA in n/2 − 1 cycles.
  • Count ATP at 2.5 per NADH, 1.5 per FADH2 and 10 per acetyl CoA, minus 2 for activation: palmitate (16 C) gives 106 ATP, stearate (18 C) 120.
  • Glycerol from fat enters glycolysis as dihydroxyacetone phosphate.
