Learning Objectives

  • Explain why a cell needs both fast (seconds) and slow (hours) ways to control its enzymes.
  • Describe covalent modification by phosphorylation: what a protein kinase and a phosphatase each do, which amino acids are modified, and what kind of bond is formed.
  • Trace the hormone-to-enzyme path by which glucagon and epinephrine turn glycogen breakdown on and glycogen synthesis off, and how insulin reverses it.
  • Distinguish enzyme induction from repression and explain, at the concept level, why inducing a liver CYP enzyme changes the blood level of another drug.
  • Compare allosteric control, feedback inhibition, zymogen activation, covalent modification, and genetic control by speed, reversibility, and example.

A patient on warfarin is told to avoid St John's wort; a patient on a statin is told to skip grapefruit juice; a diabetic patient injects insulin before a meal. Each instruction comes down to a question this section answers: how does a cell change how much an enzyme does, and how fast can it make the change? Section 11.6 covered the quickest switches, allosteric regulators and feedback inhibition, which work in seconds because a small molecule simply binds and leaves. Here you meet two more: covalent modification, a chemical change to the enzyme itself, and genetic control, a change in how much enzyme the cell makes.

Fast switches and slow switches

Think of the two controls on a gas stove. The knob changes the flame in a second; that is allosteric control. Replacing the burner with a bigger one takes a trip to the store; that is genetic control. A cell needs both. Between meals your blood glucose must be held near 4–6 mmol/L (about 70–110 mg/dL) minute by minute, so the enzymes that release glucose from liver glycogen are switched by hormones within seconds to minutes. But if you move to a high-altitude city, or start a new medication, or begin drinking alcohol daily, the body answers over days by making more or less of particular enzymes. The table at the end of this section puts all five regulation strategies side by side; keep it in mind as you read.

Covalent modification: phosphorylation and dephosphorylation

In covalent modification, an enzyme is switched between an active and an inactive shape by forming or breaking a covalent bond to a small group. By far the most common group is a phosphate. Three amino acids have a side-chain –OH that can carry it: serine, threonine, and tyrosine.

  • A protein kinase transfers the terminal phosphate of ATP onto the –OH, leaving ADP. Kinases are transferases. The new bond is a phosphate ester, the same kind of link you met in Chapter 6 when an alcohol condenses with an acid; here the acid is phosphoric acid. At cell pH the attached phosphate carries two negative charges.
  • A protein phosphatase removes the phosphate by hydrolysis, releasing inorganic phosphate (HPO42−, written Pi). Phosphatases are hydrolases. The enzyme returns to its original form.

Why does one phosphate matter so much? Its 2− charge attracts nearby –NH3+ groups (lysine, arginine) and repels –COO− groups, so the chain folds slightly differently, and the active site opens or closes. For some enzymes the phosphorylated form is the active one; for others it is the inactive one. There is no rule; you learn each case. The switch is fast (seconds to minutes), it is reversible, and because the kinase and the phosphatase are themselves controlled by hormones, it lets a signal from outside the cell change what happens inside.

Professor Tip

Students mix up the two enzymes constantly. Fix it with the names: a kinase puts a phosphate on (from ATP) and is a transferase; a phosphatase is named like any hydrolase (lipase, protease) and takes the phosphate off with water. Then remember that "on" does not mean "active": glycogen phosphorylase is active with its phosphate, glycogen synthase is active without it.

The glycogen switch

The classic example, which you will meet again in Chapter 13, is the control of glycogen in liver and muscle. Two enzymes do opposite jobs: glycogen phosphorylase breaks glycogen down to glucose-1-phosphate, and glycogen synthase builds glycogen from glucose. Running both at once would waste ATP, so the cell phosphorylates both with the same signal and gets opposite results.

  1. Blood glucose falls (between meals), or you are frightened or exercising. The pancreas releases glucagon or the adrenal glands release epinephrine.
  2. The hormone binds a receptor on the outside of the cell (glucagon acts on liver; epinephrine on liver and muscle). It never enters. The receptor activates an enzyme that makes cyclic AMP (cAMP), a small "second messenger" inside the cell.
  3. cAMP activates protein kinase A, which, through one more kinase, phosphorylates glycogen phosphorylase (switching it ON) and phosphorylates glycogen synthase (switching it OFF). Glycogen is broken down and, in the liver, glucose is released into the blood.
  4. After a meal, insulin from the pancreas activates the protein phosphatases. Both phosphates come off: phosphorylase stops, synthase starts, and the incoming glucose is stored as glycogen.

One hormone molecule activates many kinase molecules, each of which phosphorylates many enzyme molecules, so the signal is amplified thousands of times in a few seconds. That is why a tiny dose of epinephrine has such a large and rapid effect.

Other covalent changes

Phosphorylation is the main reversible modification, but not the only one. Acetylation adds an acetyl group (CH3C=O) to the –NH2 of a lysine side chain, forming an amide; it is used mostly to control the proteins that package DNA and so which genes are read. Aspirin's attack on cyclooxygenase (Section 11.4) is also an acetylation, but an unwanted and permanent one.

Zymogen activation is a covalent change too, since a peptide bond is hydrolyzed, but it runs one way only: once the blocking piece is cut off, it cannot be reattached. Cells use it for enzymes that must never act inside the cell that makes them: the digestive proteases (pepsinogen, trypsinogen, chymotrypsinogen), the clotting factors (prothrombin → thrombin; thrombin then trims fibrinogen to fibrin), and the caspases, the proteases that dismantle a cell during programmed cell death.

Genetic control: induction and repression

Every enzyme is a protein, and a protein is made only when its gene is copied into messenger RNA (transcription) and the mRNA is read by ribosomes (translation); Chapter 12 gives the details. The cell can therefore control an enzyme at the source: make more of it (induction) or make less (repression). Because mRNA and protein must be built, and the old enzyme molecules must be worn out and degraded, the change takes hours to days. It is the slow, long-term switch.

  • Lactase. The enzyme that hydrolyzes lactose is made at high levels in every infant's intestine. In most of the world's population its gene is turned down after weaning, and by adulthood too little lactase is left to handle a glass of milk: undigested lactose draws water into the gut and is fermented by bacteria, giving the bloating, cramping, and diarrhea of lactose intolerance. People of northern European and some African and Middle Eastern ancestries often carry a variant that keeps the gene on (lactase persistence).
  • Cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes. The liver clears most drugs by oxidizing them with a family of oxidoreductases called cytochrome P450s. Several of them are inducible: a drug, a herb, or alcohol taken day after day signals the liver to make more of the enzyme. The patient now metabolizes not only that substance but every other drug the same CYP handles faster, so those drugs fall to lower blood levels than the prescriber intended. The reverse also happens: a substance that inhibits a CYP (the fast, reversible kind of control from Section 11.4) raises the levels of drugs that share the enzyme. The Clinical Connection boxes give the examples every nurse and dental hygienist is taught.
  • The lac operon. The first genetic switch ever worked out is in E. coli. The bacterium prefers glucose, and the genes for using lactose (including a β-galactosidase that hydrolyzes it) sit together under a single control region, the lac operon. With no lactose around, a repressor protein sits on the DNA and blocks transcription; when lactose appears, a lactose derivative binds the repressor, pulls it off, and the enzymes are made within minutes. When the lactose is gone the repressor rebinds and production stops. Your own cells use the same logic with more layers.

Worked examples

Example 1: Predict the effect of a kinase

Problem. The table lists how phosphorylation affects four enzymes. A hormone activates a kinase that phosphorylates all four. Predict what happens to glycogen storage, fat storage, and the release of fatty acids from fat cells.

EnzymeJobActive form
Glycogen phosphorylaseBreaks down glycogenPhosphorylated
Glycogen synthaseBuilds glycogenDephosphorylated
Acetyl-CoA carboxylaseFirst step of fatty-acid synthesisDephosphorylated
Hormone-sensitive lipaseHydrolyzes stored fat to fatty acidsPhosphorylated

Solution. Read each row: if the active form is "phosphorylated," the kinase turns the enzyme on; if "dephosphorylated," it turns it off. Glycogen phosphorylase on and glycogen synthase off: glycogen is broken down, not stored. Acetyl-CoA carboxylase off: no new fat is made. Hormone-sensitive lipase on: stored fat is hydrolyzed and fatty acids pour into the blood. All four changes release stored fuel.

Check. That is exactly the pattern you would want from epinephrine during a sprint or from glucagon during a fast, which is the hormone this kinase answers to. A phosphatase (insulin's signal) would reverse every row.

Example 2: Classify the regulation

Problem. Name the regulation strategy in each scenario and say whether it is reversible. (a) A patient taking phenobarbital daily for seizures is found, three weeks later, to clear a second drug twice as fast as before. (b) In a cut, prothrombin in the blood is converted to thrombin by removal of part of its chain. (c) Five minutes after an insulin injection, the phosphate groups on glycogen synthase in the patient's liver are gone and the enzyme is active. (d) In a muscle cell during exercise, AMP binds phosphofructokinase at a site away from the active site and the enzyme speeds up.

Solution. (a) Genetic control (enzyme induction): the time scale of weeks and the change in the amount of enzyme give it away. Reversible once the inducer is stopped, over days. (b) Zymogen activation: hydrolysis of a peptide bond; irreversible. (c) Covalent modification (dephosphorylation by a phosphatase); reversible by a kinase. (d) Allosteric control by a positive regulator; reversible as soon as AMP leaves.

Check. Only one of the four involves a change in the number of enzyme molecules; the other three change the activity of enzymes that already exist.

Example 3: A CYP inducer and a second drug

Problem. A patient on a steady dose of a drug that is metabolized by the liver enzyme CYP3A4 begins taking St John's wort for mood. Over the next two weeks the blood level of the drug drops by half. Explain what happened and why it took two weeks.

Solution. St John's wort induces the CYP3A4 gene, so the liver makes more CYP3A4 enzyme. With substrate in excess, rate is proportional to enzyme concentration (Section 11.3), so the drug is now oxidized and cleared roughly twice as fast, and its blood level falls until the lower level balances the same daily dose. It took two weeks because induction is genetic control: new mRNA and new enzyme protein had to be made and accumulate.

Check. If St John's wort were an inhibitor of CYP3A4 instead, the drug level would have risen, and within hours rather than weeks. Direction (down) and time scale (weeks) both point to induction.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Drug interactions through CYP enzymes. Warfarin, an anticoagulant with a narrow margin between too little and too much, is cleared by CYP enzymes, so anything that induces them (rifampin, carbamazepine, St John's wort) lowers its level and raises clotting risk, while inhibitors raise its level and the risk of bleeding; the INR (a clotting-time test) is checked whenever a medication changes. St John's wort induces CYP3A4 and lowers the level of many drugs, including some oral contraceptives and transplant drugs. Grapefruit juice does the opposite: it inhibits CYP3A4 in the intestinal wall, so a patient on certain statins or calcium-channel blockers absorbs more drug than intended. Insulin signaling. Insulin works by switching on phosphatases and kinases that store fuel; in type 1 diabetes the hormone is missing, and in type 2 the cells answer it poorly (insulin resistance), so glycogen synthase stays off and glucose stays in the blood (hyperglycemia). Insulin is a peptide and would be hydrolyzed by digestive proteases, which is why it is injected, never swallowed.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Why the alcohol history matters. A patient who drinks heavily every day has induced several liver CYP enzymes, including those that clear amide local anesthetics such as lidocaine and mepivacaine in the liver and those that metabolize acetaminophen and many sedatives. The anesthetic is cleared from the blood faster than usual, and the induced CYP2E1 converts acetaminophen to a toxic product faster, which is why the alcohol history is part of every medical history. A patient who is acutely intoxicated is a different case: alcohol in the blood competes for the same enzymes and slows drug clearance. Epinephrine in the cartridge. The epinephrine added to local anesthetic constricts blood vessels at the injection site, but any that reaches the circulation does what it does in the liver: raises cAMP and switches glycogen breakdown on. That is one reason the dose of epinephrine-containing anesthetic is limited in patients with poorly controlled diabetes or heart disease.

Summary

  • Cells regulate enzymes on two time scales: fast changes in the activity of enzymes that already exist (allosteric control, feedback inhibition, covalent modification, zymogen activation) and slow changes in the amount of enzyme (genetic control).
  • Covalent modification usually means phosphorylation. A protein kinase (a transferase) moves a phosphate from ATP onto a serine, threonine, or tyrosine –OH, forming a phosphate ester; a protein phosphatase (a hydrolase) removes it with water.
  • Glucagon and epinephrine raise cAMP, activate protein kinase A, and phosphorylate glycogen phosphorylase (ON) and glycogen synthase (OFF); insulin activates phosphatases and reverses both. The signal is amplified at every step.
  • Zymogen activation and aspirin's acetylation of COX are covalent changes that cannot be reversed.
  • Genetic control works by induction or repression of a gene and takes hours to days. Lactase decline after weaning, CYP enzyme induction by drugs and alcohol, and the bacterial lac operon are the standard examples.
  • A CYP inducer lowers the blood level of other drugs cleared by that enzyme; a CYP inhibitor raises it.

Exercises

Exercise 1

Write a word equation for what a protein kinase does to an enzyme with a serine –OH, and classify the kinase. Then do the same for the phosphatase.

Answer
Enzyme–OH + ATP → enzyme–O–PO32− + ADP; the kinase is a transferase (it moves a phosphate group). Enzyme–O–PO32− + H2O → enzyme–OH + HPO42−; the phosphatase is a hydrolase.

Exercise 2

Which three amino acids can be phosphorylated, and what functional group do they share that makes this possible? What type of bond is formed?

Answer
Serine, threonine, and tyrosine. Each has a side-chain hydroxyl (–OH; alcohol in serine and threonine, phenol in tyrosine). The phosphate attaches as a phosphate ester, the condensation product of the –OH with phosphoric acid.

Exercise 3

A new drug blocks the enzyme that makes cAMP in liver cells. Predict its effect on glycogen phosphorylase and on glycogen synthase when glucagon is released, and explain.

Answer
Glucagon's signal stops at the second step: without cAMP, protein kinase A is not activated, so neither enzyme is phosphorylated. Glycogen phosphorylase stays off and glycogen synthase stays on; the liver keeps storing glycogen instead of releasing glucose, and blood glucose could fall.

Exercise 4

Explain why zymogen activation is listed as "irreversible" while phosphorylation is "reversible," even though both involve covalent bonds.

Answer
Phosphorylation adds a group that a second enzyme (a phosphatase) can remove by hydrolysis, so the enzyme cycles between two forms. Zymogen activation hydrolyzes a peptide bond and discards a segment of the chain; no cellular enzyme reattaches it, so the active enzyme stays active until it is degraded.

Exercise 5

A patient's blood level of a drug rises sharply, within a day, after she starts a second medication. Is the second medication more likely an inducer or an inhibitor of the CYP enzyme that clears the first drug? Give two reasons.

Answer
An inhibitor. (1) Direction: inhibiting the clearing enzyme makes the drug accumulate, so its level rises; induction would lower it. (2) Speed: inhibition is a direct binding event that acts within hours, whereas induction requires new enzyme to be made and takes days to weeks.

Exercise 6

Lactose intolerance and the lac operon both involve an enzyme that hydrolyzes lactose, but the regulation runs in opposite directions. Explain.

Answer
In E. coli, lactose induces the operon: the enzyme is made only when lactose is present. In most adult humans the lactase gene is repressed after weaning regardless of diet, so the enzyme is scarce even when lactose arrives. Both are genetic control, but one is turned on by the substrate and the other is turned down by a developmental program.

Exercise 7

Classify each as allosteric control, feedback inhibition, covalent modification, zymogen activation, or genetic control: (a) chymotrypsinogen is cut by trypsin; (b) a bacterium stops making tryptophan when tryptophan accumulates, because tryptophan binds the first enzyme of the pathway away from its active site; (c) after two weeks of rifampin, a patient makes more CYP3A4; (d) epinephrine causes hormone-sensitive lipase in fat cells to gain a phosphate and become active.

Answer
(a) Zymogen activation. (b) Feedback inhibition (allosteric, by the end product). (c) Genetic control (induction). (d) Covalent modification (phosphorylation by a kinase).

Exercise 8

Using the idea that rate is proportional to enzyme concentration when substrate is in excess, explain in one or two sentences why doubling the amount of a CYP enzyme can halve a drug's steady blood level.

Answer
With twice the enzyme, the drug is cleared twice as fast at any given concentration. A fixed daily dose can only balance that faster clearance at a lower concentration, so the steady level settles at about half.
