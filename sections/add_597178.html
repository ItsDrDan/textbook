Lecture Connection

Transcription is base pairing with one twist: U instead of T. The thing students mix up is template strand versus coding strand, so that is where the lecture spends its time. The structure and jobs of the three RNAs are in Section 12.5; here we focus on making the mRNA.

How a gene is transcribed

A gene is a stretch of DNA that codes for one protein (or one RNA). To use it, the cell makes a working copy in RNA.

  • The section of DNA containing the gene unwinds, and RNA polymerase binds at the promoter, a sequence just before the gene that says "start here" (the promoter often contains the sequence TATAAA, the “TATA box,” about 25 bases before the start of the gene). It then reads one strand, the template strand, in the 3′→5′ direction.
  • It builds the mRNA 5′→3′ by the same base-pairing rules as replication, except that U pairs with A: template A → U, T → A, G → C, C → G. No primer is needed.
  • The other DNA strand, the coding strand (or sense strand), is not read, but its sequence matches the mRNA exactly, except that it has T where the mRNA has U. Gene sequences in databases are written as the coding strand for this reason.
  • At a sequence of bases that is read as a stop signal the polymerase stops and releases the RNA. In human cells this pre-mRNA is edited before it leaves the nucleus: genes are segmented into exons, the segments that express the genetic information, and introns, non-coding segments in between. A spliceosome removes the introns and joins the exons (splicing) to give the mature mRNA, which then moves out of the nucleus to the ribosomes (Section 12.5).
  • Transcription is regulated: a cell makes a given mRNA only when it needs that protein. In repression the end product of a pathway slows the synthesis of the mRNA for its enzymes; in enzyme induction a high level of a reactant switches on transcription of the enzymes that use it (the "genetic control" of Chapter 11).

Professor Tip

"Template" and "coding" trip up more students than anything else in this chapter. The template strand is the one the polymerase actually pairs with, so the mRNA is its complement. The coding strand is the one the mRNA looks like. If a problem gives you a template strand, complement it (with U). If it gives you a coding strand, just copy it and swap T for U.

Worked example

Example 1: Coding strand to protein

Problem: The coding strand of a short gene reads 5′-ATGAAGTGGCACTAA-3′. Write the template strand, the mRNA, and the peptide.

Solution

Template strand: the complement, antiparallel: 3′-TACTTCACCGTGATT-5′.

mRNA: the coding strand with U for T (equivalently, the complement of the template): 5′-AUG AAG UGG CAC UAA-3′.

Peptide: AUG = Met, AAG = Lys, UGG = Trp, CAC = His, UAA = stop: Met-Lys-Trp-His.

Check: The mRNA and the coding strand read the same (ATG/AUG, AAG/AAG...). Fifteen bases make five codons; one is the stop, so four amino acids.

Summary

  • Three kinds of RNA make proteins: mRNA carries the code, tRNA brings the amino acids, and rRNA forms the ribosome.
  • In transcription, RNA polymerase reads the template strand 3′→5′ and builds mRNA 5′→3′; the mRNA has the coding strand’s sequence, with U in place of T.
  • A codon is three mRNA bases that code for one amino acid; AUG is the start codon, and UAA, UAG and UGA are stop codons.
  • The anticodon on a tRNA pairs with the codon, antiparallel.
