Lecture Connection

Lecture's version of this section is a short checklist: benzene is the parent, ortho/meta/para are for exactly two groups, four special parents (toluene, phenol, aniline, benzoic acid) own carbon 1, and when the ring hangs off a longer chain it becomes phenyl.

Naming substituted benzenes

  • Common names to know: toluene (methylbenzene), phenol (hydroxybenzene), aniline (aminobenzene), and benzoic acid.
  • Benzene is the "parent chain." For two substituents: ortho (1,2: next-door neighbors), meta (1,3: one ring carbon in between), para (1,4: directly opposite).

The checklist.

  • One substituent: name the group, then "benzene," with no number: chlorobenzene, nitrobenzene (–NO2), ethylbenzene. A few rings have their own parent names that you must memorize: toluene (–CH3), phenol (–OH), aniline (–NH2), and benzoic acid (–COOH).
  • Two substituents: there are only three possible arrangements, 1,2-, 1,3-, and 1,4-, called ortho (o-), meta (m-), and para (p-). You may use either the numbers or the prefix. If one of the groups gives the ring a special parent name, that group is on carbon 1 and is not named as a substituent: a chlorine straight across from the methyl of toluene is 4-chlorotoluene or p-chlorotoluene.
  • Three or more substituents: numbers only (no o/m/p). Number to give the lowest set of numbers, start from the special parent group if there is one, and list the substituents alphabetically: 2,4-dichlorotoluene; 1-bromo-3-chloro-5-nitrobenzene.

Phenyl and the polynuclear aromatics

  • When a benzene ring is attached to a chain that holds the main functional group, it is named as a substituent: phenyl (C6H5–). Example: CH3CH(C6H5)CH2CH3 is 2-phenylbutane.
  • Polynuclear aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) have two or more fused benzene rings: naphthalene (2 rings, mothballs), anthracene (3 rings). Larger PAHs such as benzo[a]pyrene form when organic matter burns incompletely and are carcinogens in tobacco smoke and charred food.

Use "phenyl" when the chain is the main part of the name, usually because it carries the functional group: 2-phenylbutane (drawn in the naming figure above), 2-phenylethanol. When the ring is clearly the parent, name the chain as an alkyl substituent instead: ethylbenzene, not phenylethane. Both names describe real molecules correctly; the convention just keeps names short.

In a PAH, neighboring rings share an edge (two carbons), so naphthalene is C10H8, not C12. All the rings lie in one flat sheet, which lets PAH molecules stack like plates; that stacking is why even naphthalene is a solid at room temperature. PAHs are nonpolar and dissolve in body fat. When the liver tries to oxidize benzo[a]pyrene to make it water soluble for excretion, some of the intermediate products react with DNA, which is how a chemically "stable" aromatic compound becomes a carcinogen (a cancer-causing agent). Wood smoke, diesel exhaust, grilled meat with char, and above all tobacco smoke are the common sources.

Example 1: Naming polysubstituted benzenes

Problem. Name each compound. (a) A benzene ring with –CH3 and, straight across the ring, –Cl. (b) A benzene ring with –OH and, on the next carbon, –NO2. (c) A benzene ring with –Br on C1 and –CH2CH3 on C3. (d) A benzene ring with –CH3 and two Cl atoms, one next to the methyl and one straight across from it.

Solution.

  • (a) The methyl makes this a toluene, so –CH3 is C1 and the Cl is on C4: 4-chlorotoluene, or p-chlorotoluene.
  • (b) The OH makes it a phenol; OH is C1 and the nitro group is on C2: 2-nitrophenol, or o-nitrophenol.
  • (c) Neither group has a special parent, so the parent is benzene and the substituents are listed alphabetically: 1-bromo-3-ethylbenzene, or m-bromoethylbenzene.
  • (d) Three substituents, so numbers only. Toluene parent: CH3 on C1, the chlorines on C2 and C4: 2,4-dichlorotoluene.

Check. Two groups: o/m/p or numbers are both fine. Three or more: numbers only. A special parent group (toluene, phenol, aniline, benzoic acid) is always on C1 and is never listed as a substituent; in (a) "1-chloro-4-methylbenzene" is not wrong, but 4-chlorotoluene is the name you will see on labels.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Three aromatic stories play out in the operatory. First, benzocaine, the active ingredient in topical anesthetic gel applied before scaling or injections, is a para-disubstituted benzene: an amino group (–NH2) on carbon 1 and an ester group straight across on carbon 4. Spotting that 1,4 pattern is exactly the naming skill in this section. Second, tobacco smoke carries polynuclear aromatic hydrocarbons such as benzo[a]pyrene directly onto oral tissue, which is why smokers have a much higher rate of oral squamous cell carcinoma and of periodontitis (infection and bone loss around the teeth that follows untreated gingivitis). Smoking also narrows gingival blood vessels, so the bleeding that normally signals gum disease may be masked. Third, the resins you work with every day are alkene chemistry: pit-and-fissure sealants and composite restorations are methacrylate monomers (Bis-GMA and its thinner partners) whose C=C bonds open under the curing light and link into a hard addition polymer, and denture bases and orthodontic retainers are poly(methyl methacrylate), the acrylic in the polymer table.
