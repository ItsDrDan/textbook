Lecture Connection

In this chapter the lecture follows only the carbohydrates through Stage 1. Digestion is hydrolysis, every digestive enzyme is a hydrolase, and the skill is to list the carbohydrate enzymes in the order the food meets them and say where each one works. Protein and fat digestion come back in Chapter 15.

Carbohydrate digestion, enzyme by enzyme

Each enzyme in the table is a glycosidic-bond-breaking hydrolase from Chapter 8, and the chain runs from the mouth to the surface of the small intestine.

The pattern to remember: amylases shorten the polysaccharide, the three disaccharidases finish the job, and the monosaccharides (mostly glucose) enter the bloodstream for transport to the cells. What happens at the brush border and afterward is in Section 13.4.

Practice: two questions the lecture asks about digestion

Practice: Why does carbohydrate digestion not take place in the stomach?

Answer
The low pH of the stomach destroys the activity of the amylase that came from the saliva (acid denatures the enzyme, Chapter 11). Starch digestion pauses until pancreatic amylase takes over in the small intestine, where bicarbonate has raised the pH again. Proteins, by contrast, are digested in the stomach, because pepsin is built to work at pH 1–2.

Practice: Starch is converted to glucose during digestion. What type of chemical reaction is this: hydrolysis, phosphorylation, combustion, or hydration? Does the process require energy to be put in, or release it?

Answer
Hydrolysis: water splits each glycosidic bond, and the enzymes are hydrolases. No ATP is spent, and the small amount of energy the hydrolysis releases is lost as heat. That is why Stage 1 captures no useful energy; the energy of the food is still inside the glucose, waiting for Stage 2.
