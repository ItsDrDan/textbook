Alkenes behave like alkanes, and the same rule extends to alkynes and aromatic compounds; the data below let you rank them. All of them are nonpolar, so dispersion forces and therefore size decide everything.

Same rules as alkanes

All of the hydrocarbons in this chapter are made of only carbon and hydrogen, so they are nonpolar and held together by dispersion forces alone. That gives them the same property profile as alkanes: insoluble in water, less dense than water (they float), and boiling points that rise with size. A double or triple bond removes two or four hydrogens but changes the size and shape of the molecule very little, so an alkene boils within a few degrees of the alkane with the same carbon skeleton. To rank boiling points, count carbons first: more carbons means more electrons and more surface contact, so stronger dispersion forces. Among molecules of the same size, rings (cycloalkanes and aromatic compounds) pack more closely than open chains, so they boil higher: cyclohexane 81 °C and benzene 80 °C versus hexane 69 °C and 1-hexene 63 °C. Flat aromatic rings also stack well, which gives them high melting points (naphthalene is a solid). Water solubility is essentially zero for every compound in this chapter; the first hydrocarbon derivatives that dissolve in water are the small alcohols and phenol in Chapter 4, because they add an O–H that can hydrogen bond. One practical consequence: benzene and toluene are good solvents for nonpolar substances and are absorbed through skin, and benzene is itself a carcinogen, so laboratories have replaced it with toluene wherever they can.

Example 1: Ranking by boiling point and predicting solubility

Problem. Rank from lowest to highest boiling point: 1-octene, propene, 1-hexene, benzene. Which of them dissolves in water?

Solution. All four are nonpolar hydrocarbons with dispersion forces only, so size decides. Carbon count: propene 3, benzene 6, 1-hexene 6, 1-octene 8. Propene is lowest (it is a gas at room temperature, bp −47 °C) and 1-octene is highest (121 °C). Benzene and 1-hexene tie on carbons; benzene is a flat ring that stacks closely, so it boils a bit higher (80 °C versus 63 °C). Ranking: propene < 1-hexene < benzene < 1-octene. None of them dissolves in water; all four float on it.

Check. The two six-carbon compounds differ by only 17 °C, while adding two carbons (hexene to octene) adds nearly 60 °C. Size is the big lever; shape only breaks ties.
