Learning Objectives

  • Tell essential from nonessential amino acids and explain why some amino acids are conditionally essential.
  • Describe how nonessential amino acids are made from metabolic intermediates, mainly by transamination.
  • Explain the link between phenylalanine, tyrosine and phenylketonuria (PKU).

In humans, ten of the standard amino acids (Glu, Gln, Pro, Asp, Asn, Ala, Gly, Ser, Tyr, Cys) can be made in the body; they are the nonessential amino acids. Most of them get their amino group from glutamate. Glutamate itself is made by adding ammonia to α-ketoglutarate (glutamate dehydrogenase running in reverse, with NADPH), and glutamate passes its amino group to other α-keto acids by transamination.

Glutamine is made by adding a second nitrogen, from ammonia, to glutamate; this is also the body’s main way to trap ammonia safely. Proline is made from glutamate in a few steps. Alanine and aspartate are made by transamination of pyruvate and oxaloacetate, and asparagine is made from aspartate.

Serine is made from the glycolysis intermediate 3-phosphoglycerate. Serine then gives glycine (using a coenzyme made from the vitamin folic acid) and the carbon skeleton of cysteine; the sulfur of cysteine comes from the essential amino acid methionine.

Tyrosine is another amino acid that depends on an essential amino acid as a precursor. In this case, the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase oxidizes phenylalanine to tyrosine, using O2 to add an –OH group to the aromatic ring. It is the enzyme missing in phenylketonuria (PKU).

In general, the synthesis of essential amino acids, usually in microorganisms, is much more complex than for the nonessential amino acids and is best left to a full-fledged biochemistry course.
