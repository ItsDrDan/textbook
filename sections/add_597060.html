One molecule, four ways to draw it

Molecules are three-dimensional objects, and how the atoms are connected is what distinguishes one molecule from another. Three kinds of 3-D model show that: a space-filling model (color-coded blobs whose merged electron clouds give the most realistic picture), a ball-and-stick model (color-coded balls with sticks for bonds), and a wedge–dash drawing (element symbols, with plain lines for bonds in the plane of the paper, wedges for bonds coming toward you, and dashes for bonds going behind the page). The 2-D structural formulas below give up the shape and keep only the connectivity.

Reading a condensed formula

A condensed formula reads like a book, left to right: each carbon is followed by the atoms bonded to it, C–H bonds are understood, and bonds to a heteroatom (any atom that is not C or H) can be drawn or implied, so CH3–COOH, CH3C(=O)OH, and CH3COOH are all the same acetic acid. Identical groups in a row can be condensed further into parentheses with a subscript: CH3CH2CH2CH2OH and CH3(CH2)3OH are both correct. The one thing you may not do is condense until the connectivity is unclear: "(CH2)5CHOH" for cyclohexanol hides the ring, so a ring is drawn as a polygon instead. It is fine to write the H's to the left of a carbon (HOCH2CH3) when that shows the bonds more clearly.

Reading a line structure

Line (skeletal) structures are the most stripped-down representation and the one organic chemists use most, because they are fast to draw. Three rules let you read any line structure:

  1. Every corner and every end of a line is a carbon atom.
  2. Each carbon has as many hidden hydrogens as it needs to reach four bonds. A carbon with two lines touching it has two hidden H's; a carbon at the end of a line has three.
  3. Nitrogen, oxygen, sulfur, and the halogens are always written, and so is any hydrogen bonded to them (the OH above). Those are the hydrogens that matter for hydrogen bonding, so they stay visible.

Professor Tip

To get a molecular formula from a line structure, count carbons first, then add hydrogens carbon by carbon until each has four bonds. Writing the condensed formula as an in-between step catches most mistakes. And when a question gives you only a molecular formula, remember that one formula can belong to several different molecules (C2H6O is both ethanol and dimethyl ether): always draw the structure.

In this course

Three small translations between the source text and the lecture vocabulary. (1) The source calls the shapes a molecule takes on by rotating about its single bonds "different configurations." In this course those are conformations (Chapter 2); configuration is reserved for arrangements that rotation cannot interconvert, such as cis/trans alkenes (Chapter 3) and chiral centers (Chapter 5). (2) The source's "isopropane" is a nickname; the compound CH3CH(CH3)CH3 is 2-methylpropane (common name isobutane). (3) The source's "Lewis formula" and "skeletal formula" are this course's Lewis structure and line (skeletal) structure; an expanded structural formula is a Lewis structure with the lone pairs left off.

Example 1: Writing a condensed formula from a line structure

Problem

Write the condensed structural formula of each molecule, condensing repeated CH2 groups where you can. (a) The seven-carbon chain below, 3-methylheptane, a component of gasoline. (b) Cyclopentanol, the five-membered ring with one OH.

Solution

(a) Read the chain from left to right, writing each carbon with its hidden hydrogens. The first carbon is an end (CH3), the second has two lines (CH2), the third has three lines because of the branch (CH), and the branch itself is a CH3 written in parentheses right after the carbon that holds it. Then come four more chain carbons: three CH2 and a final CH3. Written out: CH3CH2CH(CH3)CH2CH2CH2CH3. The three identical CH2 groups in a row can be collected: CH3CH2CH(CH3)(CH2)3CH3. Both versions are correct and say the same thing.

(b) The ring has five carbons: one CHOH and four CH2. A string such as "(CH2)4CHOH" is not acceptable, because it does not show that the first and last carbons are bonded to each other. For a ring, draw the polygon and write the groups on it, or draw the ring of CH2 units with bond lines closing the loop.

Check

Count hydrogens in (a): 3 + 2 + 1 + 3 + 3(2) + 3 = 18, and C8H18 fits CnH2n+2 for an eight-carbon alkane. In (b) the point of a structural formula is to show how the atoms are connected; if a condensed formula loses the ring, it has lost the structure. ✓
