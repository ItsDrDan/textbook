Previously, we noted that enzymes are inactivated at high temperatures and by changes in pH. These are nonspecific factors that would inactivate any enzyme. The activity of enzymes can also be regulated by more specific inhibitors. Many poisons work by bonding covalently to particular enzymes and inactivating them.

Irreversible Inhibition: Poisons

An irreversible inhibitor inactivates an enzyme by bonding covalently to a particular group at the active site. The inhibitor-enzyme bond is so strong that the inhibition cannot be reversed by the addition of excess substrate. The nerve gases, especially diisopropyl fluorophosphate (DIFP), irreversibly inhibit biological systems by forming an enzyme-inhibitor complex with a specific OH group of serine situated at the active sites of certain enzymes. The peptidases trypsin and chymotrypsin contain serine groups at the active site and are inhibited by DIFP.

Valley Connection: The Pesticide That Blocks a Nerve Enzyme

Drive through Fresno County in spring and you will pass sprayers in almond orchards, vineyards and citrus groves. For decades one of the most widely used insecticides on those crops was chlorpyrifos, an organophosphate. It was used on alfalfa, almonds, citrus, cotton, grapes and walnuts.1

Organophosphates work exactly like DIFP above. The phosphorus atom bonds covalently to the serine –OH in the active site of acetylcholinesterase (AChE). AChE normally hydrolyzes the ester acetylcholine (Chapter 6) the instant a nerve signal is delivered, which switches the signal off. With AChE blocked, acetylcholine piles up and nerves keep firing. Insects and people use the same enzyme, so the chemistry that kills an aphid can also poison the person spraying.

In the clinic. Nurses and ER staff learn the signs with the mnemonic SLUDGE: salivation, lacrimation (tears), urination, defecation, GI cramping and emesis (vomiting), plus pinpoint pupils, wheezing and a slow heart rate. Most deaths come from respiratory failure. Atropine blocks acetylcholine receptors and dries up the secretions, but it does not fix the enzyme. Pralidoxime can pull the phosphate group off serine and reactivate AChE, but only if it is given early, before the enzyme–inhibitor bond “ages” and becomes permanent.2

California’s safety net. Workers who mix, load or apply the most toxic organophosphate or carbamate pesticides on more than six days in any 30-day period must be under a doctor’s medical supervision. A lab measures their red-cell and plasma cholinesterase and compares it with their own baseline. If red-cell activity falls to 70% of baseline (or plasma to 60%), the worker is removed from exposure until both return to at least 80%.3,4

The ban. Under a 2019 agreement with the manufacturers, sales of chlorpyrifos to California growers ended on February 6, 2020, and all possession and use ended after December 31, 2020.5

Think about it: A farmworker arrives at the ER six hours after a spill. Why might the doctor give pralidoxime right away instead of waiting to see whether atropine is enough?

Answer
Atropine only treats symptoms; the enzyme is still blocked. Pralidoxime removes the organophosphate from the serine and restores AChE, but once the bond “ages” it can no longer do that. The body then has to make new enzyme, which can take weeks. Giving it early saves the enzyme the patient already has.

Reversible Inhibition

A reversible inhibitor inactivates an enzyme through noncovalent, more easily reversed, interactions. Unlike an irreversible inhibitor, a reversible inhibitor can dissociate from the enzyme. Reversible inhibitors include competitive inhibitors and noncompetitive inhibitors. (There are additional types of reversible inhibitors.) A competitive inhibitor is any compound that bears a structural resemblance to a particular substrate and thus competes with that substrate for binding at the active site of an enzyme. The inhibitor is not acted on by the enzyme but does prevent the substrate from approaching the active site.

The degree to which a competitive inhibitor interferes with an enzyme’s activity depends on the relative concentrations of the substrate and the inhibitor. If the inhibitor is present in relatively large quantities, it will initially block most of the active sites. But because the binding is reversible, some substrate molecules will eventually bind to the active site and be converted to product. Increasing the substrate concentration promotes displacement of the inhibitor from the active site. Competitive inhibition can be completely reversed by adding substrate so that it reaches a much higher concentration than that of the inhibitor.

Studies of competitive inhibition have provided helpful information about certain enzyme-substrate complexes and the interactions of specific groups at the active sites. As a result, pharmaceutical companies have synthesized drugs that competitively inhibit metabolic processes in bacteria and certain cancer cells. Many drugs are competitive inhibitors of specific enzymes.

A classic example of competitive inhibition is the effect of malonate on the enzyme activity of succinate dehydrogenase (Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\)). Malonate and succinate are the anions of dicarboxylic acids and contain three and four carbon atoms, respectively. The malonate molecule binds to the active site because the spacing of its carboxyl groups is not greatly different from that of succinate. However, no catalytic reaction occurs because malonate does not have a CH2CH2 group to convert to CH=CH. This reaction will also be discussed in connection with the Krebs cycle and energy production.

To Your Health: Penicillin

An antibiotic is a drug that kills bacteria or stops their growth. Many antibiotics work by inhibiting an enzyme that bacteria need but human cells do not have, so the drug harms the bacteria and leaves the patient’s own cells alone.

Penicillin was discovered by Alexander Fleming in 1928, in a mold growing on a bacterial culture plate. It became the first antibiotic produced in large amounts, in the 1940s.

Penicillin blocks the bacterial enzymes (transpeptidases) that cross-link the cell wall. The enzyme becomes covalently bonded to the drug, so penicillin is a good example of an irreversible inhibitor. With a weak wall, the bacterial cell bursts. Human cells have no cell wall and no transpeptidase, so they are not affected.

All penicillins share a β-lactam ring, a strained four-membered cyclic amide fused to a five-membered ring; they differ only in the R group (structures below; methicillin, the last one shown, is an older penicillinase-resistant penicillin that is no longer used in the US). The cephalosporins are related β-lactam antibiotics.

Penicillins work best against Gram-positive bacteria, which have a thick peptidoglycan cell wall and stain purple with Gram’s stain; some, such as amoxicillin, also act on certain Gram-negative bacteria. Penicillin G is destroyed by stomach acid and is given by injection; penicillin V, ampicillin and amoxicillin are acid-stable and are taken by mouth.

Resistance. Some bacteria make β-lactamase (penicillinase), an enzyme that hydrolyzes the amide in the β-lactam ring and destroys the drug. Others, such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), change the target enzyme so the drug no longer binds. Every unneeded antibiotic dose favors resistant bacteria, which is why hospitals and clinics practice antibiotic stewardship: the right drug, at the right dose, only when it is needed.

Allergy. A true penicillin allergy can cause anaphylaxis, a life-threatening reaction, so the allergy history is checked before every dose. However, most people whose charts say “penicillin allergy” turn out not to be allergic when they are tested, and an allergy evaluation can let them receive the most effective drug.

Some reversible inhibitors are noncompetitive. A noncompetitive inhibitor can combine with either the free enzyme or the enzyme-substrate complex because its binding site on the enzyme is distinct from the active site. Binding of this kind of inhibitor alters the three-dimensional conformation of the enzyme, changing the configuration of the active site with one of two results. Either the enzyme-substrate complex does not form at its normal rate, or, once formed, it does not yield products at the normal rate. Because the inhibitor does not structurally resemble the substrate, the addition of excess substrate does not reverse the inhibitory effect.

Summary

An irreversible inhibitor inactivates an enzyme by bonding covalently to a particular group at the active site. A reversible inhibitor inactivates an enzyme through noncovalent, reversible interactions. A competitive inhibitor competes with the substrate for binding at the active site of the enzyme. A noncompetitive inhibitor binds at a site distinct from the active site.

Molecule in the Clinic: Amoxicillin and the β-lactam ring

Amoxicillin, a penicillin, is the most common antibiotic for dental infections. Every penicillin contains a strained four-membered cyclic amide called a β-lactam. The bacterial enzyme that builds the cell wall attacks this ring and becomes covalently bonded to the drug, so it is permanently shut down and the bacterium bursts. Many resistant bacteria make β-lactamase, an enzyme that hydrolyzes the ring first; Augmentin adds clavulanate to block that enzyme.

Your turn: Is penicillin a reversible or an irreversible inhibitor? Explain.

Answer
Irreversible: it forms a covalent bond in the enzyme’s active site, so the enzyme cannot be recovered by adding more substrate.

Your turn: β-Lactamase breaks the β-lactam ring by hydrolysis. Which functional group is being hydrolyzed?

Answer
An amide (the ring is a cyclic amide), the same reaction as amide hydrolysis in Chapter 7.
