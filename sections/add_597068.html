Professor Tip

In a line-angle drawing, every heteroatom (O, N, Cl, etc.) is written out, along with any H attached to it. Only carbon and the hydrogens on carbon are hidden. To count the hidden hydrogens at a corner, subtract the number of lines meeting there from 4. In a condensed formula for a branched molecule, the branch goes in parentheses right after the carbon it hangs from: look to the left of the parentheses to see what the branch is connected to, so CH3CH(CH3)CH2CH3 has its methyl on the second carbon. Order matters: never rearrange atoms for the sake of condensing.

Reading a structure: hidden hydrogens and 1°, 2°, 3°, 4° carbons

Every carbon in an alkane can be labeled by how many other carbons it is bonded to. A primary (1°) carbon is bonded to one other carbon, a secondary (2°) carbon to two, a tertiary (3°) carbon to three, and a quaternary (4°) carbon to four. Count carbon neighbors, not hydrogens: in an alkane a 1° carbon happens to be a CH3, a 2° carbon a CH2, a 3° carbon a CH, and a 4° carbon has no H at all, but the definition is about the carbon–carbon bonds. That matters later, because in an alcohol the carbon carrying the OH is classified by the same rule even though it has lost a hydrogen to the oxygen.

Why bother? The classification predicts behavior. For a hydrocarbon it also tells you the hydrogen count at a glance: a 1° carbon carries three H, a 2° carbon two, a 3° carbon one, and a 4° carbon none. In Chapter 4 the same labels decide whether an alcohol can be oxidized, and in Chapter 7 they describe amines and amides. Learn the counting rule now and you will use it all semester.

Example 1: Classifying carbons

Classify each carbon in 2,2,4-trimethylpentane (the "isooctane" of octane ratings): (CH3)3CCH2CH(CH3)CH3.

Solution

  • C1, C5, and all three methyl branches: each is bonded to 1 other carbon → primary (1°)
  • C2: bonded to 4 carbons → quaternary (4°)
  • C3: bonded to 2 carbons → secondary (2°)
  • C4: bonded to 3 carbons → tertiary (3°)

This skill comes right back when we classify alcohols as 1°, 2°, or 3° in Chapter 4.

Example 2: Classifying every carbon from a line structure

Problem. Using the labeled structure above, classify each carbon in 3-ethyl-2,2-dimethylpentane and give the molecular formula.

Solution. Number the five-carbon chain so that the substituents get the lowest numbers (2,2,3), then count carbon neighbors at each position.

  • C1: bonded only to C2 → 1°. C5: bonded only to C4 → 1°.
  • C2: bonded to C1, C3, and two methyl carbons → four neighbors → 4°.
  • C3: bonded to C2, C4, and the ethyl CH2 → three neighbors → 3°.
  • C4: bonded to C3 and C5 → 2°. The CH2 of the ethyl group is bonded to C3 and to its own CH3 → also 2°.
  • The two methyls on C2 and the CH3 of the ethyl group each touch one carbon → 1°.

Totals: five 1°, two 2°, one 3°, one 4° carbon, nine carbons in all, so the formula is C9H20.

Check. Hydrogens: five CH3 (15 H) + two CH2 (4 H) + one CH (1 H) + a quaternary carbon (0 H) = 20 H, which matches CnH2n+2 for n = 9. ✓
