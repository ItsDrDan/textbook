Lecture Connection

Recombinant DNA is an overview topic: cut, paste, copy, express. What makes it chemistry rather than biology is that every step is a reaction type you already know: a hydrolase cuts, hydrogen bonds pair the sticky ends, a ligase re-forms the phosphodiester bonds, and the host's polymerases and ribosomes do the rest. PCR is the same base-pairing chemistry run in a test tube.

Cut, paste, copy, express

  1. Cut. A restriction enzyme is a hydrolase (Chapter 11) that recognizes a short sequence, often a palindrome such as 5′-GAATTC-3′, and hydrolyzes a phosphodiester bond in each strand. Many cut in a staggered way, leaving short single-stranded "sticky ends" that can base-pair with any other end cut by the same enzyme.
  2. Paste. The gene and the opened plasmid (the vector) are mixed; their sticky ends pair by hydrogen bonds, and DNA ligase (a ligase, Chapter 11) re-forms the phosphodiester bonds. The product is recombinant DNA: DNA from two sources joined covalently.
  3. Copy. The plasmid is taken up by bacteria (or yeast), which replicate it every time they divide.
  4. Express. The cells transcribe and translate the inserted gene, and the protein is harvested and purified. Recombinant human insulin, human growth hormone, clotting factor VIII, erythropoietin, and the hepatitis B and HPV vaccine proteins are all made this way. Most insulin used by people with diabetes today is produced this way.

Professor Tip

For this course, know the four-step logic of recombinant DNA, the roles of the restriction enzyme (a hydrolase) and DNA ligase (a ligase), and the medical products: insulin, growth hormone, factor VIII and the recombinant hepatitis B and HPV vaccines. The crop and cheese examples and the Human Genome Project detail are background.

PCR: base pairing in a test tube

The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) uses a heat-stable DNA polymerase to copy a chosen stretch of DNA over and over: heat separates the strands (breaking the hydrogen bonds, Section 12.2), cooling lets short primers pair with them, and the polymerase extends the primers 5′→3′; each cycle doubles the DNA, so thirty cycles give about a billion copies. PCR is behind forensic DNA profiling, many genetic tests, and the laboratory tests that detect viral genes.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Genetic testing. PCR and DNA sequencing of a blood or saliva sample can identify carriers of sickle cell trait, cystic fibrosis, and many other conditions (Section 12.7), screen for the BRCA1 and BRCA2 variants linked to breast cancer, and show which patients will metabolize a drug slowly or quickly (pharmacogenomics), so a dose can be adjusted before the first tablet. A PCR test for a viral gene is also how a swab is read for SARS-CoV-2 or influenza: these viruses have RNA genomes, so the viral RNA is first copied into DNA by reverse transcriptase (RT-PCR); if the virus's sequence is present, the primers find it and the DNA doubles every cycle until the signal is detectable.

Summary

  • Recombinant DNA is made by cutting DNA with a restriction enzyme (a hydrolase), inserting a gene into a plasmid, and sealing it with DNA ligase (a ligase); host bacteria then make the protein.
  • Medical products made this way include human insulin, growth hormone, factor VIII and the hepatitis B and HPV vaccines.
  • The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) copies a chosen DNA segment by repeated cycles of heating, primer binding and copying; each cycle doubles the DNA.
  • PCR is used in genetic testing, DNA fingerprinting and the diagnosis of infections such as Valley fever.

Answers

“Think about it: Why must the primers in a…”

A patient sample (such as sputum) is full of the patient’s own human DNA. Primers bind by complementary base pairing, so they only start copying where they find a matching sequence. If the primers matched human DNA, every sample would be amplified and every test would look positive. Primers that match only a Coccidioides sequence make the test specific: DNA is copied only if the fungus is actually there.
