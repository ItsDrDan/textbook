Organic chemistry, old and new

The old definition of organic chemistry was the study of compounds from living things; the modern definition is the study of compounds of carbon. Organic compounds contain carbon and hydrogen, usually nitrogen and oxygen, and sometimes phosphorus, sulfur, and the halogens. Carbohydrates, fats, proteins, enzymes, nucleic acids, hormones, vitamins, and even plastics are all organic. Every organic compound exists as discrete molecules held together by covalent bonds, so organic compounds are molecular. Inorganic compounds can be molecular (water, CO2) or ionic (NaCl, the hydroxyapatite of tooth enamel), held together by the electrostatic attraction between opposite charges.

The parts list: functional groups you must recognize

A functional group is a heteroatom, a multiple bond, or sometimes both: a specific atom or small cluster of atoms that gives a molecule its chemical personality. A given functional group tends to undergo the same reactions in every molecule that contains it, however large the rest of the molecule is, which is why this course is organized by functional group (alcohols in Chapter 4, aldehydes and ketones in Chapter 5, and so on). A molecule can have more than one functional group. In the table, R abbreviates "the rest of the molecule," the carbon–hydrogen part that does not take part in the reaction. The table lists each group, how to recognize it, one small example, and where you will meet it in health care.

One special case appears constantly in drugs: an O–H attached directly to a benzene ring is a phenol, not an alcohol. It looks like an alcohol but is a weak acid, so Chapter 4 treats it separately. Acetaminophen, eugenol, and morphine all contain a phenol.

Professor Tip

To find functional groups in a big molecule, hunt for carbonyl groups (C=O) first and ask what is attached to the carbonyl carbon: an H means aldehyde, two carbons mean ketone, an OH means carboxylic acid, an OR means ester, and an N means amide. Then look at any oxygen, nitrogen, or sulfur that is not next to a C=O: O–H is an alcohol (or phenol on a ring), C–O–C is an ether, N is an amine, S–H is a thiol. Finish with the carbon skeleton: C=C, C≡C, and benzene rings. Circle each group as you find it so none gets counted twice. One trap: the N of an amide and the O of an ester are part of the carbonyl group, because the C=O shares its electrons with the neighboring N or O, so they are treated as one unit and are not counted again as an amine or an alcohol/ether.

In this course

The source text introduces five families here (alkanes, alkenes, alkynes, alcohols, carboxylic acids) and says that alkanes have no functional group. Both statements are correct, and in this course you go further from day one: learn every row of the table above now, because exam questions in Chapter 1 already ask you to circle and name the groups in a drug or biomolecule. The "alkane" row is the no-functional-group baseline: a molecule with only C–C and C–H single bonds. Everything else is defined by a heteroatom, a multiple bond, or both.

Example 1: Finding the functional groups in a drug

Problem

Acetaminophen (the active ingredient in Tylenol) is shown below as a line structure. Identify every functional group, then predict whether it can hydrogen bond with water.

Solution

  1. Carbonyl first: there is one C=O, and its carbon is bonded to a CH3 and to a nitrogen. A C=O with N attached is an amide.
  2. Other O, N, S: the only remaining oxygen is an O–H bonded directly to the ring. An O–H on a benzene ring is a phenol. The nitrogen is already part of the amide, so it is not counted again as an amine.
  3. Skeleton: the six-membered ring with alternating double bonds is an aromatic ring.

Functional groups: amide, phenol, aromatic ring. The molecule has an O–H and an N–H, so it can both donate and accept hydrogen bonds with water. Yes, it hydrogen bonds with water.

Check

Count the hidden hydrogens: four ring C–H, three on the CH3, plus the O–H and N–H, giving C8H9NO2, the known formula of acetaminophen. Every O and N belongs to exactly one functional group. ✓
