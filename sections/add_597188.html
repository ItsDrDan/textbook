Lecture Connection

The lecture sorts every reaction in this unit into two piles, catabolism (energy released) and anabolism (energy absorbed), places each pathway in one of three stages, and then asks you to learn the coenzymes by their jobs: who carries electrons, who carries phosphate, who carries an acetyl group. Get those three habits down here and the rest of Chapters 13–15 is bookkeeping.

Catabolism, anabolism, and the shape of a pathway

  • Catabolism breaks large molecules into smaller ones and is generally exothermic: energy is a product and the energy change is negative. The electrons removed from the fuel are caught by NAD+ and FAD, and some of the energy is banked as ATP.
  • Anabolism builds large molecules from small ones and is generally endothermic: energy is a reactant and the energy change is positive. The energy usually arrives as ATP, and the reducing power as NADPH.
  • A linear pathway (glycolysis, Section 13.5) ends in a product that is different from every reactant. A cyclic pathway (the citric acid cycle, Chapter 14) regenerates its own first reactant.
  • Metabolic reactions run at body temperature and physiological pH, so every step needs an enzyme and most need a coenzyme.

Practice: A reaction releases energy. Is it exothermic or endothermic? Is energy a product or a reactant? Is the energy change positive or negative? Then answer the same three questions for a reaction that absorbs energy.

Answer
Releases energy: exothermic, energy is a product, the energy change is negative (catabolic reactions usually look like this). Absorbs energy: endothermic, energy is a reactant, the energy change is positive (anabolic reactions usually look like this).

The three stages, the lecture way

  • Stage 1, digestion and hydrolysis: large molecules are broken down to small ones that enter the bloodstream (polysaccharides → monosaccharides; fats → fatty acids + glycerol; proteins → amino acids). No useful energy is captured.
  • Stage 2, degradation: the small molecules are broken down to two- and three-carbon compounds. For glucose that is glycolysis (to three-carbon pyruvate) and then pyruvate oxidation to two-carbon acetyl CoA, the common product of carbohydrate, fat, and protein breakdown. A little ATP and NADH are made.
  • Stage 3, oxidation: the citric acid cycle oxidizes acetyl CoA to CO2 while loading NADH and FADH2, and electron transport uses O2 to turn those carriers into most of the cell’s ATP (Chapter 14).

Where it happens is the only cell biology you need: the cytosol (the fluid inside the cell) is the site of glycolysis, glycogen metabolism, and the pentose phosphate pathway; the mitochondrion is where pyruvate becomes acetyl CoA and where all of Stage 3 runs; ribosomes are where proteins are made (Chapter 12).

Professor Tip

On exams, use three stages: Stage 1 digestion; Stage 2 degradation to acetyl CoA (glycolysis plus pyruvate oxidation); Stage 3 the citric acid cycle plus electron transport and oxidative phosphorylation (the 3i, 3ii and 3iii above). Say cytosol for the location of glycolysis. 30.5 kJ/mol and 7.3 kcal/mol are the same quantity.

The coenzymes: know their jobs

The section above defines oxidation and reduction (loss or gain of electrons, oxygen, or hydrogen). The lecture adds the rule that keeps students out of trouble: a coenzyme acting as an oxidizing agent makes the substrate get oxidized, so the coenzyme itself is reduced (it gains H+ and e−); a coenzyme acting as a reducing agent is itself oxidized. Five carriers do most of the work, and four of them are built from a B vitamin, which is why vitamin deficiencies show up as energy problems. The table lists each one, its job, and the clue that tells you it is at work.

NADH and NADPH are chemically almost twins, but the cell keeps them separate: NADH hands electrons to the electron transport chain to make ATP; NADPH hands electrons to biosynthesis. NAD+ and FAD are each a dinucleotide, two nucleotides joined through their phosphates, which is where the “D” in each acronym comes from.

Professor Tip

Use the Latin roots and the pathway names stop looking alike: -lysis = loosening, splitting apart; -neo- = new; -gen- = birth, origin; -esis = action, process; glyco- = glucose or glycogen. So glycolysis is breaking apart glucose, gluconeogenesis is creating new glucose, glycogenesis is the process of making glycogen, and glycogenolysis is breaking apart glycogen.

Example 1: Identify the coenzyme

Problem. In one step of the citric acid cycle, the C–OH group of malate is converted to a C=O. Which coenzyme is used?

Solution. An alcohol is oxidized to a carbonyl, so the coenzyme is NAD+, which is reduced to NADH.

Check: The substrate lost two hydrogens (one from O–H, one from C–H) and gained no oxygen atoms. That is the NAD+ signature; FAD is for C–C → C=C.

Example 2: Anabolic or catabolic?

Problem. Classify: (a) glycogen → glucose-1-phosphate; (b) lactate → glucose (in the liver).

Solution. (a) Breaking down: catabolic (glycogenolysis). (b) Building glucose: anabolic (gluconeogenesis).

Check: (a) goes polymer → monomer; (b) goes 3-carbon → 6-carbon and costs ATP. Size and energy direction agree with the labels.

Health Career Connection

Vitamins are coenzymes. Niacin, riboflavin, pantothenic acid, and thiamine become NAD+, FAD, coenzyme A, and the thiamine cofactor of pyruvate dehydrogenase (Section 13.4). A patient with poor intake (alcohol use disorder, malabsorption after bariatric surgery, a severely restricted diet) can present with fatigue, neuropathy, or a rising lactate that is really a vitamin problem: the fuel is there, but the carriers that move its electrons are not. That is why thiamine is given before or with IV dextrose in a patient suspected of deficiency, and why a dental hygienist who sees glossitis (a smooth, sore tongue) or angular cheilitis (cracks at the mouth corners) thinks of riboflavin and niacin.

Summary

  • Metabolism is all the chemical reactions in a cell: catabolism breaks molecules down and releases energy; anabolism builds molecules and uses energy.
  • Food catabolism has three stages: digestion (Stage 1), degradation to acetyl CoA (Stage 2), and the citric acid cycle with electron transport and oxidative phosphorylation (Stage 3).
  • Glycolysis takes place in the cytosol; Stage 3 takes place in the mitochondria.
  • ATP stores and transfers energy (7.3 kcal/mol on hydrolysis); NAD+ and FAD carry electrons and hydrogen as NADH and FADH2.
