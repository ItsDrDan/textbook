Lecture Connection

In lecture, amide hydrolysis is "cut the C(=O)–N bond, then ask what each piece does under the conditions." The carbonyl side always becomes the acid part and the nitrogen side always becomes the amine part; acid or base decides which one ends up as a salt.

Predicting the products

Amides are the least reactive of the carboxylic acid derivatives. They are stable in water, but with heat and an acid or a base (or a specific enzyme in the body) water splits the C–N bond, the reverse of amidation; amides need more vigorous conditions than esters do. Use this table to predict the products under each set of conditions.

Professor Tip

It works just like esters: the C=O side came from the carboxylic acid and the N side came from the amine. The amide bond is also the peptide bond that links amino acids in every protein (Chapter 10). Amides are much harder to hydrolyze than esters: in the lab you need strong acid or base and prolonged heating, which is why proteins and nylon are so durable, and why your body relies on enzymes (proteases, amidases) to do the job at 37 °C. Practice: which undergoes acid hydrolysis to give acetic acid, CH3CO2CH3, CH3CONH2, or CH3CONHCH3? All three; the CH3CO– side is the acid part in each.

In this course

The worked example in the section text writes the products of "hydrolysis" as the neutral acid and ammonia (butyramide → butyric acid + ammonia) without naming the conditions. On exams you will always be told acid or base, and your answer must show the salt: in acid, butanamide gives butanoic acid + NH4+Cl−; in base it gives sodium butanoate + NH3. Use the IUPAC names (butanamide, butanoic acid), not butyramide and butyric acid.

Amide drugs: the link decides the metabolism

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Amide bonds in drugs survive stomach acid for the hour or two they spend there and are hydrolyzed later by liver enzymes, which is why amide drugs such as acetaminophen and lidocaine can be given by mouth or be expected to last. Ester drugs, by contrast, are attacked quickly by esterases in plasma. Liver disease slows amide hydrolysis, so a patient's liver enzymes (ALT/AST) matter when these drugs are dosed; that judgment belongs to the prescriber, but the chemistry behind it is this section.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Local anesthetics come in two chemical families, and the name tells you which: amide-type drugs have an "i" before the "-caine" (lidocaine, articaine, mepivacaine, prilocaine, bupivacaine); ester-type drugs do not (benzocaine, procaine). Every one of them contains a 3° amine (benzocaine is the exception, with an aromatic 1° amine) joined through a short chain to an aromatic ring by either an amide or an ester link. The link decides the metabolism: esters are hydrolyzed quickly by esterases in plasma and release p-aminobenzoic acid (PABA), a known allergen, which is why ester anesthetics are now mostly limited to topical use; amides are hydrolyzed slowly by liver enzymes, so liver disease matters in the health history.

In the Lab

Amide hydrolysis: heating an amide with NaOH releases ammonia (or an amine). You detect it by its odor and by moist red litmus turning blue, which is the base-hydrolysis row of the table above happening in your test tube: the nitrogen leaves as the free base, not as a salt.
