Lecture Connection

This is the "draw it at three pH values" section. The whole skill is one rule: below the pI everything that can be protonated is; above the pI everything that can lose an H+ has. Learn the zwitterion as your reference point and count charges last.

The zwitterion

An amino acid contains both an acid (–COOH) and a base (–NH2) in the same molecule, so it reacts with itself: the carboxyl group hands its H+ to the amine. The result is a zwitterion, a molecule that carries a positive charge (–NH3+) and a negative charge (–COO−) at the same time but has a net charge of zero. That is why amino acids behave like salts: they are crystalline solids with high melting points and dissolve in water but not in nonpolar solvents.

  • At physiological pH (about 7.4) an amino acid with a neutral R group is a zwitterion: –NH3+ and –COO−, net charge 0.
  • In strong acid (low pH), the COO− picks up H+ → –COOH. Net charge +1.
  • In strong base (high pH), the NH3+ loses H+ → –NH2. Net charge −1.

Think of the two ends as switches: the carboxyl switch flips from –COOH to –COO− at about pH 2, and the amino switch flips from –NH3+ to –NH2 at about pH 9–10. Between the two flips (which includes blood at pH 7.4) the zwitterion is the main form.

Professor Tip

Use the zwitterion as your reference point and remember the lecture rule: at a pH below the pI the functional groups are protonated; at the pI the amino acid is the zwitterion; at a pH above the pI the functional groups are deprotonated. "Acid adds H+, base takes H+." Low pH: everything that can be protonated is. High pH: everything that can lose an H+ has. Count charges last: +1 for each –NH3+, −1 for each –COO−, and add them up.

Where the pI sits, and how a side chain moves it

The pI sits halfway between the pH at which the carboxyl group gives up its H+ (about 2) and the pH at which the ammonium group gives up its H+ (about 9–10), which is why neutral amino acids have pI values near 6. A charged side chain moves the pI. Aspartate and glutamate carry a second carboxyl group that is also deprotonated at neutral pH, so they reach net zero only in quite acidic solution: pI ≈ 3.0 for Asp and 3.2 for Glu. At pH 7.4 both are well above their pI and carry a net charge of −1 (two –COO− and one –NH3+). Lysine and arginine carry a second basic nitrogen that stays protonated until the pH is high, so their pI values are about 9.7 and 10.8; at pH 7.4 they are below their pI and net +1. Histidine's imidazole ring is a weaker base, giving pI ≈ 7.6, so histidine is close to neutral in blood and is the side chain that does most of a protein's buffering.

In this course

The section text gives the pI range of neutral amino acids as 5.0–6.5 and lists lysine at 9.8. Use the lecture rule of thumb on exams: most amino acids have a pI of about 5.0–6.0; an acidic R group pulls the pI below 4.0; a basic R group pushes it above 6.0. The decimals (9.7 versus 9.8 for lysine) never matter; the pattern does. You will always be given the pI if a question depends on the exact number.

Three rules cover every exam question about pI:

  • pH below pI → the solution is more acidic than the zwitterion "wants," so an extra H+ sits on the carboxyl group: –COOH and –NH3+, net positive (+1 for a neutral R group).
  • pH at pI → zwitterion, –COO− and –NH3+, net zero.
  • pH above pI → H+ has been removed from the ammonium group: –COO− and –NH2, net negative (−1 for a neutral R group).

Electrophoresis: charge you can see

Because charge depends on pH, a mixture of amino acids (or proteins) can be separated by placing it in a buffer of known pH on a gel between two electrodes. Anything with a net negative charge migrates toward the positive electrode, anything positive toward the negative electrode, and a molecule sitting at its pI does not move. At pH 6.0, for example, glutamate (net −) moves toward the positive electrode, lysine (net +) toward the negative electrode, and alanine (at its pI) stays put. Clinical labs use the same principle to separate hemoglobin variants such as sickle hemoglobin (HbS), which has lost a negative charge (Glu → Val) and therefore migrates more slowly toward the positive electrode than normal HbA, and to separate serum proteins into albumin and globulin fractions. A protein is also least soluble at its pI, because with no net charge the molecules no longer repel one another and clump; that is how casein is precipitated from milk in the lab by adding acid to pH 4.6 (see Section 10.3).

Worked example

Example 1: Charge of alanine relative to its pI

Problem. Alanine has pI ≈ 6.0. Draw its predominant form and give its net charge at pH 2, pH 6 and pH 10. In an electrophoresis gel buffered at each pH, which electrode would it move toward?

Solution. Compare each pH with the pI.

  • pH 2 (below pI): the solution is acidic enough to keep H+ on the carboxyl group. Form: +H3N–CH(CH3)–COOH. Charges: +1 and 0, net +1. It migrates toward the negative electrode.
  • pH 6 (at pI): zwitterion, +H3N–CH(CH3)–COO−. Charges +1 and −1, net 0. It does not move.
  • pH 10 (above pI): base has removed the H+ from the ammonium group. Form: H2N–CH(CH3)–COO−. Charges 0 and −1, net −1. It migrates toward the positive electrode.

Check. Charge should fall as pH rises (+1 → 0 → −1), and the only form with both charges is the one at the pI. If you had put glutamate (pI 3.2) in the pH 6 gel instead, pH 6 would be above its pI, so it would carry a net negative charge and move toward the positive electrode while alanine stayed put. That difference is how electrophoresis separates them.
