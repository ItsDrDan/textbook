Lecture Connection

In lecture a heterocyclic amine is defined in three parts: a cyclic organic compound, with a five- or six-atom ring, that contains one or more nitrogen atoms in the ring. The goal is recognition: you should be able to spot these rings inside the nucleic acid bases, amino acids, vitamins, and drugs you meet later in the course.

The six rings to recognize

Saturated examples are pyrrolidine (five-membered) and piperidine (six-membered). The aromatic ones below are the rings you will keep meeting in nucleic acids, amino acids, and drugs.

The table lists where each ring shows up. The molecules will come back in Chapters 10–12.

Professor Tip

Two habits make these rings easy. First, count atoms in the ring (five or six) and count nitrogens; that alone separates pyridine from pyrimidine and pyrrole from imidazole. Second, decide whether the ring nitrogen still owns its lone pair. A six-membered ring N (pyridine, pyrimidine) does, so it is weakly basic and can be protonated; an N–H in a five-membered aromatic ring (pyrrole, indole) has given its lone pair to the ring and is not basic. Nicotine shows both kinds at once.

The caffeine, nicotine, and cocaine in the section text are alkaloids, heterocyclic amines made by plants; Section 7.9 covers how their basicity is used to extract them and why they are sold as hydrochloride salts. The adenosine, acetylcholine, and dopamine the text mentions as messengers are neurotransmitters, the subject of Section 7.11.
