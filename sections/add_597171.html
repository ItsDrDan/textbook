Lecture Connection

A cell cannot let every enzyme run at full speed all the time. It needs fast switches (seconds) and slow ones (hours). This section is the fast ones: allosteric control and feedback inhibition. Section 11.7 covers covalent modification (seconds to minutes), zymogen activation (minutes) and the truly slow switch, genetic control (hours to days).

Allosteric control: a regulator at a different site

An allosteric enzyme has, besides its active site, one or more regulatory sites (allos = other). A regulator molecule binds there and changes the enzyme's shape. Allosteric control is the binding of a regulator to a site on the enzyme that affects the enzyme's ability to bind substrate at its active site. Negative allosteric control: the regulator makes the active site less able to bind substrate and the rate drops (like a noncompetitive inhibitor, the regulator binds away from the active site, but it is the cell’s own reversible switch, not a drug or poison; feedback inhibition, below, works this way). Positive allosteric control: the regulator makes the active site more able to bind substrate and the rate rises. The response is fast and reversible: the regulator leaves and the enzyme snaps back. Example: ATP, the cell's energy currency, is a negative regulator of phosphofructokinase in glycolysis, so a cell with plenty of ATP slows glucose breakdown (Chapter 13).

Professor Tip

Picture the allosteric site as the inside of a shoe. A negative regulator is a nail in the shoe: once it is in, the shoe (the active site) no longer fits the foot (the substrate) and you slow down. A positive regulator is a memory-foam insole: it molds the shoe so the foot fits better and you speed up. Either way the regulator is in the shoe, not on the foot, which is what makes the control allosteric (at a different site).

Feedback inhibition: the end product shuts off the first step

The end product of a pathway is a negative allosteric regulator of the first enzyme unique to that pathway. When the product piles up, the whole pathway shuts off at its first step, so no intermediates are wasted; when the product is used up, the pathway restarts. This is how bacteria stop making an amino acid once they have enough of it (the isoleucine example in the section text); your own cells use the same logic in nucleotide and amino acid synthesis.

Professor Tip

Name the mechanism this way on exams: feedback inhibition (or feedback control) acting through an allosteric site on the first enzyme of the pathway. It is reversible and takes seconds. Keep the three inhibitor types of Section 11.4 (competitive, noncompetitive, irreversible) for questions about drugs and poisons; keep “allosteric” and “feedback” for questions about how a cell regulates its own pathways.

How the fast switches compare with the slow ones, and the worked examples that sort all five, are in the Chapter Guide and in Section 11.7.

Summary

  • An allosteric enzyme has a regulatory site separate from its active site. An activator bound there increases activity; an inhibitor decreases it.
  • In feedback inhibition, the end product of a pathway binds the allosteric site of the first enzyme and slows the whole pathway.
  • This control is reversible and acts within seconds, so a cell makes only as much product as it needs.
