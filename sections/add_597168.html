Lecture Connection

You should be able to sketch and read four graphs: rate versus substrate concentration, rate versus enzyme concentration, rate versus temperature, and rate versus pH. The lecture question is always "what happens to the rate if I change X, and is the change reversible?" Collisions go up and down reversibly; damage to the active site does not.

Four variables, four curves

Enzyme activity means the rate of the reaction it catalyzes, usually measured as product formed per minute. One way to state it is the turnover number, the maximum number of substrate molecules one enzyme molecule converts per second (anywhere from 10 to 10,000,000). Because an enzyme is a protein, it undergoes everything a protein does, including denaturation, so temperature and pH change its activity. You will test all four variables in Experiment 13.

  • Temperature: as temperature rises, molecules collide more often and harder, so the rate rises, reaching a maximum at the optimum temperature (about 37 °C for human enzymes). Above the optimum, heat breaks the hydrogen bonds and other weak interactions that hold the tertiary structure, the active site loses its shape, and the rate falls steeply: the enzyme is denatured (Chapter 10). Cooling does not denature; it just slows the enzyme, and activity returns on warming.
  • pH: each enzyme has an optimum pH at which the acidic and basic R groups in and around the active site carry the charges needed to bind the substrate. Pepsin (stomach) works best near pH 2; trypsin (small intestine) near pH 8; most enzymes inside cells near pH 7.4. Away from the optimum, the charges change (–COO− becomes –COOH, –NH3+ becomes –NH2), the substrate no longer fits, and even a shift of less than one pH unit can sharply reduce activity; far from the optimum, the enzyme denatures. That is why cells and blood are buffered to hold the pH near each enzyme's optimum.
  • Substrate concentration: at fixed enzyme concentration, the rate rises quickly as substrate is added, then levels off at a maximum rate when every active site is occupied, a condition called saturation. Beyond that, extra substrate has nowhere to bind.
  • Enzyme concentration: with substrate in excess, the rate is directly proportional to the amount of enzyme: twice the enzyme, twice the rate. This is the basis of clinical enzyme assays, where the measured rate in a blood sample tells you how much enzyme leaked out of damaged cells.

Professor Tip

On an exam, draw the optimum for a human enzyme at 37 °C and label the drop beyond it "denaturation"; you do not need the exact temperature at which a given enzyme unfolds. The rule that a 10 °C rise roughly doubles the rate applies only on the rising side of the curve.

Worked examples

Example 1: Reading the temperature effect

Problem. A human enzyme is assayed at 37 °C and then at 65 °C. Predict the activity at 65 °C and explain.

Solution. Activity drops sharply. Above its optimum the enzyme denatures, losing the tertiary structure that forms the active site.

Check. Contrast with 10 °C: the enzyme would be slow but intact, and activity would return at 37 °C. Heat damage does not reverse.

Example 2: Predict the effect of each change on rate

Problem. Lactase, the intestinal enzyme that hydrolyzes lactose, has an optimum pH of about 6 and works at 37 °C. Describe the effect of each change on the rate of the lactase-catalyzed reaction: (a) adding more lactose when the enzyme is not yet saturated; (b) adding more lactose when it is already saturated; (c) halving the amount of lactase; (d) cooling the mixture to 5 °C; (e) raising the pH from 6 to 9; (f) heating to 80 °C and then cooling back to 37 °C.

Solution.

  1. Increases. More substrate means more collisions with empty active sites.
  2. No change. Every active site is already busy; extra substrate must wait its turn (the plateau of the rate-vs-[S] curve).
  3. Decreases (to about half). With substrate in excess, rate is proportional to enzyme concentration.
  4. Decreases. Molecules move more slowly and collide less often; the enzyme is intact and recovers on warming.
  5. Decreases. Three units above the optimum, the charges on the active-site R groups change and the substrate no longer binds well.
  6. Stays near zero. 80 °C denatures the enzyme; cooling does not refold it.

Check. Only (a) raises the rate. Ask for each change whether it adds collisions (substrate, enzyme, moderate warming) or damages the active site (pH far from optimum, excess heat); the second kind is not undone by reversing the condition.

Enzymes as blood tests

Enzymes stay inside healthy cells, so an enzyme in the blood is a sign of damaged cells, and which enzyme tells you which tissue. The lab measures the rate of the enzyme's reaction in a sample, and because rate is proportional to enzyme concentration (the fourth curve above), the rate reports how much enzyme leaked out.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Temperature and the patient. A fever of 39 °C does not denature human enzymes; above about 41–42 °C (hyperthermia, as in heat stroke) they begin to unfold, which is why rapid cooling is an emergency. The same chemistry works in reverse: a donor kidney or heart is packed in ice-cold solution because near 4 °C its enzymes run so slowly that the organ uses little oxygen and lasts hours instead of minutes. Sterilization works by the same principle as a fever gone too far: the autoclave's pressurized steam at 121 °C denatures every microbial enzyme, which no cold disinfectant can guarantee.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Chemistry of the operatory. Chlorhexidine rinse denatures bacterial proteins and membranes. The hydrogen peroxide in bleaching gel is decomposed to water and oxygen by salivary peroxidase and tissue catalase, both oxidoreductases, which is why gel must be isolated from saliva and soft tissue. Fluoride, besides promoting remineralization, inhibits enolase in bacterial glycolysis, so plaque makes less acid; and plaque acid itself matters here because the enzymes of plaque bacteria keep working down to a pH that demineralizes enamel (critical pH about 5.5).
