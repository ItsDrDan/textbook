Learning Objectives

  • Explain how the 2′-OH and uracil make RNA chemically different from DNA, and why RNA is single-stranded but still folds into base-paired stems and loops.
  • Describe the structure and job of messenger RNA, transfer RNA, and ribosomal RNA, and identify each from a description.
  • Given a tRNA anticodon, write the codon it pairs with and name the amino acid.
  • Explain how the three RNAs cooperate during the stages of translation (activation, initiation, elongation, termination) and how the spliceosome edits pre-mRNA.
  • Connect RNA chemistry to mRNA vaccines and RNA viruses.

Most of the nucleic acid in a cell is not DNA. By mass, a working cell holds several times more RNA than DNA, and nearly all of it is busy: carrying messages, hauling amino acids, or building proteins. RNA is also the material of mRNA vaccines and the genome of influenza, SARS-CoV-2, and HIV. This section looks at what makes RNA different from DNA and at what each kind of RNA does.

RNA chemistry: one extra OH changes everything

RNA is a polymer of ribonucleotides joined by the same 3′→5′ phosphodiester backbone as DNA (Section 12.2). Two small changes in the monomers account for every difference in behavior.

  • The sugar is ribose. Each sugar carries an OH on C2′. That OH sits right next to the 3′ phosphate ester and can attack it, cutting the backbone (the phosphate ester is hydrolyzed with help from the neighboring OH). The practical result is that RNA falls apart in mildly basic solution and at warm temperatures, while DNA, with no 2′-OH, lasts for years. DNA is the archive; RNA is the sticky note.
  • Uracil replaces thymine. Uracil pairs with adenine exactly as thymine does (two hydrogen bonds); it simply lacks the methyl group. Because DNA never contains uracil, any uracil that appears in DNA (for example, when a cytosine is deaminated by a mutagen such as nitrous acid) is recognized by repair enzymes as damage and replaced.
  • It is single-stranded, but not unpaired. RNA is made as one strand, so nothing forces %A to equal %U. But wherever two stretches of the same strand happen to be complementary, the strand folds back and they pair, forming a short double helix called a stem closed by a loop of unpaired bases: a hairpin. A long RNA can contain dozens of hairpins, and the way they pack together gives each RNA a specific three-dimensional shape, much as hydrogen bonds and side-chain interactions fold a protein (Chapter 10). Shape is what lets rRNA and tRNA act like machines rather than messages.

Every RNA in a cell is made by transcription (Section 12.4): RNA polymerase reads the template strand of a gene and assembles ribonucleotides 5′→3′. Unlike DNA polymerase, RNA polymerase does not proofread, which matters little for a molecule that will be recycled within hours, but matters a great deal for viruses whose genome is RNA (see Example 3). Lecture sums up the four differences between RNA and DNA as: sugar (ribose vs deoxyribose), base (uracil vs thymine), strands (single vs double), and size (RNA is much smaller).

Professor Tip

Students often picture RNA as "half a DNA." Picture it instead as a strand of DNA that has been handed a 2′-OH and told to go fold itself. The OH explains the fragility; the folding explains the function. If a question asks why RNA does anything DNA does not, one of those two ideas is the answer.

Messenger RNA

Messenger RNA (mRNA) carries the genetic information for one protein from the DNA in the nucleus to the ribosomes in the cytoplasm. Its bases are read in groups of three, the codons, starting at AUG and ending at a stop codon (Section 12.5). In human cells the new transcript, the pre-mRNA, is edited before it leaves the nucleus: the non-coding introns are cut out and the exons joined (see splicing below). Most mRNAs last minutes to a few hours, which is what lets a cell change which proteins it makes as conditions change.

Transfer RNA

Transfer RNA (tRNA) is the adapter that makes the genetic code work. Nothing about the codon GCA looks like alanine, so something has to connect the two, and tRNA does it with two ends:

  • The anticodon, three bases in the bottom loop of the cloverleaf, identifies the amino acid the tRNA carries and pairs antiparallel, by hydrogen bonding, with the codon on the mRNA. A tRNA whose anticodon is 3′-CGU-5′ pairs with the codon 5′-GCA-3′.
  • The acceptor stem at the 3′ end, which always ends in the sequence CCA, carries the amino acid. The amino acid's carboxyl group is joined to a free –OH of the final ribose as an aminoacyl ester (Chapter 6). This is a high-energy ester; its hydrolysis later pays for making the peptide bond.

Who decides which amino acid goes on which tRNA? A family of enzymes, the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases (ligases, Chapter 11), one for each amino acid. In this activation step each synthetase recognizes the shape of its amino acid and the shape of its tRNAs and joins them, using the energy of ATP hydrolysis. This is the real "translation" step: once alanine is esterified to a tRNA with the anticodon for GCA, base pairing on the ribosome does the rest. A tRNA carrying its amino acid is called an aminoacyl-tRNA.

Ribosomal RNA

Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) is the most abundant RNA in any cell. A ribosome is an rRNA–protein complex: four rRNA molecules fold together with about 80 proteins (each subunit is roughly 65% rRNA and 35% protein) to form the two subunits of the ribosome, the site of translation. The small subunit binds the mRNA; the large subunit holds the tRNAs and contains the active site that joins the amino acids. For decades it was assumed that the proteins did the chemistry, but that active site, the peptidyl transferase center, turns out to be mostly rRNA. Because of this the ribosome is called a ribozyme: an enzyme made of RNA.

Other RNAs: splicing and regulation

Human genes are segmented. The stretches that express the genetic information, the exons, are separated by non-coding stretches in between, the introns, and both are transcribed together into the pre-mRNA. Before the mRNA leaves the nucleus the spliceosome, a complex of small RNAs and proteins, recognizes the boundary sequences by base pairing, cuts out each intron, and seals the exons together: splicing. The product is the mature mRNA. A mutation that changes a boundary sequence can leave an intron in place or skip an exon, which is why some inherited diseases are splicing mutations rather than changes in a codon. Because exons can be joined in more than one pattern, one gene can give several related proteins.

Transcription itself is regulated: a cell makes a given mRNA only when it needs the protein, slowing it by feedback control when the end product is plentiful and switching it on by enzyme induction when a reactant is abundant (Section 11.7).

How the three RNAs cooperate

Translation is a team effort, and each RNA brings one thing. mRNA brings the information. tRNA brings the amino acids, already matched to their codons by the synthetases. rRNA brings the workbench and the catalyst. The stages, in the words used in lecture:

  1. Activation: each aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase attaches its amino acid to its tRNA as an aminoacyl ester, using ATP.
  2. Initiation: the mRNA binds the small ribosomal subunit, and the first tRNA, carrying methionine, pairs with the start codon AUG at the P site (peptidyl site).
  3. Elongation: the next aminoacyl-tRNA pairs with the next codon at the A site (aminoacyl site); peptidyl transferase links the P-site amino acid to the A-site amino acid with a new peptide bond; the empty tRNA leaves; the ribosome shifts three bases along the mRNA, a translocation, so the growing chain sits at the P site and the A site is open again. Repeat for every codon.
  4. Termination: when a stop codon (UAA, UAG or UGA) reaches the A site, no tRNA fits; the finished polypeptide is released from the last tRNA by hydrolysis of the ester, the initial methionine is usually removed, and the chain folds into its secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure (Chapter 10).

Many ribosomes can read the same mRNA at once, one behind another, forming a polysome; reusing a message this way saves the energy of transcribing it again.

Worked examples

Example 1: Identify the RNA

Problem: Identify each RNA from the description. (a) A molecule of about 75 nucleotides with a CCA at its 3′ end and an amino acid attached there as an ester. (b) The most abundant RNA in a cell; part of it catalyzes peptide bond formation. (c) A molecule carrying a series of codons, with a reading frame that starts with AUG. (d) A short RNA in the nucleus that pairs with the boundary of an intron.

Solution

(a) tRNA: small, CCA end, carries an amino acid. (b) rRNA: most abundant and the ribozyme of the ribosome. (c) mRNA: codons read from AUG. (d) a small RNA of the spliceosome.

Check: Each description names a feature that only one RNA has: an attached amino acid (tRNA), catalysis in the ribosome (rRNA), codons (mRNA), intron boundaries (spliceosome RNA).

Example 2: From anticodon to amino acid

Problem: A tRNA has the anticodon 3′-ACC-5′. Write the mRNA codon it pairs with and name the amino acid this tRNA should carry.

Solution

The anticodon pairs antiparallel with the codon, so write the complement in the opposite direction: 3′-ACC-5′ pairs with 5′-UGG-3′. In the genetic code (Section 12.5), UGG codes for tryptophan (Trp). The tryptophan-tRNA synthetase attaches Trp to this tRNA's 3′ end.

Check: Line them up: codon 5′-U G G-3′ over anticodon 3′-A C C-5′. U pairs with A, G with C, G with C. If you had treated the anticodon as 5′-ACC-3′, you would have written the codon as 5′-GGU-3′ and gotten Gly, the wrong answer; direction matters.

Example 3: Why RNA viruses change so fast

Problem: Influenza needs a new vaccine most years, while the vaccine for hepatitis B, a DNA virus, has not changed since the 1980s. Explain using what you know about RNA polymerases.

Solution

An RNA virus copies its genome with its own RNA-dependent RNA polymerase. Like the cell's RNA polymerase, it has no proofreading: a wrong base is left in place. Its error rate is roughly one mistake per 10,000 to 100,000 bases, thousands of times higher than DNA replication with proofreading and repair. A flu genome is about 13,500 bases, so nearly every new virus particle carries a mutation somewhere. Over a season, mutations in the genes for the surface proteins accumulate until antibodies raised against last year's proteins no longer bind well, and the vaccine must be updated. A DNA virus such as hepatitis B is copied with far fewer errors, so its surface protein stays the same and the vaccine keeps working.

Check: The argument rests on two facts from this chapter: DNA polymerase proofreads, RNA polymerases do not. (Coronaviruses are a partial exception: they carry their own proofreading enzyme, which is one reason SARS-CoV-2 changes more slowly per base than influenza does.)

Clinical Connection: Nursing

An mRNA vaccine is this section in a vial. The active ingredient is a synthetic mRNA with a reading frame for one viral protein (the SARS-CoV-2 spike), packaged in lipid nanoparticles, tiny spheres of phospholipid and cholesterol (Chapter 9) that fuse with a cell membrane and deliver the message to the cytoplasm. Ribosomes translate it for a few days, the immune system learns the protein, and the mRNA is hydrolyzed; it never enters the nucleus and cannot be reverse-transcribed by human cells, which have no reverse transcriptase. The reason the first vaccines shipped at −70 °C is the chemistry of the 2′-OH: RNA cuts itself apart in water at room temperature, and nucleases, the hydrolases that digest RNA, are on every surface, which is why thawed vials are used within hours. Influenza and SARS-CoV-2 are RNA viruses that mutate quickly, which is why the flu vaccine is updated every year; HIV is a retrovirus whose reverse transcriptase, also without proofreading, generates drug-resistant variants unless several drugs are given together.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Two RNA viruses matter in the operatory: influenza and SARS-CoV-2 spread in the aerosols that ultrasonic scalers produce, which is the reason for high-volume evacuation, pre-procedural rinses, and the annual flu vaccine for clinicians. A saliva sample can be tested for these viruses because it carries their RNA; the laboratory first copies that RNA into DNA with reverse transcriptase and then amplifies the DNA by the polymerase chain reaction (PCR; see the recombinant DNA overview in the Chapter Guide). Saliva also contains nucleases, hydrolases that break down the RNA of viruses and of shed cells, which is part of the mouth's defense and part of why a saliva sample must be stabilized quickly. Finally, some antibiotics used in periodontal care work by binding bacterial ribosomes, which differ enough from human ribosomes in their rRNA that bacterial protein synthesis stops while the patient's own continues; the selectivity comes from a difference in the machine, not in the genetic code, which is the same in both.

Summary

  • RNA differs from DNA by a 2′-OH on each ribose, which makes the backbone easy to hydrolyze, and by uracil in place of thymine. It is single-stranded but folds into base-paired stems and loops (hairpins) that give it shape.
  • mRNA carries the codons for one protein from the nucleus to the ribosome; it is edited by splicing (introns out, exons joined) and is short-lived.
  • tRNA is the adapter: its anticodon pairs antiparallel with a codon, and the matching amino acid is attached as an ester at its 3′ CCA end by an aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase.
  • rRNA is the most abundant RNA; it forms the two ribosomal subunits and catalyzes peptide bond formation (the ribosome is a ribozyme).
  • Translation runs in four stages: activation (synthetase + ATP), initiation (Met-tRNA at AUG in the P site), elongation (A site, peptidyl transferase, translocation) and termination (stop codon, hydrolysis releases the chain). Many ribosomes on one mRNA form a polysome.
  • RNA polymerases do not proofread, so RNA viruses mutate quickly. mRNA vaccines must be kept cold because of RNA hydrolysis and nucleases.

Exercises

Exercise 1

List three structural differences between RNA and DNA, and state which one explains why RNA solutions must be kept cold.

Answer
(1) Ribose instead of deoxyribose (a 2′-OH is present); (2) uracil instead of thymine; (3) a single strand that folds on itself instead of a double helix. The 2′-OH explains the fragility: it attacks the neighboring phosphate ester and cuts the backbone, a reaction that is faster at higher temperature.

Exercise 2

An RNA sample is 28% A and 20% U. A student says this proves the sample is contaminated because A should equal U. Correct the student.

Answer
Chargaff's rule (%A = %T or %U) applies only to a fully base-paired double strand. RNA is single-stranded, and only the stretches that fold into stems are paired, so nothing requires %A to equal %U. The sample may be perfectly good.

Exercise 3

Name the RNA described: (a) the one that is used up and replaced within hours; (b) the one that is a ribozyme; (c) the one that is esterified to an amino acid; (d) the one that removes introns.

Answer
(a) mRNA; (b) rRNA (its peptidyl transferase center); (c) tRNA; (d) a small RNA of the spliceosome.

Exercise 4

A tRNA has the anticodon 3′-GUA-5′. Write the codon it reads and name the amino acid that should be attached to it. Which enzyme attaches it, and what kind of bond is formed?

Answer
Codon 5′-CAU-3′, which codes for histidine (His). Histidine-tRNA synthetase attaches His to the 3′-CCA end of the tRNA as an ester between the amino acid's carboxyl group and a ribose OH, using ATP.

Exercise 5

Draw (or describe) what happens to the RNA 5′-GGCAUCUUCGGAUGCC-3′ in solution. Which bases pair, and what is the underlined part?

Answer
The first six bases (GGCAUC) are complementary and antiparallel to the last six (GAUGCC): G–C, G–C, C–G, A–U, U–A, C–G. The strand folds back so these pair, forming a six-base-pair stem; the underlined UUCG is the unpaired loop. The structure is a hairpin.

Exercise 6

Match each term to its description: (1) P site, (2) A site, (3) peptidyl transferase, (4) translocation, (5) polysome. Descriptions: (A) the ribosome shifts along the mRNA by one codon; (B) where the incoming aminoacyl-tRNA pairs with the next codon; (C) several ribosomes reading one mRNA at the same time; (D) where the tRNA holding the growing peptide chain sits; (E) the rRNA active site that forms the peptide bond.

Answer
(1) D; (2) B; (3) E; (4) A; (5) C. After peptidyl transferase moves the chain onto the A-site amino acid, translocation shifts that tRNA into the P site and empties the A site for the next one.

Exercise 7

A patient asks whether an mRNA vaccine can "change my DNA." Give a two-sentence answer based on where the mRNA goes and what human cells can and cannot do with it.

Answer
The mRNA is delivered to the cytoplasm, where ribosomes translate it; it never enters the nucleus, where DNA is kept. Human cells have no reverse transcriptase to copy RNA into DNA and no way to insert it into a chromosome, and the mRNA is hydrolyzed within days.

Exercise 8

Compare and contrast: a mutation in the gene for a tRNA's anticodon versus a mutation in one codon of an mRNA. Which affects more proteins, and why?

Answer
A mutated codon in one mRNA changes (at most) one amino acid in one protein. A mutated anticodon changes which codon that tRNA reads, so the wrong amino acid would be inserted at every occurrence of that codon in every protein the cell makes. The tRNA mutation affects far more proteins because tRNA is a shared tool, while an mRNA is one message. (Cells usually survive such tRNA mutations only because several tRNA genes read the same codon.)
