Lecture Connection

Four glucose polymers

Polysaccharides contain many monosaccharide units. They come in three kinds: storage polysaccharides (starch, glycogen), structural polysaccharides (cellulose; chitin in insect shells), and acidic polysaccharides (hyaluronic acid in joint fluid). The four you must know are all long chains of glucose, and the differences that matter are the anomer in the link (α or β) and how much branching there is. Starch, the reserve carbohydrate of plants, is a mixture: about 20% amylose (water soluble) and 80% amylopectin (insoluble); hydrolysis of either gives only glucose.

PolysaccharideMonomerLinkage and shapeRole and notes
Amyloseglucoseα(1→4), linear (unbranched), up to about 4000 units; coils into a helixRoughly 20% of plant starch. The helix traps iodine (blue-black test). Digested by amylase (saliva, pancreas) to maltose, then by maltase (intestine) to glucose.
Amylopectinglucoseα(1→4) chains of about 24–30 units with α(1→6) branch pointsRoughly 80% of plant starch. Digested by amylase and maltase.
Glycogenglucoseα(1→4) with α(1→6) branches about every 10–15 units (more branched than amylopectin, and smaller)Animal storage form in liver and muscle. Glucose is hydrolyzed from the chain ends, and a highly branched molecule has many ends, so glucose is released quickly between meals.
Celluloseglucoseβ(1→4), linear, up to about 3000 units; straight chains hydrogen-bond side by side into fibersPlant cell walls (cotton is about 95% cellulose, wood about 50%), dietary fiber. The hydrogen bonding between chains gives the fibers strength and makes cellulose insoluble. Humans cannot digest it: we lack an enzyme for β(1→4) links. Cattle and termites rely on microbes that make cellulase.

Professor Tip

All four polysaccharides we cover are made of glucose. What makes them different is the α vs. β linkage and the branching, and that is exactly what decides whether we can digest them. A memory hook for the branching: the two branched ones both end in "n" (amylopectin, glycogen); amylose and cellulose are linear.

In this course

The section text gives branch points about every 25–30 units in amylopectin and every 8–12 in glycogen; the slides say 24–30 and 10–15. Exams never ask for the exact spacing, only the comparison: glycogen is more branched than amylopectin, and both use α(1→6) links at the branch points. The section's "dextrins" are the intermediate-size fragments of starch hydrolysis (starch → dextrins → maltose → glucose); you do not need them beyond knowing that sequence.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Heparin, the IV anticoagulant, is a sulfated polysaccharide of uronic acids and amino sugars, too large and charged to be absorbed from the gut, which is why it is given by injection and never by mouth. Glycogen is the reason a fasting patient keeps a normal blood glucose for most of a day: the liver hydrolyzes glucose from the many branch ends of its glycogen (Chapter 13 covers the enzymes).

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Two polysaccharides are tools of the trade: alginate impression material, a polymer of uronic acids from brown algae that gels with calcium ions, and carboxymethylcellulose, which thickens toothpaste and artificial saliva for patients with xerostomia (dry mouth). Starch is also a caries risk: salivary amylase hydrolyzes starch that sticks to teeth into maltose and glucose that plaque bacteria can ferment (Section 8.6).

In the Lab

Iodine test: amylose (starch) traps iodine in its helix and turns blue-black; as you hydrolyze starch with acid or amylase the color fades (and Benedict's test turns positive), which is how you follow the hydrolysis of a polysaccharide in Experiment 8b.
