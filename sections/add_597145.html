Lecture Connection

In lecture a triacylglycerol is Fischer esterification three times over: glycerol + 3 fatty acids → triester + 3 H2O. Four reactions follow from that: condensation to make it, hydrogenation of its C=C bonds, hydrolysis (acid or lipase) to take it apart, and saponification (base) to make soap. You must be able to draw every one of them from a fatty acid formula and the structure of glycerol.

Drawing a triacylglycerol

Triacylglycerols (triglycerides) are triesters of glycerol and three fatty acids: glycerol + 3 fatty acids → triacylglycerol + 3 H2O (three ester bonds). "Condensation: molecules join, a small molecule (H2O) is lost." This is Fischer esterification three times over, so everything you learned about esters in Chapter 6 applies. Read the name as tri-acyl-glycerol: three acyl (RC=O) groups on glycerol.

Professor Tip

You need to know the structure of glycerol from memory for this chapter and every exam after it: a three-carbon chain with an OH on each carbon, HOCH2–CHOH–CH2OH (1,2,3-propanetriol). Draw it vertically, with the three OH groups on the right, and the three fatty acids line up next to it; each OH becomes an ester link when the fatty acid's COOH condenses with it. To draw a triacylglycerol from a fatty acid formula such as CH3(CH2)12COOH (myristic acid), write glycerol's three carbons down the left, then –O–C(=O)(CH2)12CH3 on each one.

  • Fat vs. oil is a melting-point distinction, not a chemical one. A fat is solid at room temperature because most of its fatty acids are saturated (beef tallow, butter, lard). An oil is liquid because most of its chains are unsaturated (olive, corn, canola, fish oils). Natural triacylglycerols are mixed: the three chains are usually different. Coconut oil is the instructive exception: it is almost all saturated, yet it melts near 24 °C because its chains are short (lauric, 12:0).
  • Olestra, a "fat substitute," is sucrose with all eight OH groups esterified to fatty acids. The esters are too crowded for lipase to hydrolyze, so it is not absorbed and has no calories, but it also carries fat-soluble vitamins out of the body.

Professor Tip

Triglyceride and triacylglycerol mean the same thing; a clinical lab report (the lipid panel) says triglycerides. On exams, saponification means heating the fat with NaOH or KOH in one step, and the products you write are glycerol + three fatty acid salts. The industrial soap-making steps in the Looking Closer box are background.

The three reactions

  • Hydrogenation (H2, Ni catalyst, heat): adds H across C=C, turning liquid oils into semi-solid fats (margarine, shortening). Each mole of C=C consumes one mole of H2. Partial hydrogenation leaves some double bonds, and the catalyst flips a fraction of the remaining cis bonds to trans. Controlling the degree of hydrogenation controls the melting point of the product.
  • Acid or enzyme hydrolysis (H2O with H+, or the enzyme lipase): → glycerol + 3 fatty acids. "Hydrolysis: water splits the molecule." Lipases inside fat cells do this completely when stored fat is used for energy. During digestion, pancreatic lipase in the small intestine stops partway: it releases 2 fatty acids and leaves a monoacylglycerol (Chapter 15). Complete metabolism of the pieces yields CO2, H2O, and a great deal of energy (Chapter 15).
  • Saponification (NaOH or KOH, heat): → glycerol + 3 fatty acid salts (soap). Think of it as two steps in one: hydrolysis (water splits each ester into glycerol and the carboxylic acid) followed by an acid–base neutralization (each carboxylic acid reacts with the base to form a carboxylate salt and water). One mole of base is used per ester, so a triacylglycerol needs 3 mol of base. Soaps are the metal salts of fatty acids made this way.
  • Trans fats and health. A trans double bond is more stable than cis, and partially hydrogenated fats were added to processed foods for texture and shelf life. Because a trans chain has nearly the same straight shape as a saturated chain, trans fats act like saturated fats in the body: diets high in either raise the LDL-to-HDL cholesterol ratio and the risk of cardiovascular disease, and partially hydrogenated oils have been phased out of U.S. processed foods.

Why soap cleans. A soap molecule such as sodium stearate, CH3(CH2)16COO− Na+, is amphipathic: it has a polar, water-loving (hydrophilic) carboxylate head and a long nonpolar, water-fearing (hydrophobic) tail. In water, dozens of soap molecules cluster into a micelle, a ball with the tails inside and the heads facing the water. Grease dissolves into the nonpolar core, and the charged surface lets the whole ball wash away. (In hard water, Ca2+ and Mg2+ trade places with Na+ and give an insoluble "soap scum," the same salt-solubility idea from Chapter 6.) Keep the micelle in mind: bile salts (Section 9.6) and the phospholipid bilayer (Section 9.5) use the same head-and-tail logic.

Example 1: Products of saponifying a mixed triacylglycerol

Problem. A triacylglycerol carries palmitic acid on C1 of glycerol, oleic acid on C2, and stearic acid on C3. It is heated with excess KOH. Write every product, state how many moles of KOH react per mole of fat, and explain why the products dissolve in water when the starting fat did not.

Solution

Each of the three ester bonds is split by one OH−, so 3 mol KOH react per mole of fat. The alcohol part of every ester is the same glycerol, so one glycerol forms. The acid parts leave as potassium salts, one of each:

  • potassium palmitate, CH3(CH2)14COO− K+
  • potassium oleate, CH3(CH2)7CH=CH(CH2)7COO− K+
  • potassium stearate, CH3(CH2)16COO− K+

The fat was a large nonpolar molecule. Glycerol has three OH groups and hydrogen-bonds with water, and each carboxylate salt is ionic at one end: they dissolve (the salts form micelles). Base does not touch the C=C of oleate, so it survives as a cis double bond.

Check. Count: 1 fat + 3 KOH → 1 glycerol + 3 salts, four product molecules. Potassium soaps are softer and more soluble than sodium soaps, which is why liquid soaps use KOH.

Oxidation of fats (rancidity)

The C=C double bonds in unsaturated fats react with O2 and break into short-chain aldehydes and carboxylic acids that smell and taste "off." Antioxidants such as vitamin E and BHT slow this down, which is why oils are stored cool, dark, and sealed. A little more detail, without the mechanism. Oxygen attacks the CH2 that sits between two double bonds most easily, so the more polyunsaturated an oil is, the faster it spoils: fish oil and flaxseed oil go rancid in weeks, olive oil (mostly 18:1) lasts months, and a saturated fat like coconut oil lasts longest. The chain is cut at the double bond, and the fragments are volatile aldehydes (hexanal is the "stale oil" smell), which can be oxidized further to carboxylic acids. This is oxidative rancidity. A second kind, hydrolytic rancidity, is simple ester hydrolysis by moisture and microbial lipases; it releases free fatty acids, and in butter the short one, butyric acid (4:0), is the sour, "spoiled butter" odor. Antioxidants stop oxidative rancidity by giving up a hydrogen atom to the reactive intermediates before they can attack another chain. Vitamin E (α-tocopherol, a phenol) does this job inside cell membranes and in the lipid part of foods; the synthetic phenols BHT and BHA do it in packaged foods. Hydrogenation also slows rancidity, which was the original reason shortening and margarine were partially hydrogenated.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Diets high in saturated and trans fats raise LDL cholesterol and the risk of arterial plaque; saturated fats stimulate cholesterol synthesis, and plaque in arteries leads to high blood pressure, heart attack, and stroke. Omega-3 fatty acids (fish oils, EPA and DHA) are associated with heart health, and prescription omega-3 lowers triglycerides. The lipid panel that reports these numbers is in Section 9.6.

In the Lab

  • Experiment 9 – Lipids. Lipids dissolve in nonpolar solvents but not in water.
  • Bromine test for unsaturation: Br2 adds across each C=C in a fat or oil, so the more red color an oil decolorizes, the more unsaturated it is. Vegetable oils use up more bromine than solid animal fats.
  • Saponification: heating a fat with NaOH gives soap; you will test the soap's sudsing in soft and hard water and compare it with a synthetic detergent.
  • Hand lotion: you will make a lotion from stearic acid, cetyl alcohol, glycerol, and triethanolamine, and explain the role of each component.

Answers

“Think about it: Stearic acid and oleic acid both…”

Stearic acid has the higher melting point. It is saturated, so its straight chains pack tightly and have strong dispersion forces. Oleic acid has one cis double bond that kinks the chain, preventing close packing and weakening the attractions, so it melts at a much lower temperature (it is a liquid at room temperature).
