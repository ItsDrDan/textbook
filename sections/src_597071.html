Alkane molecules are nonpolar and therefore generally do not react with ionic compounds such as most laboratory acids, bases, oxidizing agents, or reducing agents. Consider butane as an example:

 

Neither positive ions nor negative ions are attracted to a nonpolar molecule. In fact, the alkanes undergo so few reactions that they are sometimes called paraffins, from the Latin parum affinis, meaning “little affinity.”

Two important reactions that the alkanes do undergo are combustion and halogenation. Nothing happens when alkanes are merely mixed with oxygen (\(\ce{O_2}\)) at room temperature, but when a flame or spark provides the activation energy, a highly exothermic combustion reaction proceeds vigorously. For methane (CH4), the reaction is as follows:

\[\ce{CH_4 + 2O_2 \rightarrow CO_2 + 2H_2O + \text{heat} } \nonumber \]

If the reactants are adequately mixed and there is sufficient oxygen, the only products are carbon dioxide (\(\ce{CO_2}\)), water (\(\ce{H_2O}\)), and heat—heat for cooking foods, heating homes, and drying clothes. Because conditions are rarely ideal, however, other products are frequently formed. When the oxygen supply is limited, carbon monoxide (\(\ce{CO}\)) is a by-product:

\[\ce{2CH_4 + 3O_2 \rightarrow​ 2CO + 4H_2O} \nonumber \]

This reaction is why unvented or poorly adjusted gas heaters, furnaces, stoves and generators can cause carbon monoxide poisoning, which still kills people in the United States every year, most often in winter. (Kerosene heaters can do the same.)

Clinical note. CO binds hemoglobin far more tightly than O2 does. A standard pulse oximeter cannot tell CO-bound hemoglobin from oxygen-bound hemoglobin, so it can show a normal oxygen saturation in a patient who is badly poisoned. Carbon monoxide poisoning is confirmed with CO-oximetry, a blood test that measures carboxyhemoglobin.

Alkanes also react with the halogens chlorine (\(\ce{Cl_2}\)) and bromine (\(\ce{Br_2}\)) in the presence of ultraviolet light or at high temperatures to yield chlorinated and brominated alkanes. For example, chlorine reacts with excess methane (\(\ce{CH_4}\)) to give methyl chloride (\(\ce{CH_3Cl}\)).

\[\ce{CH_4 + Cl_2 \rightarrow​ CH_3Cl + HCl} \nonumber  \]

With more chlorine, a mixture of products is obtained: \(\ce{CH3Cl}\), \(\ce{CH2Cl2}\), \(\ce{CHCl3}\), and \(\ce{CCl4}\). Fluorine (\(\ce{F_2}\)), the lightest halogen, combines explosively with most hydrocarbons. Iodine (\(\ce{I_2}\)) is relatively unreactive. Fluorinated and iodinated alkanes are produced by indirect methods.

Valley Connection: From Tailpipe to Smog

In this section, alkanes burn completely to give CO2 and H2O. Real engines are messier. Some fuel escapes unburned or only partly burned, adding hydrocarbons and other volatile organic compounds (VOCs) to the air, and the heat of burning fuel also produces nitrogen oxides (NOx).1,2 Cars, trucks, power plants, and off-road equipment are major sources of both.1,2

Making ozone. Ground-level ozone (O3) is not released from a tailpipe. It forms in the air when NOx and VOCs react in the presence of sunlight, which is why it builds up most on hot, sunny days.1 High in the stratosphere, ozone protects us from UV light; down where we breathe, it is a harmful pollutant.1

The Valley’s problem. Long, hot, sunny summers make the San Joaquin Valley an efficient ozone factory. The Valley Air District reports that more than 85% of the Valley’s NOx comes from mobile sources such as trucks and cars, and calls NOx the major ingredient for both ozone and fine-particle pollution here.3 In the American Lung Association’s 2026 State of the Air report (data from 2022–2024), Visalia ranked 2nd, Bakersfield 3rd, and Fresno 5th worst in the nation for ozone.4

In the clinic. Ozone can inflame and damage the lining of the airways, cause coughing and a sore throat, tighten airway muscles, and make asthma attacks more frequent.5 Children, older adults, people with asthma, and people who work outdoors, including farmworkers, are at highest risk.5 Bad ozone days can send patients to doctors’ offices and emergency rooms,5 where respiratory therapists give bronchodilator treatments, check oxygen levels, and teach patients to use their inhalers and check the daily air-quality forecast.

Think about it: Write a balanced equation for the complete combustion of octane (C8H18). Then explain why burning fuel in a real engine still contributes to ozone, even though ozone is not a product in your equation.

Answer
2 C8H18 + 25 O2 → 16 CO2 + 18 H2O. Real combustion is not perfectly complete, so some unburned or partly burned hydrocarbons (VOCs) escape, and the high heat also produces NOx. Ozone is a secondary pollutant: it forms later, in sunlit air, when VOCs and NOx react.

Key Takeaway

  • Alkanes react with oxygen (combustion) and with halogens (halogenation).
