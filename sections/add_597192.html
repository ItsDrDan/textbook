Lecture Connection

Two lecture rules sit on top of this section. First, glycolysis is an anaerobic pathway in the cytosol: no molecular oxygen is used and no CO2 is released, so its net yield is always 2 ATP and 2 NADH per glucose. Second, pyruvate has three pathways, and which one runs depends on whether oxygen is available. The step-by-step chemistry of the ten reactions is on the next page (Section 13.5); here we settle the bookkeeping.

Three fates of pyruvate

Glycolysis leaves a cell with two problems: pyruvate still holds most of the energy of glucose, and the NADH made in step 6 must be turned back into NAD+ or step 6 stops and glycolysis stalls. Oxygen decides how both problems are solved.

  • Aerobic conditions (oxygen present, cell has mitochondria): three-carbon pyruvate is oxidized and decarboxylated to two-carbon acetyl CoA + CO2 by the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex inside the mitochondrion. One carbon leaves as CO2, NAD+ is reduced to NADH, and the acetyl group is attached to coenzyme A as a thioester. The enzyme is an oxidoreductase that also needs a cofactor made from thiamine (vitamin B1). Acetyl CoA then enters the citric acid cycle, and the NADH from glycolysis is re-oxidized by the electron transport chain (Chapter 14).
  • Anaerobic conditions in humans (strenuous exercise, when not enough O2 is inhaled to re-oxidize NADH; red blood cells, which have no mitochondria): lactate dehydrogenase (an oxidoreductase) reduces pyruvate to lactate, using NADH + H+ and regenerating NAD+. The ketone C=O of pyruvate becomes the 2° alcohol C–OH of lactate. The net gain is still only 2 ATP per glucose, but it is fast and needs no oxygen. Lactate builds up in muscle during intense exercise along with H+, and the H+ contributes to the burning feeling and fatigue; deep breaths replenish O2 so lactate can be re-oxidized to pyruvate, and lactate also travels in the blood to the liver, which rebuilds it into glucose (the Cori cycle, Section 13.8).
  • Anaerobic fermentation in yeast and other microorganisms: pyruvate is decarboxylated to acetaldehyde, and acetaldehyde is reduced to ethanol, regenerating NAD+. The CO2 raises bread and carbonates beer. Humans do not have pyruvate decarboxylase, so in humans anaerobic conditions lead to lactate, never ethanol.

Practice (Cori cycle): Lactate is converted back to pyruvate in the liver. What reaction is taking place, what class of enzyme is needed, and is a coenzyme needed?

Answer
An oxidation: the 2° alcohol C–OH of lactate becomes the ketone C=O of pyruvate (more C–O bonds). The enzyme is lactate dehydrogenase, an oxidoreductase, now running in the opposite direction from muscle. Yes, a coenzyme: NAD+ is the oxidizing agent and is reduced to NADH + H+.

Keep the NAD+ bookkeeping straight: in a red blood cell, glycolysis makes 2 NADH per glucose and lactate dehydrogenase uses 2 NADH per glucose, so the cell’s net products are 2 ATP and 2 lactate with no NADH left over. In a muscle cell with enough oxygen, the same 2 NADH are instead cashed in for ATP in the mitochondria.

Example 1: Fate of pyruvate

Problem. During an all-out 400 m sprint, what happens to most of the pyruvate in leg muscle, and why?

Solution. O2 supply cannot keep up, so pyruvate is reduced to lactate. This step regenerates NAD+, which glycolysis needs to keep producing ATP.

Check: Lactate dehydrogenase is an oxidoreductase running in the reduction direction: pyruvate’s C=O becomes lactate’s C–OH while NADH becomes NAD+.

Example 2: A lactate result

Problem. A patient arriving with a severe infection has a serum lactate of 4.8 mmol/L (reference < 2). Explain in chemical terms where the lactate came from and why the blood pH is likely to be low.

Solution. Poor perfusion means the tissues are short of O2, so the pyruvate dehydrogenase route and the mitochondria are starved. To keep making ATP, cells keep glycolysis running and use lactate dehydrogenase to reduce pyruvate to lactate, regenerating NAD+. Lactic acid, a carboxylic acid, is fully ionized at blood pH, so each lactate released into blood comes with an H+; when production outruns the liver’s ability to clear it, bicarbonate is used up and pH falls: lactic acidosis.

Check: The same chemistry occurs in a sprinting athlete, but there the liver and heart clear the lactate within an hour; in sepsis, production is widespread and clearance is impaired, so the number stays high.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Lactate as a vital sign. In shock or sepsis (a body-wide response to infection), tissues do not get enough oxygen, pyruvate is shunted to lactate, and the H+ that comes with it causes lactic acidosis. A serum lactate above about 2 mmol/L is a red flag for poor tissue perfusion; watching it fall shows the resuscitation is working. Lactated Ringer’s, a common IV fluid, contains sodium lactate; a working liver converts that lactate to bicarbonate, so the fluid does not add acid. Red blood cells have no mitochondria and live on glycolysis (2 ATP per glucose) plus the pentose phosphate pathway (Section 13.6). Thiamine is the cofactor of pyruvate dehydrogenase: without it pyruvate cannot become acetyl CoA, lactate rises, and the heavily aerobic brain and heart suffer first. That is beriberi (heart failure, neuropathy) and Wernicke encephalopathy (confusion, unsteady gait) in alcohol use disorder, which is why thiamine is given with IV dextrose. In healthy exercise, lactate rises and clears within an hour; it is fuel, not a poison.
