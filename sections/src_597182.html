Recombinant DNA

DNA can be isolated from the cell and cut at a specific place using restriction enzymes. Fragments of DNA from different sources, e.g., an insulin gene from a human and a DNA from Escherichia coli (E. coli) cut using the same restriction enzyme, can be combined into a new recombinant DNA. The recombinant DNA can be introduced into the cell, e.g., back into E. coli, where it propagates using the cellular machinery of the host cell. It is then used to produce proteins of interest, e.g., human insulin used to treat diseases like diabetes.

How is recombinant DNA produced?

The DNA of host cells, e.g., small circular plasmids of DNA from E. coli, is isolated first. The host cells are soaked in a detergent solution that disrupts the plasma membrane and releases the plasmids. A restriction enzyme is used to cut the DNA of the host cell at a specific location. The same enzyme is used to cut a piece of donor DNA, e.g., an insulin gene from a human. The cut ends of the DNA are called sticky ends. When the cut donor and host DNA are mixed, they join at the sticky ends, as illustrated in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\). The resultant recombinant DNA is introduced to a new culture of E. coli. The host cells that have taken up the recombinant DNA are selected and grown. When these bacteria grow and divide, they produce the protein encoded in the inserted gene, i.e., insulin in this example. Insulin is extracted, purified, and used to treat diabetes.

Some applications of recombinant DNA technology

The recombinant DNA technology is used in food production, medicine, agriculture, and bioengineering. Some examples are listed below.

  • Chymosin is an enzyme used to manufacture cheese. Most cheese made in the US today uses chymosin produced by genetically engineered microbes.
  • Insulin is usually made by inserting the human insulin gene into E. coli or yeast; the cells produce insulin, which is processed and used to treat diabetes.
  • Human growth hormone (HGH) is needed for patients whose pituitary glands do not produce sufficient hormones. Recombinant HGH is commonly used for this purpose.
  • Blood clotting factor VIII is needed for patients suffering from the bleeding disorder hemophilia. It is produced these days by recombinant DNA technology.
  • The hepatitis B vaccine, used to prevent hepatitis B infection, is produced by recombinant DNA technology.
  • Recombinant HIV proteins are used in tests for antibodies that may have been produced in response to an HIV infection.
  • Herbicide-resistant crops, including soy, corn, canola, alfalfa, cotton, etc., have been developed that contain recombinant genes resistant to the herbicide glyphosate found in Roundup. It allowed the use of Roundup to control weeds without affecting the crop.
  • Insect-resistant crops: Bacillus thuringiensis is a bacterium that naturally produces a protein with insecticidal properties. Crops carrying this Bt gene, grown since the 1990s (for example, corn and cotton), make the protein themselves, so less sprayed insecticide is needed. The Bt protein is itself an insecticide: it is toxic to certain insects but not to people.
  • Interferon produced by recombinant DNA technology has been used to treat some cancers, multiple sclerosis and viral hepatitis; for many of these uses it has been replaced by newer drugs.
  • Influenza vaccines: most are made by growing the virus in eggs or in cultured cells, but one licensed flu vaccine is a recombinant protein, made in insect cells from the cloned gene for the virus’s surface protein.
  • Gene editing (CRISPR): in CRISPR–Cas9, a short guide RNA base-pairs with a chosen DNA sequence and steers an enzyme that cuts the DNA there. In 2023 the FDA approved the first CRISPR-based therapy, Casgevy, for sickle cell disease: a patient’s own blood stem cells are edited so they make fetal hemoglobin again, then returned to the patient.

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) quickly produces millions of copies of a DNA segment of interest. The selected DNA segment is heated to denature and separate the two strands and mixed with DNA polymerase, short synthetic DNA pieces called primers that match the ends of the segment to be amplified, and nucleotides, to produce a complementary strand. The process is repeated several times to make millions of copies of the desired DNA fragment, as illustrated in Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\).

Genetic testing

Genetic testing is done in a laboratory to study an individual's DNA and diagnose a defective gene that may cause a congenital disease, as well as ancestry studies or forensic studies. For example, breast cancer may be related to defects in breast cancer genes BRCA1 and BRCA2. Patients are screened for these defects by using blood or saliva samples to extract the DNA, and the PCR technique is applied to amplify and study the defective genes. Genetic testing is also used to study the DNA of tumors or cancer cases.

Fingerprinting

DNA fingerprinting (DNA profiling) compares regions of DNA that vary from person to person: places where a short sequence is repeated a different number of times in different people, called short tandem repeats (STRs). DNA from a small sample of blood, skin, saliva, or semen is copied by PCR using fluorescent-labeled primers for several STR regions, and the copies are separated by size by electrophoresis. The pattern of sizes identifies the individual, as illustrated in Figure \(\PageIndex{3}\). This technique is used for paternity tests, criminal investigations, and forensics.

Human genome

The Human Genome Project, which aimed to study all human DNA (human genome) comprehensively, was launched in October 1990 and completed in April 2003; the last gaps in the sequence were filled in 2022. It shows about 20,000 protein-coding genes in humans, representing only about 1.5% of the human genome. The role of the rest of the genome is still being explored. Much of it helps control when and where genes are turned on. These studies provide fundamental information to study human biology, defects in DNA that lead to genetic diseases, and find cures for conditions.

Valley Connection: Copying Fungal DNA to Find Valley Fever

The fungus Coccidioides lives in soil in the southwestern United States, and California’s southern San Joaquin Valley is one of the places where it is most common.2 Dry, windy weather, digging, and construction lift it into the air in dust, and people get Valley fever (coccidioidomycosis) by breathing it in. It does not spread from person to person. Outdoor workers in agriculture, construction, and wildland firefighting face extra risk.1 California reported nearly 12,500 cases in 2024, the most on record, and more than 5,500 provisional cases in just the first half of 2025.1 Symptoms such as cough, fever, chest pain, and tiredness that last more than 7 to 10 days can look a lot like flu or COVID-19.1

Making the diagnosis. Blood tests for IgM and IgG antibodies are the most common way to diagnose Valley fever. Growing the fungus in culture is possible, but it must be handled in a high-security (biosafety level 3) lab. The FDA has also approved a PCR test that detects Coccidioides DNA directly in samples from the lower airways.2

The chemistry. The polymerase chain reaction copies one short stretch of DNA over and over. First, heat separates the two DNA strands by breaking the hydrogen bonds between base pairs. Next, short synthetic DNA pieces called primers bind to sequences on either side of the target, choosing which segment gets copied.4 Then a heat-stable DNA polymerase (Taq) builds new complementary strands, using the original strands as templates.3 Each cycle doubles the target, and after 30 to 40 cycles there can be more than a billion copies in a few hours.3 If the primers match a sequence found only in Coccidioides, a strong signal means the fungus is present.

Careers. Clinical lab scientists run the antibody and PCR tests. Radiologic technologists take the chest X-rays and CT scans that often come first: early infection commonly shows patches of pneumonia (consolidation) and nodules, while the roughly 5% of patients who develop chronic disease may show lasting nodules or cavities in the lungs.5

Think about it: Why must the primers in a Valley fever PCR test match a DNA sequence found in Coccidioides but not in human DNA?

Answer
A patient sample (such as sputum) is full of the patient’s own human DNA. Primers bind by complementary base pairing, so they only start copying where they find a matching sequence. If the primers matched human DNA, every sample would be amplified and every test would look positive. Primers that match only a Coccidioides sequence make the test specific: DNA is copied only if the fungus is actually there.
