Lecture Connection

In lecture we call this reaction amidation and treat it as the amine version of Fischer esterification: heat a carboxylic acid with ammonia, a 1° amine, or a 2° amine, lose water, and the –OH of the acid is replaced by –NR2. The one rule students forget is that a 3° amine cannot do it.

Amidation: a condensation that needs heat

RCOOH + H2NR′ →(heat) RCONHR′ + H2O

  • The first thing an acid and an amine do at room temperature is form an ammonium carboxylate salt (Section 7.3); the reaction must be heated to drive off water and reach the amide. The section text describes the same slowness for ammonia.
  • 3° amines have no H on the nitrogen, so they do not form amides; with a carboxylic acid they give only the ammonium salt.
  • Chemists call amidation a nucleophilic acyl substitution: the amine nitrogen (the nucleophile, an electron-pair donor) takes the place of the –OH on the acyl carbon. Esterification and ester hydrolysis (Chapter 6) are the same reaction type; all acyl compounds (acids, esters, amides) share it.
  • Which amide forms tells you which amine was used: ammonia gives a 1° amide, a 1° amine gives a 2° amide, a 2° amine gives a 3° amide.

Example 1: Amidation

Problem. Draw and name the amide formed from acetic acid and methylamine.

Solution

CH3COOH + H2NCH3 → CH3CONHCH3 + H2O

Name: N-methylethanamide (N-methylacetamide).

Check. The –OH of the acid and one H from the amine left as water; the N now carries one carbon group besides the carbonyl, so the product is a 2° amide. The C=O side came from the acid and the N side from the amine, the same bookkeeping you will reverse in Section 7.7.

Professor Tip

Before you draw any amidation product, ask "does this amine have an N–H to lose?" If the answer is no (a 3° amine), stop: the only product is the ammonium salt, and no water is released. Example 5 in the Chapter Guide runs ethylamine, diethylamine, and triethylamine through the same acid so you can see all three outcomes side by side. In the body the peptide bonds of proteins are made this way too, but by enzymes at 37 °C, not by heat; the polymer versions (nylon, Kevlar) are in Section 7.10.
