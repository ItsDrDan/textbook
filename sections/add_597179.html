Lecture Connection

Translation switches languages: four bases to twenty amino acids, three bases at a time. You will be given the codon table on an exam; what you must know is how to read it (first base down the side, second across the top, third down the right), where to start (AUG), where to stop (UAA, UAG, UGA), and the four stages by name.

The genetic code

  • mRNA is read in non-overlapping groups of three bases, codons, in the 5′→3′ direction. With four bases there are 43 = 64 codons for 20 amino acids, so the code is degenerate: most amino acids have more than one codon, and the codons for one amino acid usually differ only in the third base.
  • AUG is the start codon; it sets the reading frame and codes for methionine, so every new polypeptide begins with Met (often removed later). UAA, UAG, and UGA are stop codons; no tRNA matches them, and a release factor protein frees the finished chain.
  • Each tRNA carries one amino acid and has a three-base anticodon that pairs, antiparallel, with the codon. The enzyme that loads the correct amino acid onto each tRNA (aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase) is where the "translation" really happens: once the amino acid is on its tRNA, base pairing does the rest.
  • The ribosome is an rRNA–protein complex with two subunits. The mRNA binds the small subunit; the active site that joins amino acids is in the large subunit and is made mostly of rRNA, so the ribosome acts as an enzyme made of RNA, a ribozyme. The chain grows from the N-terminus toward the C-terminus (Chapter 10).
  • The code is essentially universal: a human insulin gene is read the same way inside a bacterium, which is why recombinant DNA works (Section 12.8).

The four stages of translation

Learn the names, because they are how the process is described in class.

  1. Activation. An aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase uses the energy of ATP to attach the correct amino acid to its tRNA. The amino acid's carboxyl group bonds to a free –OH on the ribose at the tRNA's 3′ end (the acceptor stem), forming an aminoacyl ester. The tRNA is now ready to use its anticodon.
  2. Initiation. The mRNA binds the small ribosomal subunit, and the first tRNA, carrying methionine, pairs with the start codon AUG at the ribosome's P site (peptidyl site).
  3. Elongation. The next aminoacyl-tRNA pairs with the next codon at the A site (aminoacyl site). Peptidyl transferase (the rRNA active site) links the P-site amino acid to the A-site amino acid by a new peptide bond. The empty tRNA leaves, the ribosome shifts three bases (one codon) along the mRNA, a step called translocation, the growing chain is now at the P site, and the A site is open for the next tRNA. Repeat.
  4. Termination. When a stop codon (UAA, UAG, or UGA) reaches the A site, no tRNA fits; the finished polypeptide is released from the last tRNA by hydrolysis of the ester. The initial methionine is usually trimmed off, and the chain folds into its secondary, tertiary and (if any) quaternary structure to become an active protein.

Many ribosomes can read one mRNA at the same time, one behind another, forming a polysome; reusing a message this way saves the cell the energy of transcribing it again.

In this course

The section text illustrates only the elongation steps and calls the first stage "binding of an amino acid to its tRNA." On exams use the four lecture names, activation, initiation, elongation, termination, and be able to say what happens at the P site and the A site. The text's "wobble" base is the same idea as our statement that codons for one amino acid usually differ only in the third base.

Professor Tip

Codon pairs with anticodon by hydrogen bonding (A–U, G–C), the same weak attraction that holds the DNA double helix together, so the tRNA can let go after its amino acid is delivered. The amino acid is attached to the tRNA by a covalent aminoacyl ester bond, made by the synthetase at the cost of ATP. Exam questions like to ask which bond is which. And remember that the ribosome reads codons, not amino acids: the amino acid was chosen when the synthetase loaded the tRNA.

Worked example

Example 1: From mRNA to a peptide drawn at pH 7

Problem: An mRNA segment reads 5′-GGU GAA AAG-3′. (a) Use the genetic code to write the amino acid sequence. (b) Draw the tripeptide as it exists at physiological pH and give its net charge.

Solution

(a) Read the codons left to right in the table: GGU = Gly, GAA = Glu, AAG = Lys. The peptide is Gly-Glu-Lys (glycylglutamyllysine, GEK).

(b) Backbone first, N-terminus on the left, then the side chains (Chapter 10): +H3N–CH2–C(=O)–NH–CH(CH2CH2COO−)–C(=O)–NH–CH[(CH2)4NH3+]–COO−. Charges at pH 7.4: N-terminus +1, C-terminus −1, glutamate side chain −1, lysine side chain +1. Net charge 0.

Check: Three codons give three residues and two peptide bonds. If the middle codon had been GAG instead of GAA the answer would be the same (both are Glu), a reminder that the code is degenerate.
