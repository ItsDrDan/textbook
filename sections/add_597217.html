Lecture Connection

The lecture’s version of this section is short: the body makes the nonessential amino acids from glycolysis and citric acid cycle intermediates by adding nitrogen through transamination; it cannot make the essential ones because it lacks the right carbon skeleton, so they must come from food. Three nonessential amino acids are made in a single transamination step; tyrosine is made from an essential one; the rest take several reactions. That, plus the list of essential amino acids, is what you are responsible for.

In this course

The section above lists ten nonessential amino acids, which matches the lecture’s count of ten nonessential and ten essential (arginine counted as essential; see below). Its pathway details (glutamine synthetase, S-adenosylmethionine, cystathionine, tetrahydrofolate) are background. One correction: the conversion of phenylalanine to tyrosine is an oxidation, not a reduction; phenylalanine hydroxylase uses O2 and NADPH to add an OH to the aromatic ring, and this is the enzyme missing in phenylketonuria.

Essential versus nonessential

The body can make the carbon skeletons of the nonessential amino acids from glycolysis and citric acid cycle intermediates and then add nitrogen by transamination (Section 15.2); these need fewer reaction steps than the essential ones. Nine are essential for all adults; the mnemonic PVT TIM HaLL lists them: Phenylalanine, Valine, Threonine, Tryptophan, Isoleucine, Methionine, Histidine, Leucine, Lysine. The lower-case “a” is arginine: the urea cycle makes it (Section 15.10), but not fast enough in infants or during severe illness, so many GOB textbooks and the lecture slides count it as a tenth essential amino acid, leaving ten nonessential. Tyrosine and cysteine are conditionally essential for the same reason: tyrosine is made from phenylalanine and cysteine’s sulfur comes from methionine. Three nonessential amino acids, alanine, aspartate, and glutamate, are made in a single step by transamination of the matching α-keto acid (pyruvate, oxaloacetate, α-ketoglutarate), the ALT and AST reactions of Section 15.2 running in reverse; the others take several reactions in addition to transamination.

Practice: Oxaloacetate accepts an amino group from glutamate. Name the amino acid formed, the reaction type, the enzyme class, and the coenzyme, and state whether the product is essential or nonessential.

Answer
Aspartate. Transamination (amino-group transfer), by an aminotransferase (AST), a transferase, with pyridoxal phosphate (vitamin B6). Nonessential: its carbon skeleton is a citric acid cycle intermediate the body already has.

Health Career Connection

PKU and sugar-free products. People with phenylketonuria (PKU) lack phenylalanine hydroxylase, so they cannot convert phenylalanine to tyrosine. Phenylalanine and its by-products build up and damage the developing brain, which is why every newborn is screened and why the diet restricts phenylalanine while supplying tyrosine, which has become essential for them. The sweetener aspartame is a methyl ester of a dipeptide of aspartate and phenylalanine; digestion releases the phenylalanine, which is why sugar-free gums, diet sodas, and some mouth products carry a “Phenylketonurics: contains phenylalanine” warning. For a dental hygienist recommending sugar-free gum to a patient with PKU, xylitol-sweetened products are the alternative (Section 13.6). For a nurse, the lesson is the same one this section teaches: an amino acid is “nonessential” only as long as the enzyme that makes it works.
