Lecture Connection

Three kinds of inhibitor, and one test question tells them apart: does adding more substrate restore the maximum rate? Yes for competitive, no for noncompetitive, and never for irreversible, where a covalent bond has been made. Many drugs and poisons are enzyme inhibitors, so know a clinical example of each.

Competitive, noncompetitive, irreversible

  • Competitive: the inhibitor resembles the substrate and binds the active site, so enzyme molecules that are holding inhibitor cannot hold substrate. It is reversible, and it can be overcome by adding more substrate, which out-competes the inhibitor; the maximum rate is eventually reached, it just takes more substrate to get there.
  • Noncompetitive: the inhibitor binds somewhere other than the active site and changes the enzyme's shape so the active site no longer works well. More substrate does not help, because the substrate and inhibitor are not competing for the same spot; the maximum rate is lowered. Both competitive and noncompetitive inhibitors are reversible: the inhibitor binds, and activity returns when it is released.
  • Irreversible: the inhibitor forms a covalent bond with an R group of an amino acid in or near the active site, changing the enzyme's shape so the substrate cannot enter, and permanently destroying its activity. The cell must make new enzyme to recover. Lead from old paint and mercury from contaminated fish (both bind tightly to cysteine –SH groups), nerve gases, and organophosphate insecticides all work this way. In California, lead poisoning has also been traced to some imported candies, spices, glazed pottery, and traditional remedies such as greta and azarcón.

Professor Tip

Feedback inhibition, in which a pathway’s end product binds away from the active site, is covered as a form of allosteric regulation in Section 11.6; this section is about the three inhibitor types. Irreversible inhibitors to know: heavy metals (which also count as noncompetitive), organophosphates, penicillin and aspirin. A heavy-metal ion such as Hg2+ or Pb2+ is a poison that bonds to –SH groups, never a cofactor; Mg2+, Zn2+, Fe2+ and Cu2+ are the cofactors (Section 11.5).

Many enzymes are made as inactive zymogens (pepsinogen, trypsinogen, chymotrypsinogen) and activated only where they are needed (some hormones, such as insulin from proinsulin, are activated the same way); that is regulation rather than inhibition, and Section 11.7 covers it.

Drugs that are inhibitors

Because enzymes are so specific, a drug that fits one active site can shut down one pathway without touching the rest of the body. These are the examples to recognize.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Inhibitors are prescribed in the dental office too. Sub-antimicrobial-dose doxycycline is given in periodontitis not to kill bacteria but to inhibit the host's matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) by binding their Zn2+ cofactor, slowing the loss of collagen attachment. And a patient on warfarin, which blocks the recycling of vitamin K (Section 11.5) and so slows clotting, needs monitoring before any procedure that will bleed.
