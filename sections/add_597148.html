Lecture Connection

In lecture the membrane follows from one rule, "like dissolves like": phospholipids in water put their polar heads out and their nonpolar tails in, and the nonpolar core then decides how every substance gets across. You must be able to classify a crossing as diffusion, facilitated transport, or active transport.

The bilayer and the fluid-mosaic model

When phospholipids are mixed with water they assemble into a lipid bilayer, with polar heads facing the water on both sides and nonpolar tails inside, away from the water. A cell membrane is a phospholipid bilayer about 7 nm thick, described by the fluid-mosaic model: "fluid" because the phospholipids and cholesterol drift sideways like molecules in a thin oil film, and "mosaic" because proteins are scattered through the bilayer, some spanning it completely (the integral proteins of the section text; peripheral proteins sit on the surface). Glycerophospholipids and sphingomyelin supply the bilayer, cholesterol tunes its fluidity (Section 9.6), and glycolipids and glycoproteins on the outer face act as cell-identity tags (the blood-type sugars of Section 8.3). Practice: the interior of a lipid bilayer is made of nonpolar tails, not polar heads.

Professor Tip

On exams, sort membrane transport into the three types below: simple diffusion, facilitated transport, and active transport. The integral and peripheral proteins, channels and carriers described above are the proteins that make the last two possible.

Three ways across

The nonpolar core is the key to transport: anything small and nonpolar slips through; anything charged or polar and larger than water needs a protein. The table and figure sort the three cases.

Two details matter clinically. The GLUT family of glucose carriers includes GLUT4 in muscle and fat cells, which only moves to the cell surface when insulin signals; that is the molecular reason insulin lowers blood glucose. The Na+/K+-ATPase is in every cell and uses a large share of a resting cell's ATP to keep Na+ low and K+ high inside; nerve impulses and the heartbeat depend on those gradients, which is why serum K+ is watched so closely. Practice: which type of transport requires the cell to spend energy? Only active transport; a protein alone does not make it active.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Plaque biofilm bacteria are wrapped in a phospholipid bilayer; essential-oil rinses (thymol, eucalyptol, menthol, methyl salicylate) dissolve into that bilayer and disrupt it, and chlorhexidine, the positively charged bis-biguanide that is the professional "gold standard" for plaque control, binds the negatively charged bacterial surface and makes the membrane leak (brown staining of teeth is a known side effect of long-term use). Your local anesthetics (lidocaine, articaine, mepivacaine) are amines that exist as a lipid-soluble uncharged base and a water-soluble protonated cation. Only the uncharged base crosses the nerve membrane by diffusion, the first row of the table. An abscess is acidic, which pushes the drug toward the charged form, so anesthesia near inflamed tissue is less reliable. Drug from inhaled corticosteroids deposits in the mouth, suppresses local immunity, and allows oral candidiasis (thrush); teach patients to rinse and spit after each dose.
