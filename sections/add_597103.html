Geometry, forces and ranking

The carbonyl carbon has three electron groups (two single bonds and the double bond), so its geometry is trigonal planar with 120° angles. The C=O bond is polar, so aldehydes and ketones have dipole–dipole forces. Can two aldehyde (or two ketone) molecules hydrogen bond with each other? No: there is no H bonded to the oxygen. Their boiling and melting points are therefore higher than hydrocarbons of comparable size and shape (they are polar) but lower than alcohols (no hydrogen bonding). Can they hydrogen bond with water? Yes: the carbonyl oxygen is a proton acceptor, so the length of the alkyl chain decides solubility: 4 or fewer carbons, soluble; 5 carbons, sparingly soluble; 6 or more, insoluble. Two reminders. First, as the carbon chain grows, the nonpolar part wins: hexanal and 2-hexanone are only slightly soluble in water. Second, an isomeric aldehyde and ketone (propanal and propanone, for example) have very similar boiling points, because both rely on the same C=O dipole. For boiling-point rankings on an exam, treat aldehydes and ketones as one category that sits between hydrocarbons and alcohols. The section's own table adds the ether (ethyl methyl ether, 6 °C) just above the alkane, giving the full order alkane < ether < aldehyde or ketone < alcohol.

Example: Ranking by boiling point and solubility

Problem. Pentane, butanal, 1-butanol and 2-butanone all have molar masses of 72–74 g/mol. (a) Rank them from lowest to highest boiling point. (b) Which are appreciably soluble in water?

Solution. (a) Identify the strongest force each can use with its own kind. Pentane: dispersion only. Butanal and 2-butanone: dipole–dipole from the C=O, no O–H. 1-Butanol: hydrogen bonding. Ranking: pentane < butanal ≈ 2-butanone < 1-butanol (actual values 36, 75, 80 and 118 °C). (b) All three oxygen compounds can accept hydrogen bonds from water, and each has only four carbons, so butanal, 2-butanone and 1-butanol are all soluble (2-butanone and 1-butanol: roughly 7–27 g per 100 mL). Pentane is insoluble.

Check. The aldehyde and ketone land between the hydrocarbon and the alcohol, and the small difference between the two carbonyl isomers is not something you need to predict.

Common carbonyl compounds in the clinic and the lab

In the Lab

In the aldehydes and ketones experiment you will meet three of the section's compounds by smell: formaldehyde (specimen preservative), acetone (nail-polish remover) and benzaldehyde (almond). The chemical tests you then run on an unknown (Tollens', iodoform) are described in Section 5.4.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Ketones you can smell. The section's note on acetone in blood, urine and breath is the chemistry behind a bedside observation. When cells cannot use glucose, as in untreated type 1 diabetes, the liver turns fatty acids into ketone bodies, two of which are acids. The result is diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), a dangerous drop in blood pH. The third ketone body is the ketone acetone, which is volatile (small, dipole–dipole forces only) and leaves through the lungs, giving the fruity "acetone breath" you may notice on a patient. Urine ketone strips detect the ketone bodies; details come in Chapter 15. Formalin. A water solution of formaldehyde (formalin) preserves biopsy and tissue samples by cross-linking proteins, which is the same reactivity that makes free formaldehyde an irritant.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Aldehydes as sterilants. Glutaraldehyde, a five-carbon dialdehyde (OHC–CH2CH2CH2–CHO), is a cold-sterilant for heat-sensitive instruments. Its two aldehyde groups react with amino groups on microbial proteins, cross-linking them so the proteins stop working; formaldehyde fixes tissue specimens the same way. That reactivity is also why these solutions irritate eyes, skin and airways and have a sharp odor, and why instruments must be rinsed thoroughly after soaking. Flavor. Two of the section's aldehydes flavor toothpaste and mouth rinse: vanillin (vanilla) and benzaldehyde (almond, cherry). Acetone. The same ketone that appears in DKA breath is a lab and clinic solvent, used for removing nail polish and in some denture and appliance cleaning; it dissolves both polar and nonpolar residues because the C=O is polar while the two methyl groups are not. Curing lights. The photoinitiator in most light-cured composites is camphorquinone, a compound with two ketone groups; it absorbs blue light and starts the polymerization you saw in Chapter 3.
