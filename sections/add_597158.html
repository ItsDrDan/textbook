In this course

The section text names the four tertiary interactions as ionic bonding, hydrogen bonding, disulfide linkages and dispersion forces, and then explains that hydrophobic interactions are a related but different idea. In lecture, and on exams, the four R-group interactions are hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, salt bridges (the text's "ionic bonds") and disulfide bonds. "Hydrophobic interaction" is the name to use for nonpolar side chains packing together away from water.

Fibrous or globular?

Proteins are also sorted by overall shape, and the shape follows from the structure. Fibrous proteins are long, tough and water-insoluble; they are mostly one kind of secondary structure repeated, and they build things: collagen (triple helix; skin, tendon, bone, dentin, gingiva), keratin (α-helix; hair, nails, outer skin), and fibroin (β-sheet; silk). Globular proteins fold into compact, roughly spherical shapes with the hydrophobic residues inside and polar ones outside, so they dissolve in water and do chemistry: enzymes, hemoglobin, myoglobin, albumin, antibodies, and most hormones such as insulin.

Worked example

Example 1: Inside or outside?

Problem. Would the segment Ile-Phe-Leu more likely be found on the surface or in the interior of a protein in water?

Solution. All three have nonpolar R groups, so the segment is likely buried in the interior, away from water.

Check. A segment such as Asp-Lys-Ser, with charged and polar side chains, would be the opposite: on the surface, hydrogen-bonded to water.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Most of what a hygienist touches is protein. Type I collagen is the main protein of gingival connective tissue, the periodontal ligament (the fibers anchoring the tooth to bone) and the organic matrix of dentin and cementum. Enamel is about 96% hydroxyapatite mineral with almost no protein left after it matures, which is why it cannot repair itself; dentin is about 70% mineral and 20% collagen by weight, so it is tougher but softer, and in periodontitis (inflammatory loss of the tooth's supporting tissues) bacterial and host enzymes hydrolyze the collagen of the ligament and gum. Saliva is a protein solution: mucins (glycoproteins) lubricate and coat the mucosa; salivary amylase starts starch digestion; lysozyme, an enzyme, hydrolyzes the polysaccharide of bacterial cell walls; lactoferrin binds iron that bacteria need; and statherin keeps saliva's calcium and phosphate from precipitating, so the fluid stays supersaturated and ready to remineralize enamel. Within minutes of a prophylaxis, these proteins adsorb to the enamel as the acquired pellicle, the film that plaque biofilm attaches to; it protects against acid, but tea and coffee stains bind to it, and whitening pastes are rated by how well they remove it. Chlorhexidine, a positively charged antiseptic, binds to the negatively charged proteins of the pellicle and mucosa and is released over hours, a property called substantivity. Finally, hair and nails get their toughness from keratin's many disulfide bonds; enamel has none, because its strength is mineral, not protein.
