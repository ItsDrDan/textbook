Lecture Connection

The section above sorts compounds into covalent and ionic and shows that their properties differ. In lecture the point is sharpened: the contrast your textbook draws between "organic" and "inorganic" compounds is really a contrast between molecular and ionic compounds.

It is really molecular versus ionic

Textbooks like to contrast organic with inorganic, but the properties really track molecular versus ionic. The forces between molecules are much weaker than the forces between ions, so molecular compounds have lower melting and boiling points than ionic compounds, are often liquids or gases at room temperature, tend to be flammable, and are usually poor conductors because they do not form ions in water. At room temperature a solid is either an ionic compound or a very large molecule.

In this course

Your source text (like Timberlake and most GOB books) tabulates the properties of organic versus inorganic compounds. On exams, state the comparison as molecular versus ionic. Every organic compound is molecular, but inorganic compounds can be either: water and carbon dioxide are molecular; sodium chloride and hydroxyapatite are ionic. "Low melting point, does not conduct in water" tells you a compound is molecular, not that it is organic. The source's covalent-naming prefixes (mono-, di-, tri-) are CHEM 3A review; organic compounds use the IUPAC system that begins in Chapter 2.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Tooth enamel is not made of molecules at all. It is hydroxyapatite, Ca5(PO4)3OH, an ionic solid in which calcium, phosphate, and hydroxide ions sit in a rigid lattice held together by ionic bonds. Acid from plaque bacteria pulls those ions out of the lattice, a process called demineralization. Fluoride ion from toothpaste or varnish replaces the hydroxide to form fluorapatite, Ca5(PO4)3F, which resists acid better and favors remineralization. That is ionic chemistry from CHEM 3A. Almost everything else you handle in the clinic is covalent and molecular, so its behavior is decided by the intermolecular forces of Section 1.8, where this story continues.
