Learning Objectives

  • State the normal fasting blood glucose range and explain why the body defends it so tightly.
  • For insulin, glucagon, epinephrine, cortisol, and growth hormone, name the source, the trigger, and the pathways each turns on or off.
  • Describe the fed–fasting cycle: which fuel supplies blood glucose at 0–4 hours, 4–16 hours, beyond 16 hours, and in prolonged starvation.
  • Explain how hormones reach the regulatory enzymes (allosteric control of PFK-1, phosphorylation of glycogen phosphorylase and glycogen synthase).
  • Predict how trauma, surgery, infection, or fever changes glucose metabolism, and distinguish type 1 from type 2 diabetes and hyperglycemia from hypoglycemia.

A patient’s blood glucose is the most frequently measured chemical in any hospital, and most of the time the number is normal because a few hormones keep a dozen pathways in balance. This section is about that balance: what the set point is, which hormones move it, how the body runs between meals, and what happens when illness, injury, or diabetes breaks the system. Every nurse checks fingersticks and every dental hygienist screens for diabetes; both need to read the number as chemistry, not just as a value to chart.

The set point and why it matters

After an overnight fast, a healthy adult’s plasma glucose sits between 70 and 99 mg/dL (3.9–5.5 mmol/L). A fasting value of 100–125 mg/dL is called impaired fasting glucose (prediabetes); 126 mg/dL or higher on two occasions defines diabetes. Below 70 mg/dL is hypoglycemia. The number is defended on both sides because glucose is both essential and toxic.

  • Too low: the brain burns about 120 g of glucose a day and cannot use fatty acids at all; red blood cells have no mitochondria and depend on glycolysis alone. When glucose falls, the brain fails first: sweating, tremor, and a racing heart (the body’s epinephrine alarm), then confusion, slurred speech, seizure, and coma.
  • Too high: glucose above the kidney’s reabsorption limit (roughly 180 mg/dL) spills into urine and drags water with it (polyuria, then thirst and dehydration). Over months, the aldehyde form of glucose reacts with free amino groups on proteins, a non-enzymatic reaction called glycation. Glycated hemoglobin, HbA1c, is the clinical readout (normal below 5.7%, diabetes 6.5% or above) and reports the average glucose over the past 2–3 months (a red cell lives about 4 months). The same glycation stiffens blood vessel proteins, which is why uncontrolled diabetes damages eyes, kidneys, nerves, and arteries.

Two chemistry reminders. Insulin and glucagon are peptides, so neither can be taken by mouth: digestive proteases would hydrolyze them (Chapter 10), which is why insulin is injected. Epinephrine is a catecholamine (an amine, Chapter 7) that acts within seconds and is gone within minutes; cortisol is a lipid-soluble steroid (Chapter 9) that changes which enzymes the cell makes and acts over hours to days.

The fed–fasting cycle

The body switches fuel sources in a predictable order after a meal. Learning the timeline lets you predict which pathway is running in a patient from one fact: when they last ate.

  • 0–4 hours (fed, absorptive): glucose from digestion floods the portal vein; insulin is high and glucagon low. The liver takes up glucose and stores it as glycogen (glycogenesis, Section 13.7), muscle and fat take up glucose through GLUT4, and any surplus becomes fatty acids and triglycerides (lipogenesis). Glycolysis runs; gluconeogenesis is off.
  • 4–16 hours (early fasting, for example overnight): as absorbed glucose runs out, insulin falls and glucagon rises. The liver switches to glycogenolysis and exports glucose; an adult liver holds roughly 100 g of glycogen, which is largely used up within about 12 to 24 hours. Muscle glycogen cannot raise blood glucose (muscle lacks glucose-6-phosphatase, Section 13.7) and is kept for the muscle’s own use. Fat cells start releasing fatty acids, which most tissues other than the brain and red cells begin to burn instead of glucose.
  • 16 hours to about 2 days (fasting): liver glycogen is nearly gone, and gluconeogenesis (Section 13.8) becomes the main source of blood glucose. Its raw materials are lactate from red cells and muscle (the Cori cycle), glycerol from fat breakdown, and amino acids, mostly alanine, released when muscle protein is broken down. Cortisol drives that protein breakdown. Fatty acid oxidation in the liver now produces more acetyl CoA than the citric acid cycle can take, and the excess is packaged as ketone bodies (Chapter 15), which begin to appear in blood and urine.
  • Days to weeks (prolonged starvation): the brain adapts to burn ketone bodies for most of its energy, so glucose demand falls and gluconeogenesis slows. That spares muscle protein, which is why a fasting person loses fat before muscle. Red cells still need glucose, so gluconeogenesis never stops entirely. Death from starvation comes when fat is exhausted and protein breakdown resumes.

How hormones reach the enzymes

Hormones do not touch glucose. They change the activity of a handful of regulatory enzymes, using the two control mechanisms from Sections 11.6 and 11.7: allosteric control by small molecules already inside the cell, and covalent modification, usually the addition or removal of a phosphate group, triggered by the hormone. The table collects the control points you have met so far.

Notice the pattern in the glycogen rows. Glucagon and epinephrine bind receptors on the cell surface and raise the level of a second messenger (cyclic AMP), which switches on a protein kinase that phosphorylates both glycogen enzymes at once. The same phosphate turns glycogen phosphorylase on and glycogen synthase off, so one signal guarantees that the cell is breaking glycogen down and not building it at the same time. Insulin activates the phosphatases that remove those phosphates and flips both switches back. Allosteric control works on top of this: even a phosphorylated (“on”) phosphorylase in the liver slows down when free glucose is high, because the job is done.

Metabolism during stress

Major trauma, surgery, burns, severe infection (sepsis), and even a bad fever set off a coordinated stress response, and its signature in the lab is stress hyperglycemia: a blood glucose of 140–200 mg/dL or more in a patient who has never had diabetes. The chemistry is a storm of counter-regulatory signals:

  • Catecholamines (epinephrine, norepinephrine) surge within minutes: liver glycogenolysis dumps glucose, lipolysis releases fatty acids, and insulin release from the β cells is suppressed.
  • Cortisol rises and stays up for days, driving gluconeogenesis from amino acids. Muscle protein is broken down to supply them, which is why a patient in an ICU bed loses muscle quickly and goes into negative nitrogen balance (Chapter 15).
  • Cytokines, the signaling proteins of inflammation, make muscle and fat cells ignore insulin (insulin resistance), so the glucose that is poured into the blood stays there. Glucagon rises too.
  • Fever raises the basal metabolic rate by roughly 10% per degree Celsius, increasing oxygen and fuel demand at the moment a sick patient is eating least.

The plan makes sense for an injury that lasts hours: glucose for the brain and white blood cells, fatty acids for everything else, amino acids for repair. Sustained for weeks in an ICU, the same hyperglycemia impairs white-cell function and wound healing, which is why hospitals treat glucose in critically ill patients who have never had diabetes, and why a stress glucose should be rechecked after recovery before anyone is labeled diabetic.

Diabetes mellitus and hypoglycemia

Diabetes mellitus is chronic hyperglycemia from too little insulin action. The two main types fail in different places.

Long-term riskBoth: glycation damage to vessels and nerves (retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy, heart disease), delayed wound healing, periodontal disease

Hypoglycemia is the opposite emergency and, in a treated diabetic, the more common one: too much insulin for the food eaten, a skipped meal, unusual exercise (muscle takes up glucose without insulin during contraction), or alcohol, which blocks liver gluconeogenesis. The early signs are the epinephrine response (sweating, shakiness, pounding heart, hunger); the later signs are the brain running out of fuel (confusion, slurred speech, combativeness, seizure). A patient can look drunk and be hypoglycemic. The remedy is glucose itself (by mouth or IV, following protocol) or glucagon, which forces the liver to release glucose from glycogen.

Example 1: After a meal versus after an overnight fast

Problem. For each pathway, state whether it is running faster in the liver one hour after a large pasta dinner or after a 14-hour overnight fast, and name the hormone responsible: (a) glycogenesis, (b) glycogenolysis, (c) glycolysis, (d) gluconeogenesis, (e) lipogenesis.

Solution. One hour after the meal, blood glucose and insulin are high: (a) glycogenesis fed, insulin; (c) glycolysis fed, insulin; (e) lipogenesis fed, insulin. After 14 hours, insulin is low and glucagon high: (b) glycogenolysis fasted, glucagon; (d) gluconeogenesis fasted, glucagon (with cortisol supplying amino acids). At 14 hours the liver is near the end of its glycogen, so both (b) and (d) are active, with (d) taking over.

Check: Every “fed” pathway stores or burns glucose; every “fasted” pathway makes or releases it. If an answer put a storage pathway in the fasted column, the hormone logic would be backwards.

Example 2: Explain a lab result

Problem. A 58-year-old with no history of diabetes has a glucose of 188 mg/dL on the morning after emergency surgery for a broken hip, and an HbA1c of 5.4%. Interpret both numbers.

Solution. The HbA1c is normal (below 5.7%), which means the average glucose over the past 2–3 months was normal: this patient does not have undiagnosed diabetes. The high fasting glucose today is therefore new, and the context explains it: trauma plus surgery triggered the stress response. Epinephrine released glucose from liver glycogen, cortisol stimulated gluconeogenesis, and inflammatory cytokines made muscle and fat resistant to insulin, so the glucose stays in the blood. This is stress hyperglycemia. It should be managed in the hospital and rechecked after recovery, not labeled diabetes.

Check: If the HbA1c had been 7.5%, the same 188 mg/dL would point to pre-existing, uncontrolled diabetes instead. The two tests answer different questions: today versus the last three months.

Example 3: Classify hormone effects

Problem. Label each effect as anabolic or catabolic and name the hormone most responsible: (a) muscle protein broken down to supply alanine for gluconeogenesis; (b) triglyceride synthesis in fat cells after a meal; (c) glycogen breakdown in a sprinting runner’s thigh; (d) glycogen synthesis in the liver after breakfast.

Solution. (a) Catabolic; cortisol. (b) Anabolic; insulin. (c) Catabolic; epinephrine (muscle glycogen responds to epinephrine, not glucagon). (d) Anabolic; insulin.

Check: Insulin is the body’s only major anabolic (storage) hormone for fuel; every counter-regulatory hormone is catabolic (mobilizing). The one subtlety is (c): the runner’s muscle breaks down glycogen for itself, not for the blood.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Insulin regimens mirror the hormone’s two jobs. A basal (long-acting) dose replaces the background insulin that keeps the liver from running gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis all night; bolus doses of rapid-acting insulin cover meals. A patient who is NPO (nothing by mouth) still has insulin on board, which is why a dextrose IV such as D5W (5% dextrose in water) often runs alongside it: the insulin would otherwise drive glucose too low. Corticosteroid drugs are cortisol look-alikes, so expect glucose to climb on them, even without diabetes. Many people with type 2 diabetes take metformin, a GLP-1 receptor agonist, or an SGLT2 inhibitor (Section 13.4). SGLT2 inhibitors work by making the kidneys excrete glucose, so glucose in the urine is expected in these patients and does not by itself mean poor control. Know the two emergencies by their chemistry. DKA: high glucose, ketones in blood and urine, acidosis with deep, rapid Kussmaul respirations (blowing off CO2), fruity acetone breath, and dehydration from osmotic diuresis. Hypoglycemia: diaphoresis, tremor, tachycardia, then confusion; a fingerstick glucose comes before assuming intoxication. A glucagon emergency kit works only by forcing liver glycogenolysis, so it fails in a patient whose glycogen is already depleted; that patient needs glucose itself. The HbA1c tells you how the last three months went; the fingerstick tells you what is happening now.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Diabetes is the chronic condition you will see most often in the chair. Hypoglycemia is the acute risk: a patient who took insulin or a sulfonylurea and then skipped breakfast for an early appointment can become diaphoretic, shaky, and confused during a long procedure. Scheduling after a meal, confirming that the patient has eaten and taken their medication, and keeping a glucose source within reach follow directly from the chemistry. The epinephrine in anesthetic cartridges (typically 1:100,000) can nudge glucose up by triggering glycogenolysis; the effect is small, but it is one more reason to know the patient’s control. Long-term, poorly controlled diabetes and periodontitis drive each other: glycation damages the small vessels of the gingiva and blunts neutrophil function, so periodontal infection is worse and wound healing after scaling or extraction is slower; in the other direction, cytokines from chronic periodontitis increase insulin resistance and raise HbA1c, and periodontal treatment measurably improves glucose control. Expect xerostomia (dry mouth), with its higher caries risk, and oral candidiasis, yeast thriving on sugar-rich saliva. Ask for the most recent HbA1c; a high value is a reason to coordinate with the physician before elective surgery.

Valley Connection: Checking Sugar with an Enzyme and an Electrode

Diabetes close to home. When the body’s glucose regulation breaks down, the result is diabetes, and it is common in the San Joaquin Valley. CDC model-based estimates for 2023 put diagnosed diabetes at about 12.4% of adults in Fresno County, 13.1% in Tulare, 13.3% in Kern and Merced, 12.9% in Kings and 12.8% in Madera, compared with 7.5% in Marin and 9.3% in San Francisco.1 Fresno County’s own health report lists diabetes among the county’s most common chronic conditions, and diabetes was the sixth leading cause of death in the county in 2023, with 288 deaths.2,3

The numbers to know. A fasting blood glucose of 99 mg/dL or lower is normal, 100–125 mg/dL is prediabetes, and 126 mg/dL or higher is in the diabetes range. An A1C of 6.5% or higher also indicates diabetes.4

How the meter works. A glucose test strip is a tiny enzyme reactor. In many strips the enzyme glucose oxidase oxidizes glucose at C1, the aldehyde carbon (the anomeric carbon of the ring), turning it into gluconolactone, which becomes gluconic acid. The enzyme’s coenzyme FAD accepts the electrons and becomes FADH2, just like the FAD you met in metabolism. A mediator molecule (such as ferricyanide) then shuttles those electrons to an electrode, and the meter measures the current. More glucose means more electrons, so a larger current means a higher reading.5 Other strips use glucose dehydrogenase, paired with NAD+, FAD or PQQ, which does the same oxidation but does not use O2.5

In the clinic. In the hospital, fingerstick (capillary) glucose is the standard bedside test. Nurses check it before meals and at bedtime for patients who are eating, give insulin, and follow protocols for highs and lows; a reading under 70 mg/dL counts as hypoglycemia.6 A result that doesn’t fit the patient should be confirmed with a lab test on venous blood.6

Think about it: Glucose oxidase meters can read falsely low in a patient breathing a lot of supplemental oxygen.6 Using the electron path described above, suggest why.

Answer
Glucose oxidase can pass the electrons from FADH2 to O2 (making H2O2) instead of to the mediator. When the blood holds extra oxygen, more electrons go to O2 and fewer reach the electrode, so the current, and the glucose reading, comes out lower than the true value. Glucose dehydrogenase strips do not use O2, so oxygen levels don’t affect them this way.

In the Lab

Urine analysis, glucose: a urine test strip uses the enzyme glucose oxidase to detect glucose. Normal urine has almost none, because the kidneys return it to the blood; glucose appears in urine (glucosuria) when blood glucose rises above about 180 mg/dL, as in uncontrolled diabetes. In the metabolism experiment you will test simulated “urine” samples and connect each result to the hormone picture of this section.

Summary

  • Normal fasting plasma glucose is 70–99 mg/dL. The brain and red cells need a steady supply; chronic excess causes osmotic water loss and glycation (HbA1c, vascular damage).
  • Insulin (β cells, fed state) is the one hormone that lowers glucose: GLUT4 uptake, glycogenesis, glycolysis, lipogenesis on; gluconeogenesis off. Glucagon (α cells, fasting), epinephrine (acute stress), cortisol (prolonged stress), and growth hormone raise it.
  • Fed–fasting cycle: absorbed glucose (0–4 h) → liver glycogen (4–16 h) → gluconeogenesis from lactate, glycerol, and amino acids plus rising ketone bodies (beyond 16 h) → ketone-adapted brain and protein sparing (days to weeks).
  • Hormones act through regulatory enzymes: PFK-1 and pyruvate kinase (allosteric; ATP, citrate, AMP, fructose-1,6-bisphosphate), and phosphorylation that turns glycogen phosphorylase on and glycogen synthase off (glucagon, epinephrine) or the reverse (insulin).
  • Trauma, surgery, sepsis, burns, and fever cause stress hyperglycemia through catecholamines, cortisol, glucagon, and cytokine-induced insulin resistance; fever raises metabolic rate about 10% per °C.
  • Type 1 diabetes is autoimmune insulin deficiency (DKA risk); type 2 is insulin resistance (HHS risk). Hypoglycemia presents with the epinephrine response first (sweating, tremor, tachycardia) and neuroglycopenia second (confusion, seizure).

Exercises

Exercise 1

A patient’s fasting glucose is 64 mg/dL. Name two hormones that will be released within minutes, the tissue each acts on first, and the pathway each turns on.

Answer
Glucagon (from pancreatic α cells) acts on the liver to turn on glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis. Epinephrine (adrenal medulla) acts on the liver (glycogenolysis, glucose to blood) and muscle (glycogenolysis for the muscle’s own use) and fat (lipolysis). Insulin release is suppressed.

Exercise 2

Compare and contrast glucagon and epinephrine: source, time course, and the one tissue that responds to epinephrine but not glucagon.

Answer
Glucagon is a peptide from pancreatic α cells released as glucose falls over hours; epinephrine is a catecholamine from the adrenal medulla released within seconds of stress or hypoglycemia and cleared within minutes. Skeletal muscle has epinephrine receptors but no glucagon receptors, so muscle glycogenolysis is an epinephrine effect.

Exercise 3

Explain how a single phosphate group added by one protein kinase can both increase glycogen breakdown and stop glycogen synthesis. Why is that arrangement useful?

Answer
Phosphorylation (covalent modification) activates glycogen phosphorylase and inactivates glycogen synthase. Because the same signal flips both enzymes in opposite directions, the cell never runs synthesis and breakdown at the same time, which would waste ATP in a futile cycle. Insulin reverses both by activating a phosphatase.

Exercise 4

A hiker lost for three days has eaten nothing. Which fuel is supplying most of her brain’s energy, where is her blood glucose coming from, and why has she lost mostly fat rather than muscle?

Answer
By day three the brain still runs mainly on glucose, now made by gluconeogenesis, while ketone bodies made in the liver from fatty acids supply a growing share (they become its main fuel only after more days of fasting). The glucose demand (red cells, brain) is met by gluconeogenesis from glycerol, lactate, and amino acids; liver glycogen ran out on day one. Because the brain needs less glucose, less muscle protein has to be broken down for amino acids, so fat is used preferentially and protein is spared.

Exercise 5

A patient with a severe burn has a glucose of 210 mg/dL and a normal HbA1c. Name three signals responsible for the high glucose and state what each one does.

Answer
Epinephrine/norepinephrine: liver glycogenolysis and suppression of insulin release. Cortisol: gluconeogenesis from amino acids freed by muscle protein breakdown. Inflammatory cytokines: insulin resistance in muscle and fat, so glucose is not taken up. (Glucagon rises too.) The normal HbA1c shows this is stress hyperglycemia, not pre-existing diabetes.

Exercise 6

Two patients arrive confused. One is sweaty, trembling, and has a glucose of 42 mg/dL; the other is dry, breathing deeply and rapidly, has a fruity breath odor, and has a glucose of 480 mg/dL. Identify each emergency and explain the breathing and the breath odor in the second patient.

Answer
The first is hypoglycemia (the sweating and tremor are the epinephrine response; confusion is the brain short of glucose). The second is diabetic ketoacidosis: with no insulin, fat is broken down and the liver makes acidic ketone bodies faster than they can be used, so blood pH falls. The deep, rapid Kussmaul breathing blows off CO2 to compensate for the acidosis, and the fruity odor is acetone, the volatile ketone body, leaving in the breath.

Exercise 7

Why does a glucagon emergency kit work less well in someone who has been drinking heavily and not eating for a day?

Answer
Glucagon raises blood glucose mainly by forcing liver glycogenolysis. After a day without food, liver glycogen is nearly gone, so there is little to release. Alcohol also blocks gluconeogenesis, the backup route, so the liver cannot make new glucose either. Glucose must be supplied from outside (for example, IV dextrose).

Exercise 8

A dental patient with type 2 diabetes has an HbA1c of 9.2%. Using the chemistry of this section, give two reasons the hygienist expects more severe periodontitis and one reason to schedule the appointment mid-morning rather than at 7 a.m.

Answer
Chronic hyperglycemia causes glycation of vessel and tissue proteins, impairing blood flow and neutrophil function in the gingiva, so infection is worse and healing slower; the sugar-rich saliva and xerostomia also favor plaque and candida. Scheduling after breakfast and the morning medication avoids hypoglycemia in the chair (the patient has eaten, and the stress and length of the appointment are less likely to push glucose below 70 mg/dL).
