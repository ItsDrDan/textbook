Learning Objectives

  • Recognize an oxidation (more C–O bonds or fewer C–H bonds) and a reduction (the reverse) in an organic reaction.
  • Predict the products of hydrogenating an alkene and of reducing an aldehyde or ketone.
  • Predict the products of oxidizing 1° and 2° alcohols and aldehydes, and explain why 3° alcohols and ketones are not oxidized.
  • Describe the Tollens’ and Benedict’s tests, the role of NAD+/NADH in the body, and the thiol/disulfide pair in proteins.

What are oxidation and reduction?

Oxidation is i) the loss of electrons, ii) the gain of \(\ce{O}\), or iii) the loss of \(\ce{H}\). Reduction is the opposite, i.e., i) the gain of electrons, ii) the loss of \(\ce{O}\), or iii) the gain of \(\ce{H}\). The reduction and oxidation reactions are coupled, called redox reactions. Electrons' loss or gain is easily recognized in inorganic chemical reactions. For example, consider the following reaction:

\(\ce{ 4Fe + 3O2 -> 2Fe2O3}\),

\(\ce{Fe}\) lost three electrons to become \(\ce{Fe^{3+}}\) and \(\ce{O}\) gained two electrons to become \(\ce{O^{2-}}\) in this reaction.

Electron loss or gain is hard to see in organic molecules, which are held together by shared electrons. Instead, count bonds to carbon: an oxidation increases the number of C–O bonds and/or decreases the number of C–H bonds; a reduction does the opposite. Over a reaction arrow, [O] stands for any oxidizing agent and [H] for any reducing agent.

Catalytic hydrogenation of alkenes

Catalytic hydrogenation of vegetable oils

Vegetable oils are fats of tri-ester of glycerol with long chain fatty acids containing one or more \(\ce{C=C}\) bonds. The \(\ce{C=C}\) bonds create kinks in the long chains, preventing the chains' packing and resulting in lower melting points. Partial hydrogenation of \(\ce{C=C}\) bonds in vegetable oil is carried out to convert it to semisolid margarine, as illustrated in Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\). Hydrogenation makes them straight-chain alkanes that pack nicely and increases their melting points.

Reduction of aldehydes and ketones

Catalytic hydrogenation of aldehydes and ketones

Hydrogen (\(\ce{H2}\)) reduces aldehydes and ketones to alcohols in the presence of a catalyst like finely divided \(\ce{Ni}\), \(\ce{Pd/C}\), or \(\ce{Pt/C}\). as shown in the following examples.

If \(\ce{C=C}\)-bond and a \(\ce{C=O}\)-bond are present in the same molecule, catalytic hydrogenation reduces both, as shown below.

In the laboratory an aldehyde or ketone can also be reduced by a hydride reagent such as sodium borohydride (NaBH4). Whatever the reagent, the result is the same: H adds to the carbon and to the oxygen of the C=O, and on exams you write [H] over the arrow.

Reduction of aldehydes and ketones in biological systems

In the body the hydrogen comes from a coenzyme. Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (\(\ce{NADH}\)) coenzyme in its reduced form \(\ce{NADH}\) is used as a hydride donor in biological systems. \(\ce{NADH}\) is oxidized to \(\ce{NAD^{+}}\) after donating \(\ce{H^{-}}\), as illustrated below.

Enzyme-catalyzed conversion of acetaldehyde to ethanol during fermentation is an example of reduction using \(\ce{NADH}\).

Conversion of pyruvate to lactate during glycolysis under anaerobic (absence of oxygen) conditions is another example of reduction using \(\ce{NADH}\), as shown below.

Accumulation of lactate after strenuous exercise accompanies muscle fatigue, but lactate itself is no longer thought to be the main cause of that fatigue.

Reduction of disulfide \(\ce{S-S}\) bond in biological systems

The \(\ce{S-S}\) bond commonly occurs in the protein tertiary structure. It is responsible for straight or curly hair shapes. The \(\ce{S-S}\) bond can be reduced to \(\ce{-SH}\) group by reducing agents. A thiol-disulfide exchange reaction often achieves this conversion in biological systems, as in the following example.

\[\ce{R-S-S-R + 2HOCH2CH2SH <=> 2R-SH + HOCH2CH2S-SCH2CH2OH}\nonumber \]

Oxidation of alcohols and aldehydes

Primary and secondary alcohols are oxidized by several oxidizing agents, including Jones reagent (a mixture of chromium trioxide (\(\ce{CrO3}\)) and sulfuric acid (\(\ce{H2SO4}\))), chromic acid (\(\ce{H2CrO4}\)), or potassium dichromate (\(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\)) in sulfuric acid (\(\ce{H2SO4}\)). In each case the oxidizing agent removes two hydrogens, the H of the O–H and the H on the carbon that carries the O–H, and a C=O bond forms in their place.

Primary alcohols are oxidized to aldehydes. Aldehydes convert to hydrates by adding water, and the hydrates are oxidized to carboxylic acids, as illustrated below.

Secondary alcohols are oxidized to a ketone, as shown in the following example. Ketones are not oxidized further because the carbonyl carbon has no hydrogen left to remove.

Tertiary alcohols are not oxidized for the same reason, i.e., there is no hydrogen on the carbon to which the –OH is attached.

Tests for aldehydes and reducing sugar based on easy oxidation of aldehydes

The fact that an aldehyde is oxidized further easily while ketone is not was used as a test to distinguish between aldehydes and ketones. In this test Tollens' reagent (\(\ce{[Ag(NH3)2]OH}\)) is mixed with the test sample. Ketones do not react, but the aldehyde is oxidized by the following reaction:

\[\ce{2[Ag(NH3)2]OH + R-CHO -> 2Ag(s) + 4NH3 + R-COOH + H2O}\nonumber \]

\(\ce{Ag}\) produced in the above reaction deposits and forms silver mirror on the wall of the test tube as shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{3}\)

Benedict's reagent is a mixture of sodium carbonate (\(\ce{Na2CO3}\)), sodium citrate (\(\ce{Na3C6H5O7}\)), and copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate (\(\ce{CuSO4.5H2O}\)). Benedict's reagent produces a red precipitate (\(\ce{Cu2O}\)) as a positive test of aldehyde by the following reaction.

\[\ce{2Cu(C6H5O7)^{-} + 5OH^{-} + R-CHO -> Cu2O(s, red) + 2C6H5O7^{3-} + R-COO^{-} + 3H2O}\nonumber \]

This test distinguishes aldehydes from ketones and is also positive for reducing sugars (carbohydrates) that contain an aldehyde group, as shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{4}\).

Alcohol exhaled in breath is oxidized by dichromate \(\ce{Cr2O7^{2-}}\) ions along with producing green color chromium(III) ions (\(\ce{Cr^{3+}}\)) by the following reaction.

\[\ce{CH3CH2OH + Cr2O7^{2-}(reddish orange) ->[H2SO4 + H2O] CH3COOH + Cr^{3+}(green)}\nonumber \]

This reaction is used to test for alcohol in drunk-driving cases by law enforcement.

Oxidation of alcohols in living systems

Alcohols are oxidized by nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (\(\ce{NAD^+}\)) to aldehydes and ketones in the cells. For example, ethanol, methanol, and lactate are oxidized to ethanal, methanal, and pyruvate, respectively, as shown below.

Unpleasant effects of drinking alcohol, i.e., flushing, nausea, dizziness, sweating, headache, and low blood pressure, are caused by ethanal (acetaldehyde). Antabuse drugs inhibit aldehyde dehydrogenase from converting acetaldehyde to acetic acid, causing the unpleasant effects of acetaldehyde to persist. Methanal produced by the oxidation of methanol is a poisonous substance that damages many tissues and causes blindness. Methanol is added to ethanol meant to be used as a solvent (denatured alcohol) to make it undrinkable. In cases of methanol poisoning, ethanol is injected intravenously into the patient to compete with methanol for alcohol dehydrogenase enzyme. This way, methanol is excreted before converting to poisonous methanal in the body.

Oxidation of thiol (\(\ce{-SH}\)) to disulfide (\(\ce{-S-S{-}}\)) linkage

Thiol (\(\ce{-SH}\)) is present in the amino acid cysteine. Two cysteine units in the same or different protein chains can oxidize their thiol groups to form disulfide (\(\ce{-S-S{-}}\)) linkage. The disulfide linkage plays an important part in defining the tertiary structure of proteins. This oxidation reaction can be carried out easily by a variety of oxidizing agents, including iodine (\(\ce{I2}\)), bromine (\(\ce{Br2}\)), and oxygen (\(\ce{O2}\)), as shown in an example below.

\[\ce{2R-SH + 1/2O2 -> RS-SR + H2O}\nonumber \]
