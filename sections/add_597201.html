Lecture Connection

The lecture’s one-line summary of Stage 3: the citric acid cycle exists to make reduced coenzymes (NADH and FADH2), and the electron transport chain exists to turn them into ATP. Know the initial reactant and final product of the cycle (oxaloacetate → oxaloacetate), what one turn yields, the ATP each reduced coenzyme is worth, and how to tell oxidation from reduction when the carriers change form. The eight steps get their own table in Section 14.3 and the chain its own stations in Section 14.4.

The ATP table at the end of the section above uses the older convention (3 ATP per NADH, 2 per FADH2) and totals 12 ATP per acetyl CoA; the text hedges with “2.5–3” and “1.5–2.” On exams use the lecture values: NADH + H+ → NAD+ + 2.5 ATP, FADH2 → FAD + 1.5 ATP, GTP = 1 ATP, so one acetyl CoA yields 10 ATP (3 × 2.5 + 1.5 + 1) and one glucose yields 32 ATP (30 with the glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle; older books say 36–38). The half-reactions, iron–sulfur centers, and FMN described above are background; you are responsible for the four complexes, the two mobile carriers, and the order the electrons visit them.

The cycle in one paragraph

The citric acid cycle (Krebs cycle) operates under aerobic conditions, is based in the mitochondrion (its matrix), and is a cyclic pathway: the initial reactant and the final product are both oxaloacetate. Acetyl CoA (2 C) joins oxaloacetate (4 C) to make citrate (6 C); over 8 steps the acetyl portion is oxidized to CO2, NADH and FADH2 are produced, and oxaloacetate is regenerated. Per turn (per acetyl CoA): 2 CO2, 3 NADH, 1 FADH2, 1 GTP (= 1 ATP). One glucose makes 2 acetyl CoA, so the cycle turns twice. Very little ATP comes out of the cycle directly; its main job is to load the coenzymes that the chain will cash in.

Electron transport: the order of appearance

  • The electron transport chain is a multistep process using four enzyme complexes (I, II, III, IV) along the inner mitochondrial membrane. It transfers hydrogen ions and electrons from NADH and FADH2 until they react with oxygen to form water. The final electron acceptor is O2.
  • Two mobile electron carriers move electrons between the complexes: coenzyme Q (CoQ, a fat-soluble quinone that moves laterally within the membrane; the Q is for quinone) carries electrons from I and II to III, and cytochrome c (a heme-containing protein whose iron atom is reversibly oxidized and reduced, bound so that the heme cannot pick up O2 the way hemoglobin does) carries them from III to IV.
  • Order of appearance: NADH or FADH2 → (I or II) → CoQ → III → cytochrome c → IV → O2.
  • The energy released pumps H+ across the membrane; H+ flows back through ATP synthase, which makes ATP (oxidative phosphorylation, Section 14.4). FADH2 enters at Complex II and skips Complex I, so it pumps fewer protons and is worth 1.5 ATP instead of 2.5.

Know what the acronyms stand for and what the pieces are: ATP, adenosine triphosphate (adenine + ribose + three phosphates); NAD+, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (niacin); FAD, flavin adenine dinucleotide (riboflavin); CoA, coenzyme A (pantothenic acid, with a reactive –SH); CoQ, coenzyme Q (a quinone); GTP, guanosine triphosphate, which the cell uses like ATP.

Redox bookkeeping

Oxidation and reduction always happen together: one substance loses electrons while another gains them. When NAD+ oxidizes a substrate it accepts a hydride ion (H−: one proton plus two electrons) and becomes NADH; the second hydrogen from the substrate is released as H+. That is why the half-reaction is written NAD+ + 2 H → NADH + H+. FAD accepts both hydrogens (2 H+ + 2 e−) and becomes FADH2. In electron transport the carriers run backward: NADH is oxidized to NAD+, giving its electrons to the chain, and at the end O2 is reduced to water. The NAD+ and FAD regenerated this way go back to the cycle; without O2 they are not regenerated and the cycle stops, even though the cycle itself never touches O2.

Professor Tip

“LEO the lion says GER.” Loss of Electrons is Oxidation, Gain of Electrons is Reduction. NADH → NAD+ is oxidation (it gives its electrons to the chain). O2 → H2O is reduction. For organic molecules a quick shortcut also works: gaining O or losing H is oxidation; gaining H or losing O is reduction.

Example 1: Oxidized or reduced?

Problem. In each change, is the first species oxidized or reduced? (a) FADH2 → FAD; (b) NAD+ → NADH.

Solution. (a) Loses H and electrons: oxidized. (b) Gains H and electrons: reduced.

Check: LEO says GER: the carrier that loses hydrogen (FADH2) is oxidized; the one that gains it (NAD+) is reduced. Change (a) is what happens at Complex II; change (b) is what happens three times per turn of the cycle.

Practice: The reduced form of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide is represented as: FADH, FADH2, NAD+, or NADH + H+?

Answer
NADH + H+. FADH2 is the reduced flavin dinucleotide; “FADH” is not a species at all, because FAD accepts two hydrogens.
