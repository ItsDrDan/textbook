Start from Chapter 3. In hydration (Section 3.4), water adds across a C=C double bond: the H of H–OH goes to one carbon, the OH goes to the other, and the double bond becomes a single bond. An alcohol, R–O–H, does the same thing to the C=O of an aldehyde or ketone. The alcohol’s H goes to the carbonyl oxygen, its OR goes to the carbonyl carbon, and C=O becomes C–O. The only change is that the pieces are H and OR instead of H and OH.

The carbonyl group of an aldehyde or a ketone is a polar double bond, \(\ce{R-\!\!{\overset{\overset{\Huge\enspace\!{\overset{\Large{\delta{-}}}{\ce{O}}}}|\!\!|\enspace}{\overset{\delta{+}}{\ce{C}}}}\!\!-R'}\) with a partial positive carbon and a partial negative oxygen. Unlike an ester or an amide, an aldehyde or ketone has no group on the carbonyl carbon that can be swapped off, so instead other molecules add across the C=O, usually with an acid catalyst.

The partial positive carbon of a C=O attracts the oxygen of an alcohol, so an alcohol can add across the C=O double bond: the alcohol’s O bonds to the carbonyl carbon, its H goes to the carbonyl oxygen, and the C=O becomes a C–O single bond. Chemists call this kind of reaction a nucleophilic addition. The reactions you need in this course are the addition of one or two alcohol molecules, and the reverse reaction, hydrolysis.

Hemiacetals and acetals

When an alcohol (\(\ce{R-OH}\)) adds to an aldehyde or a ketone and only a limited amount of alcohol is present, the reaction stops after one \(\ce{R-OH}\) molecule has added. The product is a hemiacetal, as in the following example.

A hemiacetal has an –OR group and an –OH group on the same carbon. Most hemiacetals are unstable and are present only in small amounts at equilibrium. The exceptions are five- and six-membered rings that form when an –OH group and a C=O group in the same molecule react. In water, for example, more than 99% of glucose molecules are in the six-membered ring (cyclic hemiacetal) form, in equilibrium with the open-chain aldehyde form, as shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\).

When the alcohol is in excess, a second \(\ce{R-OH}\) reacts with the hemiacetal to form an acetal and water. An acetal has two \(\ce{-O-R}\) groups bonded to the same \(\ce{C}\), as shown in the example below.

The acetal groups in carbohydrates are called glycosidic linkages (Chapter 8). Glycosidic linkages connect monosaccharide units together in polysaccharides like starch and cellulose and in disaccharides, e.g., lactose, maltose, and sucrose, as shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\).
