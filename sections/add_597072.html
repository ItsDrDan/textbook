Lecture Connection

Haloalkanes are the first heteroatom-containing family this course studies in detail, and lecture treats them as a dress rehearsal for everything that follows: the halogen is named as a prefix exactly like an alkyl branch, it makes the molecule polar, and moving it along the chain makes a positional isomer.

Naming haloalkanes

Halogens are treated exactly like alkyl groups: they are substituents, never part of the parent name. Number the chain to give the first substituent (halogen or alkyl) the lowest number, break ties alphabetically (bromo before methyl), and list the halogen among the alkyl groups in alphabetical order: 2-bromo-4-methylpentane, 1-chloro-3-methylcyclohexane. With two or more of the same halogen use di-, tri-: 1,2-dibromoethane. Fluoro, chloro, bromo, and iodo are the only halogen prefixes you need.

Professor Tip

Recognize the common names (methyl chloride, ethyl chloride, isopropyl chloride, chloroform), because they are the ones on anesthetic sprays and solvent bottles, but write IUPAC names on exams: chloromethane, chloroethane, 2-chloropropane, trichloromethane. When a question says "name the compound," the IUPAC name is the expected answer.

Example 1: Naming two haloalkanes

Problem

Name (a) CH3CHClCH2CH3 and (b) (CH3)2CHCH2Br. Are they isomers of each other?

Solution

(a) Four-carbon chain, Cl on the second carbon from the nearer end: 2-chlorobutane. (b) The longest chain is three carbons (the two methyls on the first CH count as one chain carbon plus a branch): a propane chain with Br on C1 and a methyl on C2, so 1-bromo-2-methylpropane. Written from the bromine end, C1 carries the Br and C2 carries the methyl, which gives the lowest numbers.

Check

Formulas: (a) C4H9Cl, (b) C4H9Br. Different halogens, so they are not isomers of each other; (a) is a positional isomer of 1-chlorobutane, and (b) is a skeletal isomer of 1-bromobutane (Section 2.3). ✓

How the halogen changes the properties

  • Haloalkanes have a polar C–X bond, so they add dipole–dipole attraction to dispersion, and a heavy halogen adds many electrons. A haloalkane therefore boils higher than the alkane of the same skeleton. Neither alkanes nor haloalkanes can hydrogen bond, and both are insoluble in water.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

The inhaled anesthetics used in surgery today, sevoflurane (C4H3F7O) and isoflurane (C3H2ClF5O), are small, heavily halogenated ethers: saturated carbon skeletons in which most hydrogens have been swapped for fluorine. Their chemistry explains how they are given. They have no O–H or N–H, so they cannot hydrogen bond, and their IMFs are weak enough that they boil near 59 °C and 49 °C. A liquid that volatile evaporates readily in the anesthesia machine's vaporizer, mixes with oxygen, and reaches the lungs as a gas; being only weakly polar and fat-soluble, it crosses lipid membranes into the brain and leaves the same way when the gas is turned off. The C–F bonds are strong and unreactive, which is why these drugs are not flammable and are largely exhaled unchanged. Their history is a lesson in structure: chloroform (CHCl3), introduced in 1847, caused liver damage and dangerous heart rhythms, and halothane, a bromo-chloro-fluoro haloalkane adopted in 1956, was later linked to a rare hepatitis. The fluorinated ethers replaced both.

Answers

Molecule in the Clinic: Halothane (a)

2-bromo-2-chloro-1,1,1-trifluoroethane. Two carbons make it an ethane; the halogens are named as substituents in alphabetical order (bromo, chloro, fluoro), numbered so the set of locants is lowest.

Molecule in the Clinic: Halothane (b)

It is small and has low polarity, so it dissolves easily in the nonpolar lipid interior of cell membranes, including the membranes of nerve cells.
