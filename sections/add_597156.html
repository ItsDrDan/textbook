Lecture Connection

An amino acid does the chemistry of its two functional groups, and you already know both: the amine neutralizes acids and the carboxylic acid neutralizes bases (Section 10.2), and the two of them condense to form an amide, which between amino acids is called a peptide bond (Section 10.4). The reactions we use in lab to detect amino acids and proteins come from the same two groups.

Three reactions, two functional groups

  • Acid–base. Adding H+ protonates the carboxylate; adding base deprotonates the ammonium group. Nothing new forms, only a different charge state. This is the chemistry of pI, worked out in Section 10.2.
  • Condensation to a peptide. The –COO− of one amino acid and the –NH3+ of the next lose water and form an amide. This is the only reaction that builds a protein, and hydrolysis is its reverse (Section 10.4).
  • Oxidation of cysteine. Two –SH groups are oxidized to a disulfide, –S–S–, the same thiol chemistry you met in Chapter 4. In a protein this is the disulfide bond of tertiary structure (Section 10.8).

Precipitating a protein at its pI

A protein is least soluble at its isoelectric point: with no net charge, the molecules stop repelling one another and clump. Milk's main protein, casein, has a pI near 4.6, so adding acid to milk drop by drop until the pH reaches 4.6 curdles it; the curd is almost pure casein. The same chemistry is behind cheese making, yogurt (bacteria make the lactic acid), and the way stomach acid curdles milk protein. Heat is not needed; the change in charge is enough.

In the Lab

  • Experiment 11 – Amino Acids: classify amino acids by R group, then separate them by paper chromatography, make the spots visible with ninhydrin (it reacts with the free α-amino group to give a purple product, the reaction the section text describes), and identify an unknown from its Rf value (distance moved by the spot ÷ distance moved by the solvent). Polar, charged side chains stick to the wet paper and travel less; nonpolar side chains travel farther with the solvent.
  • Experiment 12 – Peptides and Proteins (part 1): isolate casein from milk by adding acid until it reaches its isoelectric point, then tell amino acids from proteins with two color tests. Ninhydrin needs a free amino group, so it is strongest for free amino acids. The biuret test needs two or more peptide bonds, so blue Cu2+ turns violet only with peptides and proteins, not with free amino acids. The denaturation part of this experiment belongs with Section 10.10.
