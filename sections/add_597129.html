Lecture Connection

In lecture, alkaloids are the payoff for learning amine basicity: every one of them is an amine, so every one can be pulled out of a plant with dilute acid as a water-soluble ammonium salt and freed again with base. Know the four plant sources and the one structural feature they share.

Alkaloids: amines made by plants

Alkaloids are physiologically active nitrogen-containing compounds produced by plants. Nearly all are amines, often with a heterocyclic ring (Section 7.8); they are used as stimulants, anesthetics, and antidepressants, and many are habit forming. Well-known alkaloids: cocaine (coca plant), caffeine (coffee beans and tea leaves; a purine), nicotine (tobacco; a pyridine and an N-methylpyrrolidine ring), and morphine and codeine (opium poppy; each has a 3° amine in a ring, and morphine also has a phenol). Morphine is very addictive, and patients using it long-term need ever-increasing doses for the same effect; codeine, used for milder pain, is addictive too. Eat enough poppy seeds and you can test positive for opiates on a drug screen.

The extraction chemistry in the section text is exactly Section 7.3 in reverse order: acid converts the free amine (soluble in the plant's oils, not in water) to the ammonium salt that dissolves in water; base takes the H+ back off and frees the amine. The same pair of reactions explains the two forms of cocaine the text describes: the hydrochloride salt dissolves in mucous membranes, while the free base is volatile enough to be smoked.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Opioids such as morphine, codeine, and the semisynthetic hydrocodone and oxycodone are alkaloids: each has a 3° amine in a ring, and morphine also has a phenol OH. On the unit they arrive as salts, morphine sulfate or hydromorphone hydrochloride, because the ammonium salt dissolves for injection and oral absorption while the free base does not. Naloxone, the reversal agent, shares the same amine-containing skeleton with a different group on the nitrogen, which is why it fits the same receptor.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Nicotine, the double heterocyclic amine drawn in Section 7.8, is the alkaloid in tobacco-cessation lozenges, gum, and patches. In the lozenge it is present as a salt (nicotine polacrilex, bound to an acidic resin); the lozenge also contains a carbonate buffer that makes the saliva around it slightly alkaline, so the free amine is released and absorbed through the oral mucosa, the same salt-to-free-base shift you saw for local anesthetics in Section 7.3.
