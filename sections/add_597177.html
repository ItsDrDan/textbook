Lecture adds the cast of enzymes for replication and asks you to know where each process happens. Transcription gets its own treatment in Section 12.4 and translation in Section 12.5.

The central dogma

Information flows in one direction in nearly all cells. The one famous exception is the retroviruses, which run the second step backward (Section 12.7).

Replication: one helix becomes two

Before a cell divides it must copy all of its DNA, and the copy must be nearly perfect. The double helix makes this possible: each strand is a template for its partner.

  • Semiconservative. The helix unwinds, each old strand directs the synthesis of a new complementary strand, and each daughter helix ends up with one old strand and one new strand.
  • Helicase breaks the hydrogen bonds and unzips the helix at a replication fork.
  • DNA polymerase reads a template strand and adds the complementary deoxynucleotide to the 3′-OH of the growing strand. It can only add to a 3′-OH, so every new strand grows 5′→3′, and it cannot start from nothing: a short RNA primer made by primase supplies the first 3′-OH. The energy comes from the incoming nucleoside triphosphate, which loses two phosphates (pyrophosphate) as the new phosphodiester bond forms.
  • Because the two template strands are antiparallel, one new strand (the leading strand) grows continuously toward the fork while the other (the lagging strand) is made in short pieces called Okazaki fragments, each growing away from the fork; the primers are replaced by DNA and DNA ligase joins the fragments into one continuous strand.
  • Replication usually runs bidirectionally from many starting points along a chromosome at once, which is how billions of base pairs are copied in a few hours. DNA polymerase also proofreads as it goes, so with the cell's repair enzymes the error rate is about one in a billion base pairs.

Professor Tip

For exams know four enzymes: helicase (unzips), primase (makes the RNA primer), DNA polymerase (adds nucleotides 5′→3′), and DNA ligase (seals the fragments), plus the words semiconservative, replication fork, leading and lagging strand and Okazaki fragments. “Begins at the 3′ end of the template strand” means the template is read 3′→5′ while the new strand is built 5′→3′.

Three kinds of RNA

  • mRNA (messenger): carries the gene's code from DNA to the ribosome. Its bases are read in triplets called codons.
  • tRNA (transfer): carries a specific amino acid, attached as an ester at its 3′ end, and has the anticodon that pairs with a codon.
  • rRNA (ribosomal): together with proteins, forms the ribosome, where protein synthesis takes place; rRNA makes up about two-thirds of the ribosome and catalyzes peptide bond formation. It is the most abundant RNA in a cell.

Section 12.9 describes each of these in detail and shows how they cooperate in translation.

In the Lab

In the DNA extraction lab you break open cells (strawberry, cheek, or onion) with a detergent solution, which dissolves the lipid membranes (Chapter 9). Salt is added so that Na+ ions surround the negatively charged phosphate backbone; this lets the strands clump instead of repelling one another. Cold ethanol is layered on top: DNA is much less soluble in ethanol than in water, so it comes out of solution as a white, stringy mass at the boundary. Meat tenderizer (a protease) or pineapple juice can be added to digest the proteins wrapped around the DNA. The strings you lift out on a glass rod are millions of double helices tangled together.
