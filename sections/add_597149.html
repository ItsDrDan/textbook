Lecture Connection

In lecture steroids are the non-hydrolyzable family you must recognize on sight: four fused rings, no fatty acids, so nothing for water to split. Cholesterol is the parent of every other steroid in the body and of vitamin D, and the fat-soluble vitamins share the steroids' "stored, not excreted" behavior.

The steroid nucleus and cholesterol

Every steroid is built on the same frame: three six-membered rings (A, B, C) and one five-membered ring (D) fused edge to edge, 17 carbons in all. Steroids differ only in what hangs off the frame: an OH or a C=O on ring A, a double bond here or there, methyl groups at the ring junctions, and a side chain on C17. There are no esters or amides in the frame, which is why steroids are non-hydrolyzable.

Cholesterol is the most abundant steroid in the body and the only one the body makes in large amounts (about 1 g per day, much of it in the liver); the rest comes from the diet (meat, cheese, butter, eggs). It is a component of cell membranes, myelin sheaths, and brain and nerve tissue, and elevated blood cholesterol leads to coronary artery disease. It has one polar OH (on C3) and an otherwise nonpolar frame, so it is weakly amphipathic and slips between the phospholipid tails of a membrane. There it acts as a fluidity buffer: it keeps the tails from packing into a rigid solid at low temperature and from flying apart at high temperature. Animal plasma membranes hold up to about one cholesterol for every phospholipid. Cholesterol is also the precursor (starting material) of every other steroid in the body and of vitamin D.

Bile salts (for example glycocholate and taurocholate) are made in the liver by oxidation of cholesterol, stored in the gallbladder, and released into the small intestine after a fatty meal. When too much cholesterol accumulates in the gallbladder it crystallizes as gallstones. The steroid frame is tilted so that its OH groups and the charged carboxylate or sulfonate side chain all face one way, giving a flat molecule with a polar face and a nonpolar face: amphipathic, like a soap. Bile salts emulsify dietary fat, breaking large globules into tiny droplets so that water-soluble lipase can reach the ester bonds. Without bile, most fat (and the fat-soluble vitamins dissolved in it) passes through unabsorbed.

Steroid hormones

Steroid hormones are chemical messengers made from cholesterol in the adrenal glands, ovaries, and testes. Being nonpolar, they cross cell membranes on their own and bind receptors inside the cell. The two important classes are the sex hormones (female: estrogens and progestins; male: androgens) and the adrenal corticosteroids (mineralocorticoids, which regulate the Na+/K+ electrolyte balance, and glucocorticoids, which regulate glucose level and inflammation). Progesterone, the "pregnancy hormone," prepares the uterus for a fertilized egg, and progesterone-like analogs are the basis of oral contraceptives; cortisone and cortisol (hydrocortisone) are the anti-inflammatory glucocorticoids in creams for swelling and itching; prednisone and inhaled steroid medications are synthetic corticosteroids. Fat-soluble vitamins: the other non-hydrolyzable family

Vitamins A, D, E, and K are nonpolar, so they are absorbed with dietary fat (bile salts and lipase required), carried in lipoproteins, and stored in the liver and fat tissue. Storage cuts both ways: a deficiency takes months to appear, but a large daily excess accumulates instead of being excreted in urine as the water-soluble B vitamins and vitamin C are. Fat-soluble vitamins are therefore the ones with real toxicity (hypervitaminosis). Vitamin D can be synthesized from cholesterol in a multistep process that starts with UVB light from the sun acting on the skin, so vitamin D is a modified steroid. Vitamin A is made in the body from β-carotene, the orange pigment of carrots. These four are the lipophilic ("fat-loving") vitamins: A (retinol), D3 (cholecalciferol), E (α-tocopherol), K (phylloquinone).

Clinical Connection: Nursing

A lipid panel is drawn after a 9–12 hour fast and reports four numbers in mg/dL. Cholesterol and triacylglycerols travel inside lipoproteins, particles with a phospholipid-and-protein shell (the LDL and HDL of the section text). Typical targets: total cholesterol below 200; LDL (the particle that delivers cholesterol to artery walls) below 100; HDL (the particle that returns cholesterol to the liver) 60 or higher is protective, below 40 is low; triglycerides (the lab name for triacylglycerols) below 150. Many labs calculate LDL as total cholesterol − HDL − triglycerides/5. Hyperlipidemia drives atherosclerosis: LDL lodged in an artery wall is oxidized and built into cholesterol-rich plaque. Statins inhibit HMG-CoA reductase, the enzyme that controls the liver's cholesterol synthesis, so the liver pulls more LDL from the blood. Vitamin K activates clotting factors; warfarin blocks vitamin K recycling, so a sudden change in leafy-green intake changes the INR. Because fat-soluble vitamins are stored, hypervitaminosis A and D are real risks of high-dose supplements. Bile is a cholesterol solution kept dissolved by bile salts and lecithin; when cholesterol outruns them it crystallizes as gallstones. Blocked bile flow or lipase deficiency causes steatorrhea (bulky, pale, floating stools of unabsorbed fat) and loss of vitamins A, D, E, and K.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Vitamin D and calcium support the alveolar bone that anchors every tooth; low vitamin D is associated with periodontal bone loss and poorer healing after extractions. Patients on long-term corticosteroids (prednisone, inhalers) heal more slowly and lose bone, which belongs in the health history before periodontal surgery.

Recognizing a lipid from its structure

Exam questions usually hand you a drawing and ask "what family of lipid is this?" or "is it hydrolyzable?" Do not be scared by a big molecule: look for the backbone and the functional groups, then use the table. The functional groups are the same ones you already know; what is new is only the pattern.
