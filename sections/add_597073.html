Lecture Connection

Four more ideas complete the picture of rings: the ring formula CnH2n, the numbering rules for rings with two or more substituents, the fact that rings of four or more carbons are not flat, and the fact that a ring cannot rotate about its C–C bonds, which is why two groups on a ring can be cis or trans.

Ring formulas and drawings

Closing a chain into a ring costs two hydrogens, so a cycloalkane with one ring is CnH2n: cyclopropane C3H6, cyclohexane C6H12. We draw rings as polygons with a CH2 at every corner; never condense a ring into a chain such as (CH2)6, because that hides the bond that closes the loop.

Naming rings

Rings: count the carbons in the ring and add cyclo- to the parent. With one substituent, that substituent is on carbon 1 and no number is needed (methylcyclopentane, chlorocyclohexane). With two or more, start numbering at a substituent and go the direction that gives the lowest set of numbers; if two choices give the same set, the substituent that comes first alphabetically gets C1. So a cyclohexane with Cl and CH3 on carbons 1 and 3 is 1-chloro-3-methylcyclohexane (chloro gets C1), and a cyclopentane with three methyls on adjacent carbons is 1,2,3-trimethylcyclopentane. Halogens are treated exactly like alkyl groups: they are substituents, never part of the parent name.

Most rings are not flat

  • Rings of four or more carbons are not flat (cyclopropane, with only three carbons, is flat). Cyclohexane folds into a "chair" shape that keeps every bond angle near 109.5°.
  • Because a ring cannot rotate, two substituents on a ring can be on the same face (cis) or opposite faces (trans): cis- and trans-1,2-dimethylcyclohexane are stereoisomers.

Why rings pucker. A flat hexagon has 120° corners, but a carbon with four single bonds wants 109.5°. Cyclohexane solves this by puckering: alternate carbons tip up and down, giving the chair shape in the figure below, in which every bond angle is close to 109.5°. Larger rings pucker for the same reason. Still, we draw rings as flat polygons, because the drawing is only a map of which atoms are bonded, not a photograph of the shape.

One more point is worth knowing even though it is optional in this course: because a ring prevents rotation about its C–C bonds, a ring has two faces. If two substituents sit on the same face of the ring they are cis; on opposite faces, trans. Because the ring bonds cannot rotate all the way around, the two arrangements cannot interconvert, so they are different compounds (stereoisomers) with different melting points and boiling points. You will meet the same cis/trans idea again with alkenes in Chapter 3.

Professor Tip

Keep drawing rings as flat polygons; the drawing is a map of connectivity, not a photograph. Cis/trans isomers on rings are optional in this course: recognize that they exist, but the cis/trans questions on exams are about alkenes (Section 3.2).

Example 1: Naming a disubstituted cycloalkane

Problem. A cyclopentane ring carries a bromine on one carbon and an ethyl group on the carbon next to it. Name it and give its molecular formula.

Solution. The parent is cyclopentane. With two substituents, numbering must start at one of them. Starting at either substituent and going toward the other gives the set {1, 2}, a tie, so the alphabetical rule decides: bromo precedes ethyl and gets C1. Name: 1-bromo-2-ethylcyclopentane.

Formula: ethylcyclopentane is C7H14 (CnH2n for a one-ring compound with 7 carbons), and the bromine replaces one hydrogen: C7H13Br.

Check. Would "2-bromo-1-ethylcyclopentane" also give the locants {1, 2}? Yes, but it breaks the alphabetical tie incorrectly; only one name is right. Notice also that this compound exists as cis and trans forms, because the ring holds the two groups on the same face or on opposite faces. ✓
