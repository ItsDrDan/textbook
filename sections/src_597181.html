Viruses are visible only under an electron microscope. They come in a variety of shapes, ranging from spherical to rod shaped. The fact that they contain either deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) or ribonucleic acid (RNA)—but never both—allows them to be divided into two major classes: DNA viruses and RNA viruses (Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\)).

Most RNA viruses use their nucleic acids in much the same way as the DNA viruses, penetrating a host cell and inducing it to replicate the viral RNA and synthesize viral proteins. The new RNA strands and viral proteins are then assembled into new viruses. Some RNA viruses, however, called retroviruses (Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\)), synthesize DNA in the host cell, in a process that is the reverse of the DNA-to-RNA transcription that normally occurs in cells. The synthesis of DNA from an RNA template is catalyzed by the enzyme reverse transcriptase.

In 1987, azidothymidine (AZT, also known as zidovudine or the brand name Retrovir) became the first drug approved for the treatment of AIDS. It works by binding to reverse transcriptase in place of deoxythymidine triphosphate, after which, because AZT does not have a 3′OH group, further replication is blocked. Many other reverse transcriptase inhibitors have been approved since then.

Raltegravir (Isentress) was the first drug to block integrase, the viral enzyme that inserts HIV DNA into the host cell’s DNA, an essential step in making more HIV particles. Integrase inhibitors are now a core part of most HIV treatment.

HIV can become resistant to any single drug, so people with HIV take a combination of drugs that block different viral enzymes, today usually an integrase inhibitor plus reverse transcriptase inhibitors, often combined in one pill a day. With steady treatment the amount of virus in the blood can fall so low that standard tests cannot detect it, and a person whose virus stays undetectable cannot pass HIV to sexual partners (U=U, “undetectable = untransmittable”). People without HIV who are at risk can take some of the same drugs as PrEP (pre-exposure prophylaxis) to prevent infection.

Summary

Viruses are very small infectious agents that contain either DNA or RNA as their genetic material. The human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) causes acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS).

Molecule in the Clinic: Acyclovir for cold sores

Cold sores (herpes labialis), which dental hygienists see often, are treated with acyclovir. Acyclovir looks like the nucleoside guanosine, but its “sugar” is an open chain with no 3′-OH. The viral enzyme thymidine kinase activates it, so it acts mainly in infected cells; the viral DNA polymerase then adds it to the growing DNA strand.

Your turn: Why does the growing viral DNA strand stop once acyclovir is added?

Answer
The next nucleotide must form a phosphodiester bond to a 3′-OH, and acyclovir has none. The chain cannot be extended (chain termination).
