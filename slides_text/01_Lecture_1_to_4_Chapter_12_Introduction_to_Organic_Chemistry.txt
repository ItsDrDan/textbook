What is Organic Chemistry

--- slide 12 ---
Organic Chemistry
Old definition: the study of organic compounds are derived from living sources, i.e. organisms. 
Modern definition: the study of compounds of carbon
Carbon + Hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen
Sometimes: Phosphorous, Sulfur, and Halogens (Fluorine, Chlorine, Bromine, Iodine)
Carbohydrates, fats (lipids), proteins, enzymes, nucleic acids, hormones, and vitamins are organic compounds.
Even plastics are organic

--- slide 13 ---
Organic vs. Inorganic Compounds
Organic compounds exist as discrete molecules. They are all molecular compounds. 
Inorganic Compounds can be molecular or ionic.
Molecular compounds are held together by covalent bonds (electrons are shared)
Ionic compounds are held together by ionic bonds (electrostatic attraction between opposite charges)
Intermolecular forces between molecules are much weaker than the forces between ions in ionic compounds.
Molecular compounds tend to have lower melting points and boiling points than ionic compounds
At room temperature, gases and liquids tend to be molecular compounds. Solids are either really big molecules or ionic compounds.

--- slide 14 ---
Organic vs. Inorganic Compounds
Your book tends to make generalizations about properties as that of organic vs. inorganic instead of molecular vs. ionic. 
Properties are more accurately generalized by molecular vs. ionic compounds.

--- slide 15 ---
Functional Groups
Organic molecules as classified by functional groups
Chemical Properties and Reactions of organic molecules are determined by the functional groups on the molecule
A given functional group tends to undergo the same reactions in every molecule that contains it.
A functional group is a heteroatom, a multiple bond, or sometimes both.
A heteroatom is any element that is not carbon or hydrogen
A molecule can have more than one functional group
The letter R is used to abbreviate the carbon-hydrogen (unimportant) part of the molecule.
=    R-OH

--- slide 20 ---
Structural Formulas: Lewis Dot Structure
Lewis Dot Structure
Electrons are shown as dots
Lines represent a covalent bond between atoms
1 line = 2 shared electrons (single bond)
2 lines = 4 shared electrons (double bond)
Octet Rule (8 electrons)
Carbon: 4 covalent bonds, 0 Lone Pair
Nitrogen: 3 covalent bonds, 1 Lone Pair
Oxygen: 2 covalent bonds, 2 lone pairs
Hydrogen will always have just one covalent bond (duet)
Total electrons
2(C) + 2(O) + 4(H) = 
2(4 e-) + 2(6 e-) + 4(1 e-) = 24 e-

--- slide 43 ---
Hydrocarbons
Hydrocarbons are composed only of carbon and hydrogen atoms.
Saturated hydrocarbons contains only single carbon-carbon bonds
(aka Alkanes)
i.e. saturated with hydrogen. They have the maximum number of H’s per C.
The general formula for acyclic alkanes is CnH2n+2 where n is the number of carbons in the compound.
Acyclic means not part of a ring (circular loop of carbons)
Octane

--- slide 44 ---
Hydrocarbons: Unsaturated
Unsaturated hydrocarbons contains one or more multiple bond (double or triple bond)
Alkenes contain a carbon–carbon double-bond as their functional group.
Alkynes contain a carbon–carbon triple-bond as their functional group.
Aromatic compounds contain a six-membered ring of carbon atoms with three alternating double bonds.
Carbon makes a total of 4 bonds

--- slide 76 ---
Making Predictions
Melting Point & Boiling Point
Consider intermolecular forces between separate molecules of the same compound.
Which compound has stronger intermolecular forces between molecules?
The compound with the stronger intermolecular forces will have a higher melting point and higher boiling point
Solubility
Consider intermolecular forces between solute molecules and solvent molecules
Are the strengths of the forces similar to that between just solvent molecules?
If so, the solvent will be soluble. The stronger the attractive forces between solute and solvent, the more soluble it is.
Covalent compounds do not ionize! It stays a molecule – just surrounded by solvent molecules.
