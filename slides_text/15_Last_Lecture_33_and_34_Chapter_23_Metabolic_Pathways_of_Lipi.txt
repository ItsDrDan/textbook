Digestion of Lipids
Excess energy derived from carbohydrates and proteins, beyond normal daily needs, is stored in lipid molecules in adipocyte cells to be used later when needed.
About 98% of dietary lipids are triacylglycerols.
Salivary enzymes (water soluble) in the mouth have little effect on triacylglycerols.
Triacylglycerols are insoluble in water
Bile salts, secreted from the gallbladder into the small intestine, emulsify triacylglycerols – break fat globules into smaller particles called micelles.
The bile salts act like soaps to make fat soluble in water.

--- slide 7 ---
Monoacylglycerols
Micelles of free fatty acids and monoacylglycerols go through the membranes of intestinal cells.
In the intestinal wall, the fatty acids recombine with monoacylglycerols – repackaging.
The recombined triacylglycerols are coated with phospholipids and proteins – chylomicrons.
Chylomicrons are water soluble.

--- slide 8 ---
Fat Storage
Chylomicrons transport triacylglycerols from intestinal cells, via the lymphatic system, to the bloodstream. 
Triacylglycerols are stored in adipocyte cells found in adipose tissue.
The adipose tissue is located primarily beneath the skin, especially in the abdominal region and vital organs.
The adipocyte cells differ from other cells in that most of the cytoplasm has been replaced with a large triacylglycerol droplet.

--- slide 9 ---
Complete Hydrolysis
When not enough carbohydrates are present to meet the body’s energy needs, the body turns to catabolizing stored triacylglycerols.
The triacylglycerols are completely hydrolyzed with lipoprotein lipase enzymes to glycerol and fatty acids.

--- slide 10 ---
Fat Storage
The hormones glucagon and epinephrine trigger the hydrolysis of triacylglycerols by activating a lipase.
The triacylglycerols are completely hydrolyzed with the lipase into glycerol and fatty acids.
Glycerol and fatty acids binds with plasma proteins (albumin) in the bloodstream and are transported to the tissues, muscles and fat cells.

Metabolism of Glycerol
Glycerol travels to the liver where it is converted to dihydroxyacetone phosphate in two steps:
Phosphorylation of 1o hydroxyl group of the glycerol.
Oxidation of 2o alcohol group of glycerol to a ketone.
Dihydroxyacetone phosphate is an intermediate of glycolysis and gluconeogenesis, therefore can enter either pathway.

Metabolism of Fatty Acids
Fatty acids are broken down to obtain energy in 3 stages:
Fatty acid must be activated by binding to coenzyme A. 
Fatty acid must be transported to the mitochondrial matrix.
Fatty acid must be repeatedly (fatty acid spiral) oxidized to produce acetyl CoA, FADH2, and NADH.

--- slide 45 ---
Ketone Bodies and Diabetes
In diabetes, 
insulin does not function properly.
glucose levels are insufficient for energy needs
fats are broken down to acetyl-CoA.
ketogenesis produces ketone bodies.
ketone bodies are soluble in blood and urine.
acetone is volatile and is mainly excreted by exhalation. It is detectable in the breath of a diabetic. 

--- slide 46 ---
Ketosis
The buildup of ketone bodies in urine (due to starvation, vigorous dieting, alcoholism, and uncontrolled diabetes is called ketosis.
Symptoms of mild ketosis: headache, dry mouth, and sometimes acetone-smelling breath.
Ketosis can develop into ketoacidosis, where the acidic ketone bodies actually lower the pH of the bloodstream.
Symptoms of ketoacidosis: heavy breathing and increased urine output that can lead to dehydration. Ultimately can cause coma and death.

--- slide 59 ---
Lipogenesis: Chain Elongation
Further cycles of the preceding four-step process convert the 4-C acyl group to a 6-C acyl group, then to an 8-C acyl group, and so on.
Elongation of the acyl group chain through this procedure stops upon formation of the C16 acyl group (palmitic acid).
Shorter fatty acids undergo fewer cycles and are released earlier in biosynthesis.
Longer fatty acids are synthesized with special enzymes from the C16 fatty acid by adding C2 units on the carboxyl end of the fatty acid.

Amino Acid Pool
The amino acid pool is the total supply of free amino acids available for use in the human body. 
The amino acid pool has 3 sources:

biosynthesis of amino acids in the liver (only non-essential amino acids)

--- slide 70 ---
Nitrogen Balance
Nitrogen balance occurs when the amount of nitrogen taken into the human body as protein equals the amount of nitrogen excreted from the body in waste materials.
Two types of nitrogen imbalance can occur:
negative nitrogen balance
positive nitrogen balance

--- slide 71 ---
Negative Nitrogen Balance
Negative nitrogen balance:
Protein degradation exceeds protein synthesis. 
Tissue is wasted – more tissue proteins are being catabolized than are being replaced by protein synthesis.
Due to protein-poor diets, starvation, and wasting illnesses.

--- slide 72 ---
Positive Nitrogen Balance
Positive nitrogen balance:
Protein synthesis exceeds protein degradation. 
Large amount of tissues are synthesized.
Due to growth, pregnancy, and convalescence from an emaciating illness.

Amino Acid Carbon Skeletons
The removal of the amino group of an amino acid by transamination/oxidative deamination produces an a-keto acid that contains the carbon skeleton from the amino acid. 
Each amino acid has a different degradation pathway for its carbon skeleton. 
The degradation can occur in one or several steps.
20 amino acids = only 7 degradation products
Some amino acids appear more than once:
either they degrade by different pathways
or parts of their skeletons degrade by different pathways

--- slide 90 ---
Amino Acid Carbon Skeletons
Amino acids that are degraded to citric acid cycle intermediates can serve as glucose precursors and are called glucogenic.
Amino acids that are degraded to acetyl CoA or acetoacetyl CoA can contribute to the formation of fatty acids or ketone bodies are called ketogenic.
