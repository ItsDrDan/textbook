At any given moment in time, the electrons in an atom or molecule can create an instantaneous (temporary) dipole
This temporary dipole may induce a temporary dipole on nearby molecules, causing them to be attracted to one another.
Since all atoms/molecules have electrons, Dispersion Forces can occur between any atom and/or molecule.

Dipole-Dipole Forces
A dipole-dipole force is an attractive force between polar molecules (permanent dipoles)

Opposite charges attract

--- slide 15 ---
Hydrogen Bonding
Hydrogen Bonding is an extra strong dipole-dipole force that occurs between:
A lone pair on N, O, or F on one molecule   AND
A hydrogen covalently bonded to N, O, or F on neighboring molecule
N, O, and F are the three most electronegative elements. 
They pull electrons away from H, making the H almost +1 charged. Hδ+ highly attracted to lone pairs.

--- slide 17 ---
Making Predictions
Melting Point & Boiling Point
Consider intermolecular forces between separate molecules of the same compound.
Which compound has stronger intermolecular forces between molecules?
The compound with the stronger intermolecular forces will have a higher melting point and higher boiling point
Solubility
Consider intermolecular forces between solute molecules and solvent molecules
Are the strengths of the forces similar to that between just solvent molecules?
If so, the solvent will be soluble. The stronger the attractive forces between solute and solvent, the more soluble it is.
Covalent compounds do not ionize! It stays a molecule – just surrounded by solvent molecules.

Cis and Trans Isomers
Cis = groups are on the same side of the double bond (not bonded to same C)

--- slide 39 ---
Are cis- and trans- isomers possible for all alkenes?
When the two groups on one end of the double bond are identical, no cis- and trans- isomers are possible.
Whenever the two groups on each end of a C═C are different from each other, two isomers are possible

Aromatic Compounds

--- slide 44 ---
What are Aromatic Compounds?
Aromatic compounds are cyclic and planar with conjugated double bonds (alternating double and single bonds)
Benzene has 3 double bonds, so it’s aromatic
Aromatic compounds are very stable and not very reactive
They are a separate functional group because they react differently than regular alkenes.
The actual structure is a hybrid of all resonance structures.
The 6 electrons in the double bonds are delocalized (shared equally) between all carbons in the ring.

--- slide 62 ---
Markovnikov’s Rule
To determine which of the two products will actually form, we use Markovnikov’s rule.
Markovnikov’s rule states that:
 the H atom of H–X will bond to the less substituted C atom in the C-C double bond.
The X atom to the most substituted C atom.

--- slide 63 ---
Markovnikov’s Rule
Hydrohalogenation is regioselective. One direction of bond breaking/forming occurs
One possible intermediate far more stable than the other

--- slide 64 ---
Markovnikov’s Rule
If same number of H atoms attached to the double-bond carbons (a symmetrically-substituted double bond), both possible products are formed in approximately equal amounts.

--- slide 78 ---
Conclusions
Alkenes and Alkynes can undergo addition reactions with halogens, water, or binary acids (HX)
Follow Markovnikov’s rule
Alkenes and alkynes can form polymers
Aromatic compounds, like benzene, are special classes of alkenes that are cyclic and planar
Benzene compounds are named with “benzene” or special mono-substituted benzene names as the root
Positions of substituents are numbered
Aromatic compounds are generally more stable and less reactive than alkenes
Need catalyst to undergo electrophilic aromatic substitution

Alkanes have single bonds, Alkenes have double bonds, Alkynes have triple bonds
