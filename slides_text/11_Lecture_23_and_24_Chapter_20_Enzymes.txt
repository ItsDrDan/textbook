Enzymes: Structure and FunctionLearning Objective: Describe enzymes and their role in enzyme catalyzed reactions

--- slide 4 ---
Enzyme Structure
Enzymes are proteins that serve as biological catalysts for reactions in all living organisms
Enzymes can have a non-protein part that is essential to enzyme’s catalytic activity.
An inorganic substance (i.e. metal ion) that binds to an enzyme is called a cofactor 
An organic molecule that binds to enzymes is called a coenzyme

--- slide 5 ---
Enzyme Structure: Globular
Most enzymes are globular proteins: Globe-shaped/round

--- slide 6 ---
Enzyme Function: Catalysts
Catalysts are un-changed in a chemical reaction
Catalysts lower the activation energy for a reaction
Catalysts provide an alternative pathway for a reaction
Enzymes increase the rate of a reaction (106 to 1012 times faster)
Enzymes are the most effective catalysts known

--- slide 7 ---
Enzyme Function: Specificity
Enzymes are very specific.
Three categories of specificity: 
Absolute specificity: one substrate only
Group specificity: one type of substrate
Linkage specificity: one type of bond
Enzymes are also specific with respect to stereochemistry.

--- slide 8 ---
Models of Enzyme Action
An enzyme contains an active site that binds the substrate, forming an enzyme-substrate complex.
The active site is formed due to the folding and bending of the protein.
Once the reaction has occurred, the catalyst released the product(s). The catalyst is regenerated.
Two models have been proposed to explain the specificity of a substrate for an enzyme’s active site.

--- slide 9 ---
Lock & Key Model
In the lock-and-key model, the active site is a rigid cavity; to react the substrate must exactly match the shape of the active site.

--- slide 10 ---
Induced-Fit Model
In the induced-fit model, the active site has a flexible shape, which can adjust to fit a variety of substrate shapes.

--- slide 14 ---
Enzyme Classification: Names
The name of an enzymes is based on the reaction it catalyzes or the substrate involved in the reaction.
The names of most enzymes end with the suffix “-ase”
The type of reaction of an enzyme is often used as a prefix.
Often the identity of the substrate is used in addition of the type of reaction.
Enzymes are classified into six categories by the type of reaction they catalyze.

--- slide 15 ---
Oxidoreductases
Oxidoreductases catalyze oxidation-reduction reactions.
When a substrate is oxidized or reduced, a cofactor (coenzyme) is required, which serves as the oxidizing or reducing agent.

Transferases
Transferases catalyze the transfer of a functional group from one molecule to another.
Transaminases: transfer of an amino group
Kinases: transfer of phosphate group

--- slide 17 ---
Hydrolases
Hydrolases catalyze hydrolysis reactions – the cleavage of bonds with water.
Proteases: hydrolysis of peptide bond in proteins
Lipases: hydrolysis of ester bond in lipid
Nucleases: hydrolysis of phosphate –ester bonds in nucleic acids
What type of hydrolase is shown here?

--- slide 18 ---
Lyases
Lyases catalyze the addition or removal of a small molecule. (no hydrolysis involved)
Decarboxylases: remove CO2
Deaminases: remove NH3
Dehydratases: remove H2O
Hydratases: add H2O

--- slide 19 ---
Isomerases
Isomerases catalyze the conversion of one isomer to another.

--- slide 20 ---
Ligases
Ligases catalyze bond formation accompanied by energy release from a hydrolysis reaction (ATP to ADP).
This process is energetically unfavorable, so the energy released in a hydrolysis reaction is used to drive the reaction.
Synthetases: form bond between two substrates
Carboxylases: form bond between CO2 and substrate

--- slide 24 ---
Enzyme Activity
The catalytic activity of an enzyme is measured by its turnover number: the maximum number of substrate molecules acted upon by one molecule of enzyme per unit time.
Turnover numbers range from 10 to 10,000,000 molecules per second.
Enzymes undergo all the reactions of proteins including denaturation
Changes in temperature and/or pH will affect the enzyme catalytic activity.

--- slide 25 ---
The charge on acidic and basic amino acids located at the active site depends on pH. 
Small changes in pH (less than one unit) can result in denaturation and subsequent loss of catalytic activity.
Need buffers to keep pH around optimum pH.
pH
[notes] Changing pH can denature protein

--- slide 26 ---
pH: Optimum
The optimum pH is the pH at which the enzyme exhibits maximum activity, usually within the physiological pH range of 7.0–7.5.
Exception: 
Pepsin: Optimum pH = 2.0 
Trypsin: Optimum pH = 8.0

--- slide 27 ---
Temperature: Reaction Rate
As temperature increases, the molecules move faster.
The faster the molecules, the more collisions, the faster the reaction.
The more molecules with enough energy, the more collisions with enough energy, the faster the reaction.

--- slide 28 ---
Temperature
Too high temperatures cause disruptions in the tertiary structure of the enzyme – denaturation.
Change in the  tertiary structure at the active site impedes catalytic action
enzyme activity quickly decreases as the temperature climbs past this point
The optimum temperature is the temperature at which the enzyme exhibits maximum activity, usually around 37 oC.

--- slide 29 ---
SubstrateConcentration
As concentration increases, the number of collisions increases.
The more collisions, the faster the reaction.
There is a maximum reaction rate – saturation.
When each enzyme is working at full capacity, the incoming substrate molecules must “wait their turn” for an empty active site.
[notes] Each substrate must occupy an enzyme active site for a finite amount of time, and the products must leave the site before the cycle can be repeated.

--- slide 30 ---
EnzymeConcentration
At a constant substrate concentration, enzyme activity increases with the increase in enzyme concentration.
More substrate molecules can be accommodated in a given amount of time.

--- slide 33 ---
Enzyme Regulation
Cells continually produce large amounts of an enzyme and plentiful amounts of a product.
Too much enzyme for low substrate concentration is a waste of energy.
Having more product than needed is also a waste of energy.
The rates of enzyme-catalyzed reactions can be controlled by regulatory enzymes.
The reaction rate can be increased when more of a particular substance is needed – activation.
The reaction rate can be decreased when a substance is not needed – inhibition.

--- slide 34 ---
Allosteric Control: Negative
Some enzymes change their conformation when a regulator molecule binds to it.
Allosteric control is the binding of a regulator to a site on an enzyme that affects the enzyme’s ability to bind a substrate at its active site.
Not all enzymes possess sites for allosteric binding. Those that do are called allosteric enzymes.
Negative allosteric control occurs when a regulator makes the active site less able to bind the substrate, and the rate of reaction decreases.
[notes] Nail in Shoe

--- slide 35 ---
Allosteric Control: Positive
Positive allosteric control occurs when a regulator makes the active site more able to bind the substrate, and the rate of the reaction increases.
[notes] Memory foam shoe insole

--- slide 36 ---
Feedback Control
Feedback control is an enzyme-regulation process where  the product of a series of enzyme-catalyzed reactions inhibits an earlier reaction in the sequence.
If D inhibits enzyme 1, the amount of A and B synthesized decreases when no more D is needed. 
When more D is needed, enzyme 1 will no longer be inhibited.

--- slide 37 ---
Covalent Modification
Covalent modification is a process in which enzyme activity is altered by covalently modifying the structure of the enzyme:
by adding a chemical group 
or removing a chemical group

--- slide 38 ---
Zymogens
Some enzymes are synthesized in an inactive form – zymogens (or proenzymes).
These inactive enzyme precursors are:
transported to the part of the body where the active form is needed.
and then “turned on” when needed by covalent modification.

--- slide 39 ---
Example: Insulin
Covalent Modification: Covalent bonds are broken/formed to make enzyme active/inactive
Insulin becomes biologically active when the polypeptide chain of 33 amino acids is removed by peptidases from the zymogen proinsulin.

--- slide 40 ---
Phosphorylation
An inactive enzyme undergoes covalent modification in which an enzyme:
can be activated by the addition of a phosphate group – phosphorylation with a kinase.
can be deactivated by the removal of a phosphate group group – dephosphorylation with a phosphatase.

--- slide 41 ---
Enzyme Inhibitors
Inhibitors are substances that slow down or stop the catalytic activity of an enzyme by binding to the enzyme.
A reversible inhibitor binds to an enzyme but then enzyme activity is restored when the inhibitor is released.
An irreversible inhibitor covalently binds to an enzyme, permanently destroying its activity.

--- slide 42 ---
Reversible Inhibitors
Non-Competitive Inhibitor
Bonds to the enzyme, but not to the active site.
Distorts the shape of the enzyme - Prevents the binding of the substrate at the active site. 
Cannot have its effect reversed by adding more substrate.
Competitive Inhibitor
Bonds to the enzyme in the active site.
Has structure/polarity similar to that of substrate
Effects reversed by increasing substrate concentration
[notes] Start Here

--- slide 43 ---
Irreversible Inhibitors
An irreversible inhibitor:
covalently bonds with R groups of an amino acid that may be near the active site.
changes the shape of the enzyme, which prevents the substrate from entering the active site.
destroys enzyme activity.
Lead poisoning from paint, mercury poisoning from fish, chemical warfare agents (nerve gases) and organophosphate insecticides

--- slide 44 ---
Example
Penicillin is an antibiotic
Penicillin inhibits transpeptidase, an enzyme that catalyzes the formation of peptide cross-links between polysaccharide strands in bacterial cell walls. 
If the cell walls of bacteria are weak then it is easier to break the cell.

Cofactors
Learning Objective: Describe the types of cofactors found in enzymes.

--- slide 47 ---
Enzyme Cofactors
Some enzymes need to be combined with a a non-protein component in order to be active.
A cofactor is an inorganic substance such as metal ions.
A coenzyme is an organic molecule (vitamins).

--- slide 48 ---
Metal Ions
Many enzymes require metal ion cofactors
Cofactors can be tightly held or loosely bound, so that they can enter and leave the active site.
The ions form coordinate covalent bonds with nitrogen or oxygen.
Zn2+, a cofactor for carboxypeptidase, stabilizes the carbonyl oxygen during the hydrolysis of a peptide bond of a C-terminal aromatic amino acid.
[notes] Iron: Heme (hemoglobin)
Zinc, Magnesium: DNA polymerase

--- slide 49 ---
Vitamins
Coenzymes are synthesized in the human body using building blocks obtained from other nutrients, such as vitamins.
Vitamins cannot be synthesized by the body, therefore must be obtained from the diet.
Only a few mg to mg are needed per day.

--- slide 50 ---
Vitamins: Overview
Water-soluble Vitamins
are carried in the bloodstream.
are rapidly eliminated from the body in the urine. 
must be constantly replenished. 
are unlikely to be toxic.
Fat-soluble Vitamins
are carried in the blood by protein carriers.
are stored in fat tissues.
are needed in periodic doses.
are more likely to be toxic.

Vitamin C – Ascorbic Acid
Vitamin C:
is found in blueberries, citrus fruits, strawberries, tomatoes.
has an RDA of 75–90 mg.
is required in collagen synthesis and healing of wounds
is necessary to avoid scurvy: bleeding gums, weakened connective tissues, slow-healing wounds, and anemia.

--- slide 55 ---
The B Vitamins
The B vitamins serve:
as precursors for enzyme cofactors.
as temporary carriers of atoms or functional groups in redox and group transfer reactions associated with metabolism of ingested food in order to obtain energy

--- slide 56 ---
Vitamin B1 – Thiamin
Vitamin B1, associated with coenzyme thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP):
is found in the liver, yeast, whole grain bread, cereals, and milk.
has an RDA of 1.2 mg.
is necessary to avoid beriberi, whose symptoms are fatigue, poor appetite, and weight loss.

--- slide 57 ---
Vitamin B2 – Riboflavin
Vitamin B2, associated with coenzymes flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) and flavin mononucleotide (FMN):
is found in the liver, beef, chicken, eggs, green leafy vegetables, dairy foods, peanuts, and whole grains.
has an RDA of 1.2–1.8 mg.
is necessary to avoid dermatitis; dry skin; red, sore tongue; and cataract.
[notes] FAD!!

is necessary to avoid pellagra: dermatitis, muscle fatigue, loss of appetite, diarrhea, and mouth sores.
[notes] NAD+
