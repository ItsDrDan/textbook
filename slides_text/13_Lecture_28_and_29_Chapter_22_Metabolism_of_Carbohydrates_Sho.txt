Metabolism
Metabolism is the sum of all the chemical reactions that take place in an organism – providing energy for the body
Catabolism is the breakdown of large molecules into smaller ones
Energy is generally released during catabolism
Anabolism is the synthesis of large molecules from smaller ones
Energy is generally absorbed during anabolism
Learning Goal: Define catabolic reactions and anabolic reactions. 
[notes] Energy released = catabolic
Energy absorbed = anabolic

--- slide 4 ---
Metabolic Pathways
Linear Metabolic Pathway 
A series of reactions that generates a final product. 
Product is different from any of the reactants.
Cyclic Pathway
A series of reactions that regenerates the first reactant.
[notes] Often, the process is a series of consecutive reactions called a metabolic pathway, which can be linear or cyclic.

Coupled Reactions
Some processes are energetically unfavorable
Coupling an energetically unfavorable reaction with a favorable one that releases more energy than the amount required is common in biological reactions.
Coupled reactions are pairs of reactions that occur together.
The energy released by one reaction is absorbed by the other reaction.

a		gain	of electrons.
a		loss		of oxygen atoms.

--- slide 18 ---
Coenzymes in Metabolism: Redox
Coenzymes are required in redox reactions to carry the hydrogen ions and electrons from or to the substrate.
A coenzyme acting as an oxidizing agent causes an oxidation reaction to occur, so the coenzyme is reduced.
It gains H+ and e−.
A coenzyme acting as a reducing agent causes a reduction reaction to occur, so the coenzyme is oxidized.
It loses H+ and e−.

Stage 1: Digestion of Carbohydrates
Digestion is the breakdown of food molecules by hydrolysis into simpler chemical units that can be used by cells for metabolic processes
Begins in the mouth: amylase breaks down polysaccharides into smaller polysaccharides (dextrins).
continues in the small intestine, where pancreatic amylase hydrolyzes dextrins to maltose and glucose
hydrolyzes maltose, lactose, and sucrose to monosaccharides, mostly glucose, which enter the bloodstream for transport to the cells

Glycolysis: Overall Process
The net overall equation for the process of glycolysis is:
Two ATP add phosphate to glucose and fructose-6-phosphate.
There is a net gain of two ATP molecules for every glucose molecule converted into two pyruvates.

--- slide 56 ---
Other Carbohydrates
The breakdown products from carbohydrate digestion are glucose, galactose, and fructose.
Both galactose and fructose are converted, in the liver, to intermediates that enter into the glycolysis pathway.
All the common hexoses enter glycolysis and are metabolized into pyruvate.

--- slide 57 ---
Regulation of Glycolysis
The amount of glucose broken down depends on the need for pyruvate and ATP
Glycolysis is regulated by three enzymes:
Hexokinase: Step 1 of glycolysis
Inhibited by high levels of glucose-6-phosphate
Phosphofructokinase:  Step 3 of glycolysis
Allosteric Control – inhibited by high levels of ATP, activated by high levels of ADP or AMP
Pyruvate Kinase: Step 10 of glycolysis
Inhibited by high levels of ATP or acetyl CoA
[notes] In biochemistry, allosteric regulation (or allosteric control) is the regulation of a protein by binding an effector molecule at a site other than the enzyme's active site.

Glucose Metabolism: Gluconeogenesis

--- slide 67 ---
Gluconeogenesis
Gluconeogenesis is the synthesis of glucose from non-carbohydrate sources such as pyruvate, lactate, amino acids, or glycerol.
Without gluconeogenesis, the brain, which is dependent on glucose as a fuel would have problems functioning if food intake were restricted for even one day.
Catabolic or anabolic pathway? anabolic
About 90% of gluconeogenesis takes place in the liver. 
Gluconeogenesis, because of its ATP-consuming nature, occurs only under specific conditions, which include:
To replenish depleted liver glycogen stores. 
To convert lactate (produced by strenuous exercise) back to glucose.
To maintain blood glucose level when glycogen stores have been depleted.

Glycogen
Glycogen
Branched polymer of glucose with α(14) glycosidic bonds and α(16) in branches.
Glycogen stored mainly in liver and muscle
operates when high levels of glucose-6-phosphate are formed in the first reaction of glycolysis

Glycogenesis:

occurs when high levels of glucose-6-phosphate are formed in the first reaction of glycolysis.
does not occur when energy stores (glycogen) are full, which means that additional glucose is converted to triacylglycerols and stored as body fat.
 involves three steps.

--- slide 80 ---
Glycogenolysis
Glycogenolysis is: 
the metabolic pathway by which glucose 6-phosphate is produced from glycogen. 
NOT the reverse of glycogen synthesis (glycogenesis) because it does not require UTP or UDP molecules.
a two-step process.

--- slide 81 ---
Glycogenolysis: Step 1
In step 1, a glucose unit is removed by phosphorylation from a glycogen molecule as glucose 1-phosphate using the glycogen phosphorylase enzyme.
No UDP involved. Therefore this is NOT the reverse of step 3 of glycogenesis. 

--- slide 82 ---
Glycogenolysis: Step 2
In step 2, glucose 1-phosphate is isomerized to glucose 6-phosphate using the phosphoglucomutase enzyme.
This step is the reverse of step 1 of glycogenesis.
The glucose-6-phosphate formed in step 2 of glycogenolysis, can enter glycolysis at step 2 for energy production.

Pentose Phosphate Pathway
Glucose can be degraded by a pathway different from glycolysis – the pentose phosphate pathway.
Major functions of this alternative pathway are:
synthesis of the coenzyme NADPH needed in lipid biosynthesis.
and production of ribose 5-phosphate, a pentose derivative needed for the synthesis of nucleic acids and many coenzymes.
Two step Pathway

Conclusions
Carbohydrates are they body’s main source of energy
Polysaccharides are digested into glucose molecules
Glucose is converted into pyruvate via glycolysis
