--- slide 3 ---
Carbohydrates
Carbohydrates are composed of C, H, and O
Once thought to be hydrates of carbon
Also called saccharides, which means “sugars”
65% of our diet (a major source of energy!)
Carbohydrates are polyhydroxyaldehydes or polyhydroxyketones
The simplest carbohydrates are monosaccharides

--- slide 4 ---
Carbohydrates in life
Plants make carbohydrates by photosynthesis to store energy
Carbohydrates provide energy when oxidized
Carbohydrate storage, in the form of glycogen, provides a short-term energy reserve.
Carbohydrates supply carbons for other biological molecules (proteins, lipids, nucleic acids)
Carbohydrates form part of the structural framework of DNA and RNA

Classification of Monosaccharides

--- slide 6 ---
Classification: Aldoses and Ketoses
Monosaccharides are classified by both their functional group (aldehyde or ketone) as well as the number of carbons in their chain
General formula CnH2nOn where n varies from 3-8 (most common are 3-6 carbons)
Suffix –ose indicates the molecule is a carbohydrate

--- slide 7 ---
Classification: Number of Carbons
A monosaccharide is also characterized by the number of carbon atoms in the chain.
A triose has 3 C’s.
A tetrose has 4 C’s.
A pentose has 5 C’s
A hexose has 6 C’s
The terms are then combined with the words aldose and ketose:
Glyceraldehyde is an aldotriose.
Dihydroxyacetone is a ketotriose.

--- slide 8 ---
Isomerism
Aldoses and ketoses with the same number of carbons are functional group isomers (a type of constitutional isomer)
An aldotriose
A ketotriose

Physical Properties
Monosaccharides are sweet tasting, but their relative sweetness varies greatly
Would you expect monosaccharides to have a high melting point? Why or why not?
High melting point because:
They are polar: dipole-dipole intermolecular forces (IMFs)
They have lots of hydroxy groups so they are capable of hydrogen bonding (donor and acceptor)
Would you expect monosaccharides to be water soluble? Why or why not?
Very water soluble!
Polar and capable of hydrogen-bonding with water

--- slide 11 ---
Chirality: Enantiomers
All carbohydrates have at least one chiral centers (4 different groups bonded to a carbon)
These two molecules are non-superimposable mirror images. They are two of glyceraldehyde.

--- slide 12 ---
D- and L- Notation
The prefix D- is used when the  –OH group on the  bottom-most chiral carbon is drawn on the right side of the carbon chain
The prefix L- is used when the  –OH group on the  bottom-most chiral carbon is drawn on the left side of the carbon chain
In a Fischer projection, chiral carbons are replaced with a cross

--- slide 13 ---
Chirality: Diastereomers
Enantiomers: Stereoisomers whose molecules are non-superimposable mirror images of each other.
Diastereomers: Stereoisomers whose molecules are not mirror images of each other.
Epimers: Diastereomers whose molecules differ only in the configuration at one chiral center.
C-4 epimer of D-glucose
C-3 epimer of D-glucose

--- slide 14 ---
Natural Selection
D- glucose is a mirror image of L-glucose
Naturally occurring sugars are pretty much always the D- isomer

Common Monosaccharides
D-glucose is the most abundant monosaccharide
Normal blood glucose levels are 70-110 mg/dL.
A glucose tolerance test measures blood glucose for several hours after ingesting glucose.
Excess glucose is stored as the polysaccharide glycogen or as fat.
[notes] Insulin regulates blood glucose levels by stimulating the uptake of glucose into tissues or the formation of glycogen.

Patients with diabetes produce insufficient insulin to adequately regulate blood sugar levels, so they  must monitor their diet and/or inject insulin daily.

--- slide 19 ---
Common Monosaccharides: Galactose
Galactose is one of the components of the disaccharide lactose (milk sugar).
Also called brain sugar (part of brain and nerve tissue)
Used to differentiate between blood types.
Patients with galactosemia lack an enzyme needed to metabolize galactose, which accumulates and causes cataracts and cirrhosis

--- slide 20 ---
Common Monosaccharides: Fructose
Fructose is one of the components of the disaccharide sucrose.
Also called fruit sugar.
It is a ketohexose found in honey and almost twice as sweet as table sugar with the same number of calories per gram.
High-fructose corn syrup (HFCS) is a sweetener that is produced by using an enzyme to break down sucrose to glucose and fructose.
[notes] Some “lite” food products have fewer calories because they use only half as much fructose as sucrose for the same level of sweetness .

Clyclic forms of Monosaccharides
The hemiacetal carbon is a new chiral center
Called anomeric carbon
If new hydroxy group is trans to CH2OH called alpha
If new hydroxy group is cis to CH2OH called beta 
Haworth Projections show the structure as a hexagon with vertical lines. (the molecule is not really flat like this)
[notes] D- Back CH2OH goes up
L- Back CH2OH goes down
Alpha: symbol crosses self: trans
Beta: symbol has bumps on same side: cis

--- slide 32 ---
Conclusions
Monosaccharides are the simplest carbohydrate
Classified by functional group (aldehyde or ketone) and number of carbons
Many stereoisomers possible (the more chiral carbons, the more possibilities!)
D- and L- notation is used to indicate which stereoisomer is present
Epimers are diastereomers that differ in just one chiral carbon
Monosaccharides can form cyclic structures (hemiacetals) by reacting with an alcohol group with the carbonyl group
This creates a new chiral carbon which can be alpha or beta

Redox Chemistry of Monosaccharides
The redox chemistry of monosaccharides is closely linked to that of the alcohol and aldehyde functional groups.

The carbonyl group of an aldose is reduced to a 1o alcohol using NaBH4 or H2 with Pd.

Alditols are named by changing the suffix “-ose” to “-itol”.

Reduction of Monosaccharides
Sorbitol is found in the plant world in many berries and in cherries, plums, pears, apples, seaweed, and algae.
About 60 percent as sweet as sucrose (table sugar) and is used in the manufacture of candies and as a sugar substitute for diabetics.
Used as a moisturizing agent in foods and cosmetics and as a sweetening agent in chewing gum.

--- slide 44 ---
Oxidation of Monosaccharides: Weak [O]
Oxidation can yield three different types of acidic sugars depending on the type of oxidizing agent used:
Weak oxidizing agents, such as Tollens and Benedict’s solutions oxidize the aldehyde end of an aldose to give an aldonic acid.
The hydroxy groups are not oxidized
[notes] Ald__ic acid – aldehyde oxidized to carboxylic acid
On – alcohol

--- slide 45 ---
Oxidation of Monosaccharides: Strong [O]
2) Strong oxidizing agents can oxidize both ends of a monosaccharide at the same time (the carbonyl group and the terminal primary alcohol group) to produce a dicarboxylic acid.
Such polyhydroxy dicarboxylic acids are known as aldaric acids.
[notes] Ald__ic acid: aldehyde oxidized to carboxylic acid.
Ar: alcohols oxidized

--- slide 46 ---
Oxidation of Monosaccharides: Enzymes
3) In biochemical systems, enzymes can oxidize the primary alcohol end of an aldose such as glucose, without oxidation of the aldehyde group, to produce an alduronic acid.
[notes] Ald – aldehyde there
Uron – oxidized alcohol to carboxylic acid

--- slide 47 ---
Ketoses can oxidize! (in basic solutions)
The ketose undergoes a structural rearrangement in basic solutions that produces an aldose, and the aldose then reacts. 
This is called tautomerization

--- slide 53 ---
Condensation: Disaccharide Forms
One monosaccharide acts as a hemiacetal and the other as an alcohol.
The bond that joins the two units together is called a glycosidic linkage.
Condensation: Molecules join, small molecule (H2O) lost
[notes] Condensation Reaction: Reaction that joins two molecules together and a small molecule (like water) is lost.

--- slide 55 ---
Hydrolysis: Monosaccharides Form
Hydrolysis of a disaccharide cleaves the C-O glycosidic linkage and forms two monosaccharides.
Hydrolysis: water splits molecule

--- slide 56 ---
Disaccharides: Maltose
Maltose consists of two molecules of glucose joined by a glycoside bond between carbon-1 (the anomeric carbon) of one glucose and carbon-4 of the second glucose (an a-1,4-glycoside bond). 
Anomer named based on orientation of remaining hemiacetal
Can mutarotate between α or β anomers
Maltose is a reducing sugar.
[notes] alpha-1,4’-glycoside bond

Anomer named alpha or beta (+ disaccharide name) depending on orientation or remaining hemiacetal

Any disaccharide with a hemiacetal is a reducing sugar

--- slide 57 ---
Disaccharides: Lactose
Lactose is the principal sugar in milk. It makes up about 5–8% of human milk and 4–6% of cow’s milk.
Hydrolysis of lactose yields glucose and galactose.  
In lactose, a unit of galactopyranose is joined by a b-glycoside bond to carbon-4 of glucopyranose.  (β-1,4’ glycoside bond)
Note that lactose is a reducing sugar. 
Pyranose = 6-membered heterocyclic sugar
[notes] β-1,4’ glycoside bond

Lactose Intolerance:
The enzyme lactase hydrolyzes β(14) glycosidic linkages.
Individuals who are lactose intolerant no longer produce this enzyme.
Deficiency of lactase can be caused by a genetic defect, physiological decline with age, or by injuries to intestinal mucosa.
Without the enzyme, lactose cannot be digested, causing abdominal cramps and diarrhea.

--- slide 58 ---
Disaccharides: Sucrose
Sucrose
Sucrose (table sugar) is the most abundant disaccharide in the biological world.  
It is obtained principally from the juice of sugar cane and sugar beets.  
In sucrose, carbon-1 of glucose is joined to carbon-2 of fructose by a α,β-1,2-glycoside bond. 
Note:  sucrose is a non-reducing sugar because the anomeric carbons of both glucose and fructose are involved in formation of the glycoside bond

Polysaccharides
Polysaccharides contain many monosaccharide units joined together. 
Polysaccharides exist as
Storage polysaccharides – starch and glycogen 
Structural polysaccharide – cellulose and chitin 
Acidic polysaccharide – hyaluronic acid 

--- slide 66 ---
Polysaccharides: Starch
Starch
Starch is the reserve carbohydrate for plants. 
It is found in all plant seeds and tubers and is the form in which glucose is stored for later use by plants.
Starch is composed of two principal polysaccharides: 
~20% amylose (water soluble)
~80% amylopectin (insoluble)
Hydrolysis gives only glucose

Starch: Digestion 
Both starch molecules can be digested by humans using the enzyme amylase (in saliva) and maltase (in the intestines).

--- slide 70 ---
Polysaccharides: Glycogen
Glycogen is the reserve carbohydrate for animals. 
Like amylopectin, glycogen is a nonlinear polymer of glucose joined by a-1,4- and a-1,6-glycoside bonds.
But, it has a lower molecular weight and an even more highly branched structure (every 12–18 units): 
[notes] aDD secondary structure – intermolecular forces
Biological consequences – digestions

Both branched polysaccharides end with “n”

--- slide 71 ---
Glycogen: Hydrolysis
Glycogen is stored mainly in the liver and in muscle cells.
When glucose is needed for energy, glucose units are hydrolyzed from the ends of the glycogen polymer.
Because glycogen is highly branched, there are many ends available for hydrolysis.

--- slide 72 ---
Polysaccharides: Cellulose
Cellulose is found in the cell walls of all plants, where it gives support and rigidity to wood, plant stems, and grass. 
Cotton is almost pure cellulose (95%), wood is 50% cellulose. 
Cellulose is a linear polymer of up to 3000 glucose monomers joined by b-1,4-glycosidic bonds. 
b(14)
cellulose
Humans lack enzymes to hydrolyze β-1,4 glycosidic bonds. We cannot digest cellulose
[notes] Glucose – Beta 1-4

--- slide 73 ---
Polysaccharides: Cellulose IMF
Cellulose fibers consist of bundles of parallel polysaccharide chains held together by hydrogen bonds between hydroxyl groups on adjacent chains. 
Gives cellulose fibers high mechanical strength, and insoluble in water
[notes] Humans do not posses the enzyme to hydrolyze cellulose (b-glycosidase) and cannot digest it.

--- slide 82 ---
Conclusions
Aldoses can be oxidized or reduced because of the aldehyde functional group
In basic conditions, Ketoses can undergo a structural rearrangement into an aldose, and then undergo similar reactions to aldoses
A reducing sugar is a sugar that is a reducing agent (can be oxidized) - gives positive test with Benedicts or Tollens reagent
Cyclic monosaccharides (hemiacetals) can react with an –OH group on another cyclic monosaccharide to form an acetal
There are many disaccharides and polysaccharides in nature that differ in the types of linkages between saccharide units.
Blood types A, B, AB, and O differ by the carbohydrates on the surface of red blood cells
