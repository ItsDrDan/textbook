Lecture Connection

Two levels of structure, just like proteins. Primary structure is the base sequence on a covalent sugar–phosphate backbone, always written 5′→3′. Secondary structure is the double helix: two antiparallel strands held by A=T and G≡C hydrogen bonds. The exam skill is to write a complementary strand with the ends labeled correctly and to use Chargaff's rules.

Primary structure: the backbone and its two ends

  • Nucleotides are joined by phosphodiester bonds: the phosphate on C5′ of one nucleotide forms an ester with the 3′-OH of the next sugar. The phosphate is esterified twice (one ester to each sugar), hence diester. The result is a repeating sugar–phosphate backbone with the bases sticking out to the side.
  • Every strand has two different ends: a 5′ end (a free phosphate on C5′) and a 3′ end (a free OH on C3′). By convention a sequence is written and read 5′→3′, and only the bases are written: 5′-ACG-3′. If no ends are shown, assume the left end is 5′.
  • At body pH each phosphate carries a negative charge, so DNA and RNA are polyanions. That is why they dissolve in water, why they are attracted to positive ions such as Mg2+, and why they move toward the positive electrode in gel electrophoresis.

In this course

The section text writes a DNA sequence as 5′-dG-dT-dA-dC-3′ or dGTAC, with a lowercase d for deoxy. In lecture and on exams we write just the bases with the ends labeled, 5′-GTAC-3′; the T already tells you it is DNA. Always include the 5′ and 3′ labels when you write a complementary strand.

Secondary structure: the double helix

  • The two strands are antiparallel: one runs 5′→3′ while its partner runs 3′→5′.
  • The sugar–phosphate backbones are on the outside; the bases point inward and pair by hydrogen bonds: A=T (two H-bonds) and G≡C (three H-bonds). In RNA, A pairs with U. The width of the helix decides who can pair: two purines are too big for the space, two pyrimidines too small, so a purine always pairs with a pyrimidine and every "rung" of the ladder is the same width.
  • Because of this pairing the strands are complementary: the sequence of one strand fixes the sequence of the other. Chargaff's rules follow directly: in any double-stranded DNA the amounts of A and T are always equal and the amounts of G and C are always equal, so %A + %G = %C + %T = 50%.
  • The flat base pairs also stack on one another like coins, and these stacking forces (London dispersion forces, Chapter 1) plus thousands of hydrogen bonds make the helix stable at body temperature. Heating DNA to about 90 °C separates ("melts") the strands; cooling lets them re-pair, which is what PCR relies on (Section 12.8).

Professor Tip

Always label the ends. When you write a complementary strand, flip the direction: if the given strand reads 5′→3′ left to right, the partner reads 3′→5′ left to right. Then, by convention, rewrite it 5′→3′. Chargaff's rules apply only to double-stranded DNA; in a single strand of DNA or in RNA nothing forces A to equal T (or U).

DNA versus RNA

Both are nucleic acids, but most of their differences follow from one chemical difference, the 2′-OH. Use this table to compare them; Section 12.9 goes deeper into RNA.

Worked example

Example 1: Chargaff's rules

Problem: A sample of double-stranded DNA from a bacterium is 24% adenine. Predict the percentages of T, G, and C.

Solution

Because A pairs only with T, %T = %A = 24%. Together A and T account for 48%, which leaves 100% − 48% = 52% for G plus C. Because G pairs only with C, they split that evenly: %G = %C = 26%.

Check: 24 + 24 + 26 + 26 = 100%, and the purines (A + G = 50%) equal the pyrimidines (T + C = 50%), as they must in any double helix. (These rules do not apply to a single-stranded RNA, where nothing forces A to equal U.)
