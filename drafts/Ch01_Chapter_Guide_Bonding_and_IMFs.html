Learning Objectives

  • Use valence electrons and the octet rule to draw Lewis structures of small organic molecules.
  • Recognize when C, N, or O carries a positive or negative charge from its bonds and lone pairs.
  • Predict the shape and ideal bond angle around any carbon, nitrogen, or oxygen atom.
  • Translate a molecule among its Lewis structure, expanded structural formula, condensed structural formula, and line (skeletal) structure.
  • Name the common functional groups and find them in the structure of a drug or biological molecule.
  • Decide whether a molecule is polar and which intermolecular forces (IMFs) it can form.
  • Use IMFs to predict relative boiling points, melting points, and water solubility.

Why start here?

Every organic molecule this semester is built from the same few atoms: carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, and sometimes sulfur, phosphorus, and the halogens. If you can draw a correct Lewis structure and decide where the polar bonds are, you can predict almost every physical property we talk about, from why alcohols dissolve in water to why fats are solids and oils are liquids. This chapter is the CHEM 3A material you will use every single week.

Key Ideas

1. The "bonding cheat sheet"

Atoms in period 2 want 8 valence electrons (an octet). Hydrogen wants only 2 (a duet). For neutral atoms, that gives a reliable pattern:

AtomGroupValence e−Bonds (neutral)Lone pairs (neutral)
H1110
C14440
N15531
O16622
F, Cl, Br, I17713

Professor Tip: Memorize "carbon 4, nitrogen 3, oxygen 2, hydrogen and halogens 1." If an atom breaks this pattern, it carries a charge:

  • More bonds / fewer lone pairs than usual → positive (+). Example: N with 4 bonds and 0 lone pairs (as in NH4+), or O with 3 bonds and 1 lone pair (as in H3O+).
  • Fewer bonds / more lone pairs than usual → negative (−). Example: O with 1 bond and 3 lone pairs (as in OH− or CH3O−).

2. Shape follows electron groups (VSEPR)

Count the electron groups around an atom. A single, double, or triple bond each count as one group, and so does each lone pair. In organic molecules an atom has 2, 3, or 4 electron groups, so you only need three shapes (plus the two "lone-pair" versions of tetrahedral). Use this table as a lookup.

3. Polar bonds and polar molecules

Electronegativity increases toward fluorine (up and to the right on the periodic table). A bond between C or H and N, O, F, or Cl is polar. C–H and C–C bonds are treated as nonpolar.

Deciding polarity is a two-step question. Bond polarity asks about one bond: the larger the electronegativity difference between the two atoms, the more polar the bond, with the partial negative charge (δ−) on the more electronegative atom. Molecular polarity asks about the whole molecule: add up the bond dipoles using the shape from VSEPR. If they point in different directions and cancel, the molecule is nonpolar even though its bonds are polar.

Professor Tip: If a molecule contains N, O, or F (or another halogen) and the polar bonds do not cancel by symmetry, the molecule is polar. Hydrocarbons are nonpolar. A lone pair on the central atom (as in water or an amine) almost always breaks the symmetry, so those molecules are polar.

4. Intermolecular forces (IMFs): the forces between molecules

Covalent bonds hold atoms together inside a molecule. IMFs hold separate molecules next to each other. When a substance melts or boils, only IMFs are broken. The covalent bonds stay intact.

IMFWho has it?Relative strength
London dispersionEvery molecule. Grows with size and surface area.Weakest (per contact)
Dipole–dipolePolar moleculesMedium
Hydrogen bondingMolecules with an H directly bonded to N, O, or F (an N–H, O–H, or F–H bond)Strongest
  • Boiling and melting point: compare the IMFs between molecules of the same compound. Stronger IMFs mean a higher boiling point.
  • Solubility ("like dissolves like"): compare the IMFs between solute and solvent. Molecules that can hydrogen bond with water tend to dissolve in water. Large nonpolar sections work against solubility.
  • Size matters: In molecules of similar polarity, more carbons means more dispersion forces and a higher boiling point. Branching lowers the boiling point because branched molecules cannot pack as closely.

5. Four ways to draw the same molecule

Organic chemists draw one molecule four different ways, depending on how much detail they need. The table shows propan-1-ol, the three-carbon alcohol, in each representation; this book, the games, and your exams switch between them without warning (structures drawn with the Bond Voyage! engine).

Three rules let you read any line structure:

  1. Every corner and every end of a line is a carbon atom.
  2. Each carbon has as many hidden hydrogens as it needs to reach four bonds. A carbon with two lines touching it has two hidden H's; a carbon at the end of a line has three.
  3. Nitrogen, oxygen, sulfur, and the halogens are always written, and so is any hydrogen bonded to them (the OH above). Those are the hydrogens that matter for hydrogen bonding, so they stay visible.

Professor Tip: To get a molecular formula from a line structure, count carbons first, then add hydrogens carbon by carbon until each has four bonds. Writing the condensed formula as an in-between step catches most mistakes.

6. Functional groups: the parts list of organic chemistry

A functional group is a specific atom or small cluster of atoms that gives a molecule its chemical personality. Molecules with the same functional group react the same way and have similar physical properties, however large the rest of the molecule is, which is why this course is organized by functional group (alcohols in Chapter 4, aldehydes and ketones in Chapter 5, and so on). In the table, R means "the rest of the molecule," any carbon chain or ring. The table lists each group, how to recognize it, one small example, and where you will meet it in health care.

Professor Tip

To find functional groups in a big molecule, hunt for carbonyl groups (C=O) first and ask what is attached to the carbonyl carbon: an H means aldehyde, two carbons mean ketone, an OH means carboxylic acid, an OR means ester, and an N means amide. Then look at any oxygen, nitrogen, or sulfur that is not next to a C=O: O–H is an alcohol (or phenol on a ring), C–O–C is an ether, N is an amine, S–H is a thiol. Finish with the carbon skeleton: C=C, C≡C, and benzene rings. Circle each group as you find it so none gets counted twice.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Drawing a Lewis structure

Draw the Lewis structure of methylamine, CH3NH2.

Solution

  1. Count valence electrons: C (4) + N (5) + 5 H (5 × 1) = 14 e−.
  2. Connect the atoms as the formula reads: C is bonded to N. Three H go on C and two H go on N.
  3. That is 6 single bonds = 12 e−. The remaining 2 e− go on N as one lone pair.
  4. Check: C has 4 bonds, 0 lone pairs. N has 3 bonds, 1 lone pair. Each H has 1 bond. Everyone is neutral, and the electron count is 14. ✓

Example 2: Spotting charges

In each species, which atom carries a charge, and is it + or −? (a) CH3O− (methoxide)    (b) CH3NH3+ (methylammonium)

Solution

(a) The oxygen has only 1 bond and 3 lone pairs (one fewer bond than usual), so O is negative.

(b) The nitrogen has 4 bonds and 0 lone pairs (one more bond than usual), so N is positive. You will see this exact ion again when amines act as bases (Chapter 7).

Example 3: Shape around each atom

Give the shape and ideal bond angle around each carbon and the oxygen in ethanal (acetaldehyde), CH3CHO.

Solution

  • CH3 carbon: four single bonds, 4 groups → tetrahedral, 109.5°.
  • CHO carbon: one C=O double bond, one C–H, and one C–C, 3 groups → trigonal planar, 120°.
  • The oxygen is attached to only one atom, so it does not have a "shape" of its own. The C=O bond simply lies in the trigonal plane.

Example 4: Polar or nonpolar?

Is chloroform (CHCl3) polar? Is carbon tetrachloride (CCl4) polar?

Solution

Both are tetrahedral around carbon, and both have polar C–Cl bonds. In CCl4 all four bonds are identical and point symmetrically, so the bond dipoles cancel: nonpolar. In CHCl3 one position is a C–H, so the dipoles do not cancel: polar. Polar bonds do not always mean a polar molecule. Always check the symmetry.

Example 5: Ranking boiling points with IMFs

Propane (CH3CH2CH3), dimethyl ether (CH3OCH3), and ethanol (CH3CH2OH) all have nearly the same molar mass (44–46 g/mol). Rank them from lowest to highest boiling point.

Solution

  • Propane: nonpolar, dispersion only.
  • Dimethyl ether: polar C–O bonds and a bent shape at O, so it has dipole–dipole forces. It has no O–H, so it cannot hydrogen bond with itself.
  • Ethanol: has an O–H bond, so it forms hydrogen bonds.

Lowest → highest: propane (−42 °C) < dimethyl ether (−24 °C) < ethanol (78 °C). Same size, very different boiling points. The IMFs made the difference.

Example 6: Predicting water solubility

Which is more soluble in water: ethylamine (CH3CH2NH2) or propane (CH3CH2CH3)?

Solution

Ethylamine has N–H bonds and a lone pair on N, so it can hydrogen bond with water molecules. Propane is nonpolar and can only use dispersion forces, which are much weaker than the hydrogen bonds water molecules form with each other. Ethylamine is far more soluble.

Example 7: Translating among representations

Problem

The line structure below is 2-methylbutane, a component of gasoline. Write its condensed structural formula, draw its expanded structural formula, and give its molecular formula.

Solution

  1. Count carbons: the zigzag has four corners and ends, plus the branch, so there are 5 carbons. The longest chain is 4 carbons long, with a one-carbon branch on the second carbon.
  2. Add hidden hydrogens so every carbon has four bonds. Reading along the main chain: the end carbon needs 3 H (CH3), the branched carbon already has three bonds to carbon so it needs 1 H (CH), the next carbon needs 2 H (CH2), and the last carbon needs 3 H (CH3). The branch carbon is a CH3.
  3. Condensed formula, with the branch in parentheses after the carbon that holds it: CH3CH(CH3)CH2CH3.
  4. Expanded formula: draw every bond as a line.

Molecular formula: 5 C and 3 + 1 + 2 + 3 + 3 = 12 H, so C5H12.

Check

Every carbon in the expanded formula has exactly four lines touching it, and an alkane with 5 carbons should have 2(5) + 2 = 12 hydrogens. ✓

Example 8: Bond polarity versus molecular polarity

Problem

(a) Which bond is more polar, C–O or C–N? (b) Is methanal (formaldehyde, H2C=O) a polar molecule? (c) Is carbon dioxide (O=C=O) a polar molecule?

Solution

(a) Electronegativity increases from C to N to O, so the C–O bond has the larger electronegativity difference and is more polar. In both bonds the partial negative charge (δ−) sits on the N or O, and the partial positive charge (δ+) sits on carbon.

(b) Methanal: the carbon has three electron groups (two C–H bonds and one C=O double bond), so it is trigonal planar. The C–H bonds are essentially nonpolar, and the one polar C=O bond has nothing to cancel it. Polar.

(c) Carbon dioxide: the carbon has two electron groups (two double bonds), so it is linear. Both C=O bonds are polar, but they point in exactly opposite directions and cancel. Nonpolar, even though its bonds are polar.

Check

Ask the two questions in order: polar bonds? Then, does the shape let them cancel? Only CO2 has identical bonds on every side of carbon, so only CO2 is nonpolar. ✓

Example 9: Ranking three molecules of the same size by boiling point

Problem

Butane (CH3CH2CH2CH3), propanal (CH3CH2CHO), and propan-1-ol (CH3CH2CH2OH) have molar masses of 58–60 g/mol. Rank them from lowest to highest boiling point and explain your ranking.

Solution

The three molecules are nearly the same size, so their dispersion forces are similar and the ranking comes down to which other IMFs each can form. The comparison table summarizes them.

MoleculeFunctional groupPolar?H bonded to N or O?Strongest IMFBoiling point
ButaneAlkaneNoNoDispersion−1 °C
PropanalAldehydeYes (C=O)No (the H is on carbon)Dipole–dipole48 °C
Propan-1-olAlcoholYes (C–O and O–H)Yes (O–H)Hydrogen bonding97 °C

Lowest to highest: butane < propanal < propan-1-ol. Butane is nonpolar and relies on dispersion alone. Propanal's C=O makes it polar, adding dipole–dipole attraction. Propan-1-ol's O–H lets its molecules hydrogen bond, the strongest of the three forces.

Check

The boiling points climb by roughly 50 °C at each step, matching dispersion < dipole–dipole < hydrogen bonding at constant size. The aldehyde's H is on carbon, so it cannot donate a hydrogen bond. ✓

Example 10: Finding the functional groups in a drug

Problem

Acetaminophen (the active ingredient in Tylenol) is shown below as a line structure. Identify every functional group, then predict whether it can hydrogen bond with water.

Solution

  1. Carbonyl first: there is one C=O, and its carbon is bonded to a CH3 and to a nitrogen. A C=O with N attached is an amide.
  2. Other O, N, S: the only remaining oxygen is an O–H bonded directly to the ring. An O–H on a benzene ring is a phenol. The nitrogen is already part of the amide, so it is not counted again as an amine.
  3. Skeleton: the six-membered ring with alternating double bonds is an aromatic ring.

Functional groups: amide, phenol, aromatic ring. The molecule has an O–H and an N–H, so it can both donate and accept hydrogen bonds with water. Yes, it hydrogen bonds with water.

Check

Count the hidden hydrogens: four ring C–H, three on the CH3, plus the O–H and N–H, giving C8H9NO2, the known formula of acetaminophen. Every O and N belongs to exactly one functional group. ✓

Health Career Connection

  • Nursing – why some drugs are injected and others are swallowed: A drug has to dissolve in watery blood plasma and slip through fatty cell membranes. Chemists tune this balance by adding or removing polar groups (–OH, –NH2, –COOH) that hydrogen bond with water. The IMF reasoning in this chapter is the same reasoning behind instructions like "take with food" or "dissolve in water."
  • Dental hygiene – local anesthetics: Lidocaine and articaine contain an amine nitrogen with a lone pair. Dental cartridges contain the protonated form (N+ with 4 bonds) because the charged ion dissolves well in water. Once injected, some of it loses that H+ and becomes neutral again, which lets it cross nerve membranes. You will see this exact charge pattern again in Chapter 7.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Before a venipuncture (drawing blood from a vein), you wipe the skin with a 70% isopropyl alcohol swab and feel the spot go cold within seconds. That chill is intermolecular forces at work. Isopropyl alcohol, CH3CH(OH)CH3, has one O–H group that hydrogen bonds, but it also has a three-carbon hydrocarbon frame that holds its neighbors only by weak dispersion forces. Water, by contrast, is a dense network of hydrogen bonds. The alcohol therefore escapes into the air far more easily (boiling point 82 °C versus 100 °C), and every molecule that evaporates carries away heat from the skin. The same mixed polar-and-nonpolar character explains why the alcohol works as an antiseptic (a substance that kills microbes on living tissue): it mixes with water in any proportion yet can also push into the fatty bacterial membrane. Pure alcohol evaporates too fast to do this well, which is one reason swabs are 70% rather than 100%.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Tooth enamel is not made of molecules at all. It is hydroxyapatite, Ca5(PO4)3OH, an ionic solid in which calcium, phosphate, and hydroxide ions sit in a rigid lattice held together by ionic bonds. Acid from plaque bacteria pulls those ions out of the lattice, a process called demineralization. Fluoride ion from toothpaste or varnish replaces the hydroxide to form fluorapatite, Ca5(PO4)3F, which resists acid better and favors remineralization. That is ionic chemistry from CHEM 3A. Almost everything else you handle in the clinic is covalent and molecular, and IMFs decide how it behaves. Eugenol, the clove-oil phenol used in temporary cements, is mostly benzene ring and hydrocarbon with a single O–H, so it is only slightly soluble in water and does not wash away in saliva. Salivary mucins, by contrast, are coated with hundreds of O–H groups that hydrogen bond with water, which is what makes saliva slippery and protective; when saliva flow drops (xerostomia, dry mouth), that protection is lost.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Giving hydrogen two bonds. Hydrogen always makes exactly one bond.
  • Forgetting lone pairs on N and O. They count as electron groups and change the shape.
  • Saying a molecule hydrogen bonds because it "has H and O." The H must be directly attached to N, O, or F. In CH3OCH3, every H is on carbon.
  • Thinking boiling breaks covalent bonds. Boiling only separates molecules (breaks IMFs).
  • Forgetting the hidden hydrogens in a line structure. Every corner is a carbon, and every carbon needs four bonds; count the hydrogens in before you write a molecular formula.
  • Counting a double bond as two electron groups. A C=O or C=C is one group, so a carbonyl carbon has three groups and is trigonal planar, not tetrahedral.
  • Calling an ether an alcohol because it "has an oxygen." An alcohol needs an O–H; in an ether the oxygen sits between two carbons and there is no hydrogen on it.
  • Stopping at "it has polar bonds" when asked whether a molecule is polar. Check the shape: CO2 and CCl4 have polar bonds but are nonpolar molecules.

Practice

Exercise 1

How many valence electrons are in ethanol, C2H5OH? How many lone pairs are on the oxygen?

Answer
2 C (8) + 6 H (6) + O (6) = 20 valence electrons. The oxygen has 2 bonds and 2 lone pairs.

Exercise 2

A carbon atom is drawn with 3 bonds and 1 lone pair. What is its charge?

Answer
Negative (−). Carbon normally has 4 bonds and 0 lone pairs. Fewer bonds and an extra lone pair means it is electron rich.

Exercise 3

Give the shape and ideal bond angle around each carbon in acrylonitrile, CH2=CH–C≡N.

Answer
CH2 carbon: trigonal planar, 120°. CH carbon: trigonal planar, 120°. The C≡N carbon: linear, 180°.

Exercise 4

Which molecules can hydrogen bond with each other (with molecules of the same compound)? CH3F, CH3NH2, CH3OCH3, CH3OH

Answer
Only CH3NH2 and CH3OH, because only they have an H bonded directly to N or O. In CH3F and CH3OCH3 every H is on carbon (although both can still accept a hydrogen bond from water).

Exercise 5

Which has the higher boiling point: hexane, CH3(CH2)4CH3, or its branched isomer 2,3-dimethylbutane, (CH3)2CHCH(CH3)2? Explain.

Answer
Hexane (bp 69 °C vs. 58 °C). Both are nonpolar with only dispersion forces, but the straight chain has more surface contact with its neighbors, so its dispersion forces are stronger.
