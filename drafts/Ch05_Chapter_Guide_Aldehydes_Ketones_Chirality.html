Learning Objectives

  • Identify and name aldehydes and ketones.
  • Compare their boiling points and solubility with alkanes and alcohols.
  • Predict results of Tollens' and Benedict's tests, and products of oxidation and reduction.
  • Recognize hemiacetals and acetals and draw them from an aldehyde or ketone plus an alcohol.
  • Find chiral carbons, tell enantiomers from diastereomers, and assign D or L from a Fischer projection.

Key Ideas

1. The carbonyl group, C=O

  • Aldehyde: the C=O carbon has at least one H (the C=O is at the end of a chain). Written –CHO. Ending: -al. The CHO carbon is always C1, so no number is needed: butanal.
  • Ketone: the C=O carbon is bonded to two carbons (the C=O is inside the chain). Ending: -one, with a number: 2-pentanone. Common names: acetone (propanone), MEK (butanone).
  • Common aldehyde names: formaldehyde (methanal), acetaldehyde (ethanal), benzaldehyde.

2. Physical properties

The C=O is polar, so aldehydes and ketones have dipole–dipole forces, but no O–H, so they cannot hydrogen bond with each other. Their boiling points fall between alkanes and alcohols of similar size. The carbonyl O accepts hydrogen bonds from water, so those with about 1–4 carbons are water soluble. The same ranking logic you used in Chapters 2 and 4 applies here; the table compares three compounds of nearly the same molar mass.

Two reminders. First, as the carbon chain grows, the nonpolar part wins: hexanal and 2-hexanone are only slightly soluble in water. Second, an isomeric aldehyde and ketone (propanal and propanone, for example) have very similar boiling points, because both rely on the same C=O dipole. For boiling-point rankings on an exam, treat aldehydes and ketones as one category that sits between hydrocarbons and alcohols.

3. Reactions

  • Oxidation: aldehyde → carboxylic acid. Ketones do not oxidize easily.
  • Tollens' test (Ag+): aldehydes give a silver mirror. Ketones give no reaction.
  • Benedict's test (Cu2+, blue): most simple aldehydes, and the sugars of Chapter 8, give a brick-red Cu2O precipitate. Ketones give no reaction (except α-hydroxy ketones such as fructose; see the table).
  • Reduction (add H2, the reverse of oxidation): aldehyde → 1° alcohol, ketone → 2° alcohol.
  • Adding an alcohol: aldehyde or ketone + 1 alcohol → hemiacetal (one OH and one OR on the same carbon). Hemiacetal + a second alcohol → acetal (two OR on the same carbon) + H2O. (When the starting compound is a ketone these are sometimes called hemiketals and ketals.)

The two oxidizing reagents you will meet in lab both test for the aldehyde group, but they look different and do not respond to exactly the same compounds. The table summarizes what to expect.

Reduction runs the other way. Hydrogen gas with a metal catalyst (Ni, Pd, or Pt) or a hydride reagent such as sodium borohydride, NaBH4, adds two H atoms across the C=O: one to the carbon, one to the oxygen. The carbonyl carbon keeps whatever it already had, so the number of carbons attached to it tells you the class of the alcohol.

  • Aldehyde (C=O carbon holds one C and one H) → 1° alcohol.
  • Ketone (C=O carbon holds two C) → 2° alcohol.
  • You can never get a 3° alcohol by reducing a carbonyl, and the reverse is also true: a 3° alcohol has no H on the carbinol carbon, so it cannot be oxidized to a carbonyl (Chapter 4).

Hemiacetal, then acetal: two separate steps. Both steps are reversible and both are catalyzed by a trace of acid (H+). Learn them as a pair.

  1. Step 1 – addition. The O–H of an alcohol adds across the C=O. The H goes to the carbonyl oxygen (making an OH) and the OR group goes to the carbonyl carbon. Product: a hemiacetal, one carbon holding OH and OR. Nothing is lost; it is a simple addition reaction.
  2. Step 2 – condensation (substitution of OH by OR). A second alcohol replaces the hemiacetal OH, and a water molecule leaves. Product: an acetal, one carbon holding two OR groups, plus H2O.

Because every arrow is reversible, the reaction direction depends on conditions (Le Châtelier’s principle, Chapter 4). Excess alcohol and removal of water push toward the acetal; excess water and acid push back toward the aldehyde (see Key Idea 6).

Professor Tip

Look for the "goggle eyes": one carbon with two oxygens attached by single bonds. If one O has an H, it is a hemiacetal. If both oxygens are bonded to carbons, it is an acetal. That carbon used to be the C=O carbon. Learn to spot it now, because every sugar ring in Chapter 8 is a hemiacetal.

4. Chirality

  • A chiral carbon (stereocenter) has four different groups attached. Mark it with a star (*).
  • A molecule with one chiral carbon has a non-superimposable mirror image, its enantiomer (like left and right hands).
  • With two or more chiral carbons, stereoisomers that are not mirror images are diastereomers.
  • Fischer projection: horizontal lines come toward you, vertical lines go back, and the most oxidized carbon (CHO) goes at the top. Look at the chiral carbon farthest from the C=O: OH on the right = D, OH on the left = L.

Handedness. Your two hands are mirror images, yet you cannot lay one on top of the other and have every finger match. Any object with that property is chiral (from the Greek for hand); an object that can be superimposed on its mirror image, like a plain coffee mug, is achiral. Molecules work the same way. The most common reason a molecule is chiral is a tetrahedral carbon with four different groups. Swap any two of those groups and you get the mirror-image molecule, not the same one.

How to find and count chiral carbons. Go carbon by carbon and ask one question: does this carbon carry four different things? You can skip carbons quickly when:

  • it has two or more H atoms (CH2 and CH3 groups are never chiral);
  • it is part of a double or triple bond (C=O, C=C, and ring carbons of benzene have only three neighbors);
  • it carries two identical groups, such as two CH3 groups or, in a ring, two identical paths around the ring.

That leaves only CH carbons with four single bonds to check. Count them; call the total n.

How many stereoisomers? Each chiral carbon can be arranged two ways, so a molecule with n chiral carbons has at most 2n stereoisomers. One chiral carbon gives one pair of enantiomers (2). Two chiral carbons give up to 4 stereoisomers: two pairs of enantiomers, and any two that are not mirror images are diastereomers. Enantiomers have identical melting points, boiling points, and solubilities and differ only in how they interact with other chiral things (plane-polarized light, enzymes, receptors). Diastereomers are different compounds with different physical properties. Glucose, with four chiral carbons, is one of 24 = 16 aldohexoses (Chapter 8).

Reading a Fischer projection. A Fischer projection is a flattened drawing of a tetrahedral carbon. The chiral carbon sits at the crossing point; the two horizontal bonds point toward you and the two vertical bonds point away. The carbon chain runs vertically with the most oxidized carbon (CHO for a sugar) on top. The D/L label comes from the single chiral carbon of glyceraldehyde: OH to the right is D, OH to the left is L. In a longer sugar, use the chiral carbon farthest from the CHO (the bottom-most one). The letters say nothing about which way the compound rotates light; they are simply a family label traced back to glyceraldehyde.

Why it matters. Nearly all of biochemistry is one-handed. The amino acids in your proteins are L, the sugars you metabolize are D, and enzymes and receptors are themselves chiral, so they fit one enantiomer of a drug and not the other. Ibuprofen is sold as a 50:50 mixture of its two enantiomers (a racemic mixture), but mainly one enantiomer relieves pain; the body even converts some of the inactive one into the active one. Thalidomide, prescribed in the late 1950s as a sedative, caused birth defects partly because one enantiomer is harmful and the two interconvert in the body, a tragedy that changed how drugs are tested.

5. Naming molecules with more than one functional group

Many biological molecules carry two or more functional groups: lactic acid is an alcohol and a carboxylic acid; the ketone bodies of Chapter 15 are ketones with an acid or a second alcohol. IUPAC settles this with one rule: the highest-priority group gets the suffix and decides the numbering (it gets the lowest possible number). 6. Hydrolysis of acetals

Acetal formation is reversible. With water and an acid catalyst, an acetal splits back into the aldehyde or ketone and two alcohol molecules. Hemiacetals revert even more easily. This matters in Chapter 8: the glycosidic bonds that link sugars are acetals, and digestion hydrolyzes them.

To predict the products, find the acetal carbon (the one with two single-bonded oxygens), then run the formation steps backward:

  • Each –OR group leaves as a separate alcohol, R–OH. Two OCH3 groups give two methanol molecules; two OCH2CH3 groups give two ethanol molecules.
  • The acetal carbon becomes the C=O carbon again. If it still holds an H, the product is an aldehyde; if it holds two carbons, the product is a ketone.
  • Count: acetal + H2O → carbonyl compound + 2 alcohol. A hemiacetal needs no water; it simply splits into the carbonyl compound + 1 alcohol.

Example: CH3CH2CH(OCH3)2 + H2O (H+) → propanal + 2 CH3OH. Acetals are stable in base and in neutral water, so they survive the small intestine's near-neutral pH until an enzyme does the hydrolysis instead of acid.

7. Cyclic hemiacetals: a preview of sugars

A single molecule that contains both a C=O and an OH can react with itself. If the OH sits on C4 or C5 (counting the carbonyl carbon as C1), the chain can curl around so the OH adds to the C=O, giving a five- or six-membered ring that contains the oxygen. The product is a cyclic hemiacetal: the old carbonyl carbon sits in the ring holding one OH (outside the ring) and one O that is now part of the ring. Take 5-hydroxypentanal as the simplest case.

Rings of five or six atoms form easily and, because hemiacetal formation is reversible, the open-chain and ring forms exist together in solution, with the ring strongly favored. Glucose is exactly this: a six-carbon aldehyde with an OH on C5 that closes to a six-membered cyclic hemiacetal. When a cyclic hemiacetal then reacts with a second alcohol (another sugar's OH, for instance) it becomes a cyclic acetal, which is what the glycosidic bond in sucrose, lactose, and starch is. Hydrolysis of those acetal links is digestion. You will draw all of this in Chapter 8; for now, make sure you can spot the hemiacetal carbon in a ring.

Health Career Connection

  • Nursing – acetone breath: In uncontrolled diabetes (diabetic ketoacidosis), the body makes ketone bodies, including the ketone acetone, which gives the breath a fruity smell. More in Chapter 15.
  • Nursing and lab – formalin: A water solution of formaldehyde (formalin) preserves biopsy and tissue samples by cross-linking proteins.
  • Dental hygiene – curing lights: The photoinitiator in most light-cured composites is camphorquinone, a compound with two ketone groups. It absorbs blue light and starts the polymerization you saw in Chapter 3.
  • Pharmacology – chiral drugs: Enantiomers can act very differently in the body. Only the L enantiomer of DOPA (levodopa) treats Parkinson's disease, and for ibuprofen one enantiomer does most of the pain relief.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Ketones you can smell. When cells cannot use glucose, as in untreated type 1 diabetes, the liver turns fatty acids into ketone bodies, two of which are acids. The result is diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), a dangerous drop in blood pH. The third ketone body is the ketone acetone, which is volatile and leaves through the lungs, giving the fruity "acetone breath" you may notice on a patient. Urine ketone strips detect the ketone bodies; details come in Chapter 15.

An aldehyde and the hangover. Alcohol dehydrogenase oxidizes ethanol (a 1° alcohol) to ethanal (acetaldehyde), the same alcohol → aldehyde oxidation you learned in Chapter 4. Aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH2) then oxidizes the aldehyde to acetate. Acetaldehyde is toxic and reactive; people with a common, less active ALDH2 variant accumulate it and experience facial flushing, a rapid heartbeat, and nausea after a small drink.

Testing for sugar. Copper-reduction tablets (Clinitest, a Benedict's-type reagent) detect any reducing sugar in urine, including galactose and lactose; glucose meters and dipsticks use the enzyme glucose oxidase and respond to glucose only (Chapter 8).

One-handed drugs. Levothyroxine (the thyroid hormone replacement) is the single L enantiomer; esomeprazole is one enantiomer of omeprazole sold on its own; and one enantiomer of ibuprofen does most of the work. Same atoms, same bonds, different hands, different effects.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Aldehydes as sterilants. Glutaraldehyde, a five-carbon dialdehyde (OHC–CH2CH2CH2–CHO), is a cold-sterilant for heat-sensitive instruments. Its two aldehyde groups react with amino groups on microbial proteins, cross-linking them so the proteins stop working; formaldehyde (formalin) fixes tissue specimens the same way. That reactivity is also why these solutions irritate eyes, skin, and airways and have a sharp odor, and why instruments must be rinsed thoroughly after soaking.

Flavor and cooling. Two aldehydes flavor toothpaste and mouth rinse: vanillin (vanilla) and benzaldehyde (almond, cherry). Menthol, the cooling agent in mouthwash, has three chiral carbons, and only one of its eight stereoisomers, (–)-menthol, gives the strong minty cooling; its enantiomer, (+)-menthol, smells musty and barely cools. Your cold receptors, like all proteins, are chiral.

Acetone. The same ketone that appears in DKA breath is a lab and clinic solvent, used for removing nail polish and in some denture and appliance cleaning; it dissolves both polar and nonpolar residues because the C=O is polar while the two methyl groups are not.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Naming

Problem. Name (a) CH3CH2CH(CH3)CHO and (b) CH3CH2COCH2CH2CH3.

Solution.

(a) The CHO carbon is C1 and the methyl is on C2: 2-methylbutanal.

(b) Six carbons with the C=O at C3 (numbered from the closer end): 3-hexanone.

Check. An aldehyde never needs a locant; a ketone always does unless only one position is possible (propanone, butanone).

Example 2: Tollens' test and reduction

Problem. Propanal and propanone (acetone) are isomers. (a) Which gives a positive Tollens' test? (b) What does each give when reduced?

Solution.

(a) Propanal (an aldehyde) is oxidized to propanoic acid and deposits silver. Propanone: no reaction.

(b) Propanal → 1-propanol (1°). Propanone → 2-propanol (2°, rubbing alcohol).

Check. Reduction is the reverse of the Chapter 4 oxidations: 1-propanol oxidizes back to propanal, 2-propanol back to propanone.

Example 3: Hemiacetal and acetal

Problem. Draw the products when ethanal reacts with one, then a second, molecule of methanol.

Solution.

Hemiacetal: CH3CH(OH)OCH3. The old C=O carbon now holds an OH and an OCH3.

Acetal: CH3CH(OCH3)2 + H2O. The OH was replaced by a second OCH3.

Check. Overall: C2H4O + 2 CH4O → C4H10O2 + H2O; atoms balance.

Example 4: Finding chiral carbons

Problem. Which of these has a chiral carbon: 2-propanol, 2-butanol, 3-pentanol?

Solution. Check the carbon holding OH in each:

  • 2-propanol: H, OH, CH3, CH3. Two groups are the same, so it is not chiral.
  • 2-butanol: H, OH, CH3, CH2CH3. All four are different, so it is chiral.
  • 3-pentanol: H, OH, CH2CH3, CH2CH3. Not chiral.

Check. Swap the two identical groups in 2-propanol or 3-pentanol and you get the same molecule back, so no mirror-image twin exists.

Example 5: D or L?

Problem. In a Fischer projection of glyceraldehyde (CHO at the top, CH2OH at the bottom), the OH on the middle carbon points to the left. Is it D or L?

Solution.

The middle carbon is the only chiral carbon, so it is also the one farthest from the CHO. OH on the left = L-glyceraldehyde. Its enantiomer, with the OH on the right, is D-glyceraldehyde.

Check. Make sure the projection is drawn the standard way (CHO on top) before reading left or right; flipping the drawing upside down would reverse the answer.

Example 6: Naming a molecule with two functional groups

Problem. Name the two compounds shown.

Solution.

(a) The molecule has a ketone C=O and an OH. Ketone outranks alcohol, so the suffix is -one and the OH becomes hydroxy-. Five carbons: the parent is pentanone. Number from the end that gives the C=O the lowest number: C=O on C2, OH on C4. Name: 4-hydroxy-2-pentanone.

(b) The molecule has an aldehyde (CHO at one end) and a ketone. Aldehyde outranks ketone, so the suffix is -al, the CHO carbon is C1, and the ketone becomes oxo-. Four carbons: butanal, with the ketone O on C3. Name: 3-oxobutanal.

Check. Rebuild each structure from the name. 4-Hydroxy-2-pentanone: CH3COCH2CH(OH)CH3, five carbons, matches. 3-Oxobutanal: OHC–CH2–CO–CH3, four carbons, matches. If you had numbered (a) from the other end you would get 2-hydroxy-4-pentanone, and the suffix group would not have the lowest number, so that name is wrong.

Example 7: Ranking by boiling point and solubility

Problem. Pentane, butanal, 1-butanol, and 2-butanone all have molar masses of 72–74 g/mol. (a) Rank them from lowest to highest boiling point. (b) Which are appreciably soluble in water?

Solution.

(a) Identify the strongest force each can use with its own kind. Pentane: dispersion only. Butanal and 2-butanone: dipole–dipole from the C=O, no O–H. 1-Butanol: hydrogen bonding. Ranking: pentane < butanal ≈ 2-butanone < 1-butanol (actual values 36, 75, 80, and 118 °C).

(b) All three oxygen compounds can accept hydrogen bonds from water, and each has only four carbons, so butanal, 2-butanone, and 1-butanol are all soluble (2-butanone and 1-butanol: roughly 7–27 g per 100 mL). Pentane is insoluble.

Check. The aldehyde and ketone land between the hydrocarbon and the alcohol, as Key Idea 2 predicts, and the small difference between the two carbonyl isomers is not something you need to predict.

Example 8: Counting chiral carbons in a drug

Problem. How many chiral carbons does ibuprofen have (see the figure in Key Idea 4)? How many does citric acid have? How many stereoisomers are possible for each?

Solution.

Ibuprofen. Skip every CH2 and CH3 and every ring carbon (each ring carbon has only three neighbors). Two CH carbons remain. The CH of the isobutyl group holds H, CH2–ring, CH3, and CH3: two methyls, not chiral. The CH next to the COOH holds H, CH3, COOH, and the aromatic ring: four different groups, chiral. Total 1; 21 = 2 stereoisomers (one pair of enantiomers).

Citric acid, HOOC–CH2–C(OH)(COOH)–CH2–COOH. The only candidate is the central carbon: OH, COOH, CH2COOH, and CH2COOH. The two arms are identical, so it is not chiral. Total 0; citric acid has no stereoisomers.

Check. A quick test for any candidate carbon: imagine swapping two of its groups. If the result is the same molecule (as it is when two groups are identical), the carbon is not chiral.

Example 9: Hydrolysis of an acetal

Problem. CH3C(OCH2CH3)2CH2CH3 is heated with water and a drop of acid. Give the products and name them.

Solution.

Find the carbon with two single-bonded oxygens: it is C2, bonded to CH3, CH2CH3, and two OCH2CH3 groups, so this is an acetal from a ketone. Each OCH2CH3 leaves as ethanol (two molecules). C2 becomes C=O again, holding CH3 and CH2CH3: a ketone. Products: butanone (2-butanone) + 2 ethanol.

Check. Run it forward: butanone + 2 ethanol (H+) → the acetal + H2O. Atoms balance, and the water consumed in hydrolysis is the water released in formation.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Giving the aldehyde carbon a number (1-butanal). It is always C1, so it is just butanal.
  • Saying ketones give a positive Tollens' test. They do not oxidize.
  • Calling a carbon with two identical groups (two CH3 groups or two H's) chiral.
  • Deciding D/L from the top chiral carbon. Use the one farthest from the C=O.
  • Numbering a hydroxy ketone from the end nearest the OH. The suffix group (the C=O) gets the lowest number: 4-hydroxy-2-pentanone, not 2-hydroxy-4-pentanone.
  • Writing an acetal with only one alcohol consumed. A hemiacetal uses one alcohol; an acetal uses two and releases one water. Hydrolysis of an acetal therefore gives back two alcohol molecules.
  • Counting a ring carbon of benzene or a C=O carbon as chiral. A carbon in a double bond has only three neighbors and can never be a chiral center.
  • Assuming enantiomers differ in melting point or solubility. They do not; only diastereomers have different physical properties.
