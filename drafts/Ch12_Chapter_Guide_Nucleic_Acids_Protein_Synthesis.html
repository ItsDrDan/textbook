Learning Objectives

  • Identify the parts of a nucleotide, tell purines from pyrimidines, and name nucleosides and nucleotides (adenosine, AMP, ADP, ATP).
  • Describe the primary structure of a nucleic acid (the 3′→5′ phosphodiester backbone) and the secondary structure of DNA (antiparallel double helix with A=T and G≡C pairs).
  • Compare the structures, locations, and jobs of DNA and RNA, and of the three main kinds of RNA.
  • Write complementary DNA and RNA strands with correct 5′/3′ ends and use Chargaff's rules.
  • Describe replication, transcription, and translation, and the main enzymes involved.
  • Use the genetic code to translate mRNA, identify a change in DNA as a point mutation, insertion or deletion and predict its effect, and give an overview of recombinant DNA and of how viruses infect a cell.

Every protein you met in Chapter 10, including every enzyme in Chapter 11, is built from instructions stored in DNA. This chapter is about how that information is stored as a sequence of bases, copied when a cell divides, and read out to make a protein. The chemistry is familiar: esters of phosphoric acid (Chapter 6), heterocyclic amines (Chapter 7), cyclic sugars (Chapter 8), and hydrogen bonds (Chapter 1). What is new is that the order of the pieces carries a message.

Key Ideas

This guide is the short version. Each Key Idea is the lecture in a few lines; the full explanation, figures, worked examples and clinical boxes now sit on the section pages they belong to ("Lecture Connection" at the end of each section).

1. Nucleotides

Nucleotide = nitrogen base + five-carbon sugar + phosphate. Purines (two rings) are A and G, "Pure As Gold"; pyrimidines (one ring) are C, T (DNA) and U (RNA). DNA uses 2-deoxyribose, RNA uses ribose (2′-OH). Base + sugar = nucleoside (adenosine, guanosine; cytidine, uridine, thymidine); add phosphate = nucleotide (AMP → ADP → ATP). Three questions name any nucleotide: which base, which sugar, how many phosphates. Figures, naming table and example: Section 12.1.

2. Structure

Primary structure: bases on a sugar–phosphate backbone joined by phosphodiester bonds, written 5′→3′ (free phosphate at 5′, free OH at 3′). Secondary structure of DNA: a double helix of two antiparallel, complementary strands held by hydrogen bonds, A=T (two) and G≡C (three); a purine always pairs with a pyrimidine. Chargaff: %A = %T and %G = %C in double-stranded DNA. RNA is single-stranded, uses ribose and U, and is short-lived. Figures, the DNA vs. RNA table, the Try It widget and the Chargaff example: Section 12.2.

3. The central dogma

DNA → DNA (replication, nucleus), DNA → mRNA (transcription, nucleus), mRNA → protein (translation, ribosomes in the cytoplasm). A codon is 3 mRNA bases; AUG = start (Met), UAA/UAG/UGA = stop. Only retroviruses run transcription backward. Table and diagram: Section 12.3.

4. Types of RNA

mRNA carries the code in codons; tRNA carries one amino acid (as an ester at its 3′ end) and has the anticodon; rRNA plus protein is the ribosome, and rRNA is the catalyst that forms the peptide bond. Full treatment: Section 12.9.

5. Recombinant DNA (overview)

Cut (restriction enzyme, a hydrolase, leaves sticky ends), paste (sticky ends pair; DNA ligase re-forms the phosphodiester bonds) into a plasmid vector, copy (bacteria replicate it), express (bacteria make the human protein: insulin, growth hormone, factor VIII, vaccine proteins). PCR copies a stretch of DNA by heat–cool–extend cycles with a heat-stable polymerase. Details and genetic testing: Section 12.8.

6. Replication

Semiconservative: each daughter helix has one old strand and one new. Helicase unzips, primase lays an RNA primer, DNA polymerase adds nucleotides to a 3′-OH so every new strand grows 5′→3′ (leading strand continuous, lagging strand in Okazaki fragments), and DNA ligase seals the fragments. Details and the DNA extraction lab: Section 12.3.

7. Transcription

RNA polymerase binds the promoter, reads the template strand 3′→5′ and builds mRNA 5′→3′ with U for T; the mRNA matches the coding strand except T → U. Given a template, complement it; given a coding strand, copy it and swap T for U. Introns are spliced out before the mRNA leaves the nucleus. Details and example: Section 12.4.

8. Translation and the genetic code

64 codons for 20 amino acids: the code is degenerate (third base varies), non-overlapping, read 5′→3′ from AUG to a stop codon, and nearly universal. Four stages: activation (synthetase + ATP makes the aminoacyl ester), initiation (Met-tRNA at AUG, P site), elongation (A site, peptidyl transferase, translocation), termination (stop codon, release). Codon–anticodon is hydrogen bonding; amino acid–tRNA is a covalent ester. Codon table, stages and example: Section 12.5.

9. Mutations

Identify the change in the DNA first: a point mutation (one base replaced; silent, missense or nonsense), a deletion or an insertion (one or two bases: frameshift; a multiple of three: one amino acid gained or lost, as in ΔF508). Mutagens: UV (thymine dimers), ionizing radiation, chemicals (nitrous acid, smoke). Sickle cell is one missense change, GAG → GUG, Glu → Val. Table, mutagens, examples and newborn-screening box: Section 12.6.

10. Viruses

Nucleic acid in a protein coat, no machinery of its own. DNA viruses (herpes, HPV, hepatitis B) use the host directly; RNA viruses (influenza, SARS-CoV-2) copy their RNA with a viral polymerase and mutate fast; retroviruses (HIV) use reverse transcriptase to make DNA from RNA and integrate it. Antivirals block viral-only enzymes; vaccines (killed virus, recombinant protein, mRNA) show the immune system a piece in advance. Details and clinical boxes: Section 12.7.

Health Career Connection

  • Nursing – sickle cell disease: one DNA base substitution changes codon GAG (Glu) to GUG (Val) in the mRNA for the β chain of hemoglobin (Section 12.6; Chapter 10).
  • Nursing – mRNA vaccines and HIV drugs: an mRNA vaccine is translated by your ribosomes for a few days and never enters the nucleus; HIV drugs block reverse transcriptase and other viral-only enzymes (Section 12.7).
  • Nursing – genetic testing: PCR and sequencing of blood or saliva identify carriers and predict drug metabolism (Section 12.8).
  • Dental hygiene – HPV and cold sores: HPV-16 (a DNA virus) causes most oropharyngeal cancers, so screen at every recall; HSV-1 reactivates as herpes labialis and elective treatment waits until the lesion heals (Section 12.7). Radiographs follow ALARA because ionizing radiation damages DNA (Section 12.6).

Worked Examples

Example 1: Complementary DNA

Problem: Write the complementary strand of 5′-GACCTA-3′.

Solution

Pair base by base (G–C, A–T): 3′-CTGGAT-5′. Rewritten 5′→3′: 5′-TAGGTC-3′.

Check: Line the two up antiparallel: 5′-GACCTA-3′ over 3′-CTGGAT-5′. Every column is A/T or G/C, and the answer has the same number of bases (6) as the original.

Example 2: Transcription and translation

Problem: A DNA template strand reads 3′-TACGGAACT-5′. Write the mRNA and the peptide.

Solution

mRNA is complementary to the template, with U in place of T: 5′-AUG CCU UGA-3′.

Codons: AUG = Met (start), CCU = Pro, UGA = stop. Peptide: Met-Pro.

Check: The mRNA begins with AUG, as a message must, and ends with a stop codon, so the peptide has (3 − 1) = 2 amino acids.

Example 3: Mutation effect

Problem: In the mRNA above, the second codon changes from CCU to CCC. What happens to the peptide?

Solution

CCC also codes for Pro, so the peptide is unchanged. This is a silent mutation. The genetic code has more than one codon for most amino acids.

Check: Look at the CC_ block of the codon table: all four third bases give Pro. Changes in the third base are the ones most likely to be silent.

Example 4: Identify the type of change in the DNA

Problem: The coding strand of a short gene normally reads 5′-ATG CCA GTT TGG TAA-3′. Three mutant versions are found: (a) 5′-ATG CCA GCT TGG TAA-3′; (b) 5′-ATG CCA GTT GGT AA-3′; (c) 5′-ATG CCC AGT TTG GTA A-3′. For each, identify the type of change in the DNA (point mutation, deletion or insertion), write the mRNA and peptide, and state the effect.

Solution

Normal: mRNA 5′-AUG CCA GUU UGG UAA-3′ = Met-Pro-Val-Trp (UAA = stop).

(a) Same number of bases; one T became C in the third codon: a point mutation. mRNA AUG CCA GCU UGG UAA = Met-Pro-Ala-Trp. One amino acid changed (Val → Ala, both nonpolar, so probably a mild effect).

(b) One base fewer (14 instead of 15); the first T of the third codon is gone: a deletion mutation. mRNA AUG CCA GUU GGU AA = Met-Pro-Val-Gly-… and no stop codon in frame: a frameshift that changes every codon after the deletion.

(c) One base more (16); an extra C after CC: an insertion mutation. mRNA AUG CCC AGU UUG GUA A = Met-Pro-Ser-Leu-Val-…, again a frameshift; the protein is scrambled from the second codon on.

Check: Count the bases first. The same count means a point mutation (silent, one amino acid changed, or a new stop codon); one fewer or one more means a deletion or insertion, and unless the change is a multiple of three it shifts the frame.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Putting T in RNA or U in DNA.
  • Forgetting to reverse direction when writing a complementary strand.
  • Reading codons in overlapping or shifted groups. Start at AUG and read in non-overlapping triplets.
  • Mixing up purines and pyrimidines. "Pure As Gold": purines are A and G.
  • Confusing nucleoside with nucleotide. Nucleoside = base + sugar; nucleotide adds the phosphate.
  • Writing the mRNA as a copy of the template strand. The mRNA is the complement of the template (and a copy of the coding strand with U for T).
  • Calling every insertion or deletion a frameshift. Adding or removing three bases (or six, or nine) keeps the reading frame; ΔF508 in cystic fibrosis removes exactly one amino acid.
  • Applying Chargaff's rules to single-stranded RNA or to one strand of DNA. %A = %T only when every A is paired with a T.
  • Assuming the ribosome "reads" the amino acids. It reads codons; the amino acid is already attached to the tRNA by the synthetase enzyme, and only base pairing happens on the ribosome.
  • Mixing up the two kinds of bond in translation. Codon to anticodon is hydrogen bonding; amino acid to tRNA is a covalent aminoacyl ester.
  • Naming a purine nucleoside with -idine. Purines take -osine (adenosine, guanosine); pyrimidines take -idine (cytidine, uridine, thymidine).

Practice

Exercise 1

Which bases are pyrimidines? Which base is found only in RNA?

Answer
C, T, and U are pyrimidines. Uracil is only in RNA.

Exercise 2

A DNA segment is 30% adenine. What percent is guanine?

Answer
A = T = 30%, so G + C = 40%. G = C = 20%.

Exercise 3

Translate the mRNA 5′-AUG UUU GGC UAG-3′.

Answer
Met-Phe-Gly (UAG = stop)

Exercise 4

Which enzyme seals the gaps between DNA fragments during replication?

Answer
DNA ligase

Exercise 5

Name the nucleotide that contains uracil, ribose, and two phosphates. Could it be part of DNA?

Answer
Uridine diphosphate (UDP). No: it has ribose and uracil, both found only in RNA. (UDP is also the carrier that activates glucose for glycogen synthesis, Chapter 13.)

Exercise 6

The coding strand of a gene reads 5′-ATGGGTTGCTGA-3′. Write the mRNA and the peptide. Then say what happens if the C in the third codon is deleted.

Answer
mRNA 5′-AUG GGU UGC UGA-3′: Met-Gly-Cys (UGA = stop). Deleting the C gives AUG GGU UGU GA: Met-Gly-Cys and then no stop codon in frame. This is a frameshift; the ribosome would keep reading into whatever follows until it hits a stop in the new frame.
