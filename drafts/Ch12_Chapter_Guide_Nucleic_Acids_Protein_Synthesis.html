Learning Objectives

  • Identify the parts of a nucleotide, tell purines from pyrimidines, and name nucleosides and nucleotides (adenosine, AMP, ADP, ATP).
  • Describe the primary structure of a nucleic acid (the 3′→5′ phosphodiester backbone) and the secondary structure of DNA (antiparallel double helix with A=T and G≡C pairs).
  • Compare the structures, locations, and jobs of DNA and RNA, and of the three main kinds of RNA.
  • Write complementary DNA and RNA strands with correct 5′/3′ ends and use Chargaff's rules.
  • Describe replication, transcription, and translation, and the main enzymes involved.
  • Use the genetic code to translate mRNA, classify mutations by their effect, and explain the chemistry behind recombinant DNA, PCR, viruses, and vaccines.

Every protein you met in Chapter 10, including every enzyme in Chapter 11, is built from instructions stored in DNA. This chapter is about how that information is stored as a sequence of bases, copied when a cell divides, and read out to make a protein. The chemistry is familiar: esters of phosphoric acid (Chapter 6), heterocyclic amines (Chapter 7), cyclic sugars (Chapter 8), and hydrogen bonds (Chapter 1). What is new is that the order of the pieces carries a message.

Key Ideas

1. Nucleotides

A nucleotide is the monomer of a nucleic acid. It has three parts: nitrogen base + five-carbon sugar + phosphate.

  • Purines (two fused rings, one six-membered and one five-membered): adenine (A), guanine (G). You saw the purine ring among the heterocyclic amines in Chapter 7.
  • Pyrimidines (one six-membered ring): cytosine (C), thymine (T, DNA only), uracil (U, RNA only). Thymine is simply uracil with a methyl group on C5.
  • The sugar is a five-membered cyclic hemiacetal (a furanose, Chapter 8). DNA uses 2-deoxyribose (an H in place of the OH on C2′). RNA uses ribose (an OH on C2′). The sugar carbons are numbered with primes (1′ to 5′) so they are never confused with the ring atoms of the base.

Nucleoside versus nucleotide. Join a base to C1′ of the sugar through the base's nitrogen (N9 of a purine, N1 of a pyrimidine) and you have a nucleoside. The bond is an N-glycosidic bond, the nitrogen cousin of the glycosidic bond in disaccharides (Chapter 8). Add one or more phosphate groups to the C5′-OH as a phosphate ester (Chapter 6) and you have a nucleotide. The names follow a pattern:

The same nucleoside triphosphates are also the building blocks that polymerases use to make DNA (dATP, dGTP, dCTP, dTTP) and RNA (ATP, GTP, CTP, UTP).

Professor Tip

Three parts, three questions. For any nucleotide ask: (1) Which base, purine or pyrimidine? (2) Which sugar, ribose (2′-OH present) or deoxyribose? (3) How many phosphates? Those three answers name it. "Deoxyguanosine triphosphate" tells you guanine, deoxyribose, three phosphates: dGTP, a DNA building block.

2. Structure

Primary structure is the sequence of bases along one strand, held together by a covalent backbone.

  • Nucleotides are joined by phosphodiester bonds: the phosphate on C5′ of one nucleotide forms an ester with the 3′-OH of the next sugar. The phosphate is esterified twice (one ester to each sugar), hence diester. The result is a repeating sugar–phosphate backbone with the bases sticking out to the side.
  • Every strand has two different ends: a 5′ end (a free phosphate on C5′) and a 3′ end (a free OH on C3′). By convention a sequence is written and read 5′→3′, and only the bases are written: 5′-ACG-3′. If no ends are shown, assume the left end is 5′.
  • At body pH each phosphate carries a negative charge, so DNA and RNA are polyanions. That is why they dissolve in water, why they are attracted to positive ions such as Mg2+, and why they move toward the positive electrode in gel electrophoresis.

Secondary structure is the way strands pair up. DNA is a double helix of two strands wound around a common axis.

  • The two strands are antiparallel: one runs 5′→3′ while its partner runs 3′→5′.
  • The backbones are on the outside; the bases point inward and pair by hydrogen bonds: A=T (two H-bonds) and G≡C (three H-bonds). In RNA, A pairs with U. A purine always pairs with a pyrimidine, so every "rung" of the ladder is the same width.
  • Because of this pairing the strands are complementary: the sequence of one strand fixes the sequence of the other. Chargaff's rules follow directly: in any double-stranded DNA, %A = %T and %G = %C, so %A + %G = %C + %T = 50%.
  • The flat base pairs also stack on one another like coins, and these stacking forces (London dispersion forces, Chapter 1) plus thousands of hydrogen bonds make the helix stable at body temperature. Heating DNA to about 90 °C separates ("melts") the strands; cooling lets them re-pair. PCR (Key Idea 5) is built on this.

Professor Tip

Always label the ends. When you write a complementary strand, flip the direction: if the given strand reads 5′→3′ left to right, the partner reads 3′→5′ left to right. Then, by convention, rewrite it 5′→3′.

3. The central dogma

Information flows in one direction in nearly all cells: DNA is copied to DNA (replication), DNA is read into RNA (transcription), and RNA is read into protein (translation). This flow is called the central dogma of molecular biology. The one famous exception is the retroviruses, which run the second step backward (Key Idea 10).

  • A codon is 3 mRNA bases. AUG = start (Met). UAA, UAG, UGA = stop.
  • Mutations: a substitution changes one base, which may change one amino acid. An insertion or deletion shifts the reading frame (frameshift) and usually ruins the whole protein.
  • Viruses carry DNA or RNA and use the host's machinery. Retroviruses (HIV) use reverse transcriptase to make DNA from RNA.

Key Ideas 6 to 10 take each of these steps apart.

4. Types of RNA

  • mRNA (messenger): carries the gene's code from DNA to the ribosome. Its bases are read in triplets called codons.
  • tRNA (transfer): carries a specific amino acid, attached as an ester at its 3′ end, and has the anticodon that pairs with a codon.
  • rRNA (ribosomal): together with proteins, forms the ribosome and catalyzes peptide bond formation. It is the most abundant RNA in a cell.

Section 12.9 describes each of these, plus the small RNAs that edit and regulate mRNA.

5. Recombinant DNA (overview)

Restriction enzymes cut DNA at specific sequences. A human gene can be cut out and inserted into a bacterial plasmid (a small circle of DNA), joined with DNA ligase, and placed back into bacteria. The bacteria then make the human protein. Most insulin used by people with diabetes today is produced this way.

Here is the logic in four steps, each one a reaction type you already know:

  1. Cut. A restriction enzyme is a hydrolase (Chapter 11) that recognizes a short sequence, often a palindrome such as 5′-GAATTC-3′, and hydrolyzes a phosphodiester bond in each strand. Many cut in a staggered way, leaving short single-stranded "sticky ends" that can base-pair with any other end cut by the same enzyme.
  2. Paste. The gene and the opened plasmid (the vector) are mixed; their sticky ends pair by hydrogen bonds, and DNA ligase (a ligase, Chapter 11) re-forms the phosphodiester bonds. The product is recombinant DNA: DNA from two sources joined covalently.
  3. Copy. The plasmid is taken up by bacteria (or yeast), which replicate it every time they divide.
  4. Express. The cells transcribe and translate the inserted gene, and the protein is harvested and purified. Recombinant human insulin, human growth hormone, clotting factor VIII, erythropoietin, and the hepatitis B and HPV vaccine proteins are all made this way.

PCR. The polymerase chain reaction copies a chosen stretch of DNA millions of times in a test tube. A mixture of the DNA, two short synthetic primers that flank the target, the four dNTPs, and a heat-stable DNA polymerase (originally from a hot-spring bacterium) is cycled: heat to about 95 °C to separate the strands, cool to about 55 °C so the primers pair with their targets, warm to about 72 °C so the polymerase extends each primer. Every cycle doubles the DNA, so 30 cycles give about a billion copies. PCR is the basis of forensic DNA profiling, paternity testing, many genetic tests, and the laboratory tests that detect viral genes, such as RT-PCR for SARS-CoV-2 (the RT step uses reverse transcriptase to copy the viral RNA into DNA first).

6. Replication

Before a cell divides it must copy all of its DNA, and the copy must be nearly perfect. The double helix makes this possible: each strand is a template for its partner.

  • Semiconservative. The helix unwinds, each old strand directs the synthesis of a new complementary strand, and each daughter helix ends up with one old strand and one new strand.
  • Helicase breaks the hydrogen bonds and unzips the helix at a replication fork.
  • DNA polymerase reads a template strand and adds the complementary deoxynucleotide to the 3′-OH of the growing strand. It can only add to a 3′-OH, so every new strand grows 5′→3′, and it cannot start from nothing: a short RNA primer made by primase supplies the first 3′-OH. The energy comes from the incoming nucleoside triphosphate, which loses two phosphates (pyrophosphate) as the new phosphodiester bond forms.
  • Because the two template strands are antiparallel, one new strand (the leading strand) grows continuously toward the fork while the other (the lagging strand) is made in short pieces away from the fork; the primers are replaced by DNA and DNA ligase seals the pieces together.
  • Proofreading. DNA polymerase checks each new base pair; if it has just added the wrong base, it backs up, removes it, and tries again. With proofreading and the cell's separate repair enzymes, the error rate is about one in a billion base pairs. (RNA polymerases do not proofread; see Section 12.9.)

In the Lab

In the DNA extraction lab you break open cells (strawberry, cheek, or onion) with a detergent solution, which dissolves the lipid membranes (Chapter 9). Salt is added so that Na+ ions surround the negatively charged phosphate backbone; this lets the strands clump instead of repelling one another. Cold ethanol is layered on top: DNA is much less soluble in ethanol than in water, so it comes out of solution as a white, stringy mass at the boundary. Meat tenderizer (a protease) or pineapple juice can be added to digest the proteins wrapped around the DNA. The strings you lift out on a glass rod are millions of double helices tangled together.

7. Transcription

A gene is a stretch of DNA that codes for one protein (or one RNA). To use it, the cell makes a working copy in RNA.

  • RNA polymerase binds a promoter, a sequence just before the gene that says "start here," unwinds a short stretch of the helix, and reads one strand, the template strand, in the 3′→5′ direction.
  • It builds the mRNA 5′→3′ by the same base-pairing rules as replication, except that U pairs with A: template A → U, T → A, G → C, C → G. No primer is needed.
  • The other DNA strand, the coding strand (or sense strand), is not read, but its sequence matches the mRNA exactly except that T replaces U. Gene sequences in databases are written as the coding strand for this reason.
  • At a termination signal the polymerase stops and releases the RNA. In eukaryotes the new RNA is then processed before it leaves the nucleus: it gets a 5′ cap and a 3′ poly-A tail, and non-coding stretches called introns are cut out so the coding exons are joined (Section 12.9).

Professor Tip

"Template" and "coding" trip up more students than anything else in this chapter. The template strand is the one the polymerase actually pairs with, so the mRNA is its complement. The coding strand is the one the mRNA looks like. If a problem gives you a template strand, complement it (with U). If it gives you a coding strand, just copy it and swap T for U.

8. Translation and the genetic code

Translation turns a sequence of bases into a sequence of amino acids, which means switching languages: four letters (A, U, G, C) to twenty amino acids. The dictionary is the genetic code.

  • mRNA is read in non-overlapping groups of three bases, codons, in the 5′→3′ direction. With four bases there are 43 = 64 codons for 20 amino acids, so the code is degenerate: most amino acids have more than one codon, and the codons for one amino acid usually differ only in the third base.
  • AUG is the start codon; it sets the reading frame and codes for methionine, so every new polypeptide begins with Met (often removed later). UAA, UAG, and UGA are stop codons; no tRNA matches them, and a release factor protein frees the finished chain.
  • Each tRNA carries one amino acid and has a three-base anticodon that pairs, antiparallel, with the codon. The enzyme that loads the correct amino acid onto each tRNA (aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase) is where the "translation" really happens: once the amino acid is on its tRNA, base pairing does the rest.
  • The ribosome (rRNA plus proteins, two subunits) holds the mRNA and two tRNAs at a time, joins the amino acids by peptide bonds (an amidation, Chapter 7, catalyzed by the rRNA itself), and moves one codon down the mRNA. The chain grows from the N-terminus toward the C-terminus (Chapter 10).
  • The code is essentially universal: a human insulin gene is read the same way inside a bacterium, which is why recombinant DNA works.

9. Mutations

A mutation is a permanent change in the base sequence of DNA. It is copied into every descendant of that cell and, if it is in a sperm or egg, into every cell of the next generation. Classify a mutation by what it does to the DNA and then by what that does to the protein.

What causes mutations? Some are spontaneous copying errors that escape proofreading. Others are caused by mutagens:

  • Ultraviolet (UV) light makes two neighboring thymines bond covalently to each other (a thymine dimer), which the polymerase cannot read. Repair enzymes remove most dimers; the ones that escape cause skin cancer.
  • Ionizing radiation (X-rays, gamma rays, radioactive particles) breaks the backbone directly or makes reactive radicals from water that attack the bases.
  • Chemicals: nitrous acid converts an NH2 on a base to C=O, changing which base it pairs with; polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons in tobacco smoke (Chapter 3) and aflatoxin from moldy grain become reactive after the liver oxidizes them and then bond to guanine. A chemical that causes mutations is a mutagen; one that causes cancer is a carcinogen, and most carcinogens are mutagens.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Many inherited diseases are single mutations that you can now read. Sickle cell disease is one missense substitution: in the gene for the β chain of hemoglobin, codon 6 changes from GAG to GTG (DNA coding strand), so the mRNA codon GAG (Glu) becomes GUG (Val). A polar, charged glutamate on the protein surface becomes a nonpolar valine; deoxygenated hemoglobin molecules stick to one another at that patch and polymerize into rods that bend the red cell into a sickle, causing hemolytic anemia and painful vaso-occlusive crises. Cystic fibrosis is most often ΔF508, a three-base deletion that removes one phenylalanine (residue 508) from the CFTR chloride-channel protein; the reading frame is intact, but the protein misfolds and is destroyed, so thick mucus clogs the airways and pancreatic ducts. Phenylketonuria (PKU) is a mutation in the gene for phenylalanine hydroxylase, the enzyme that converts phenylalanine to tyrosine (Chapter 11), so phenylalanine builds up and damages the developing brain. All three are autosomal recessive and all three are caught by newborn screening of the heel-stick blood spot (abnormal hemoglobin, elevated phenylalanine, and a CF marker confirmed by sweat chloride or DNA testing), so management such as the PKU low-phenylalanine diet can start in the first weeks of life. Finally, every sunburn is DNA damage: UV light forms thymine dimers in skin cells.

10. Viruses

A virus is a package of nucleic acid, DNA or RNA, inside a protein coat (and often a lipid envelope borrowed from the last host cell). It has no ribosomes, no metabolism, and no way to make ATP, so it is not a cell: it must get inside a host cell and borrow the host's polymerases, ribosomes, tRNA, amino acids, and energy to make copies of itself.

  • DNA viruses (herpes simplex, hepatitis B, HPV, chickenpox) deliver DNA that the host transcribes and translates like its own genes.
  • RNA viruses (influenza, SARS-CoV-2, measles, hepatitis C) deliver RNA that is copied by a viral RNA polymerase and translated directly. RNA polymerases make many errors, so RNA viruses mutate quickly (Section 12.9).
  • Retroviruses (HIV) carry RNA plus the enzyme reverse transcriptase, which copies the RNA into DNA, reversing the usual flow of information. A second viral enzyme, integrase, splices that DNA into a host chromosome, where it stays for the life of the cell. HIV infects helper T cells and gradually destroys the immune system (AIDS).

Antivirals target the steps that are unique to the virus. Nucleoside analogs (acyclovir for herpes, and the nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors for HIV) are fake building blocks: the viral polymerase adds them to the growing chain, but they lack the 3′-OH that the next nucleotide needs, so the chain stops (a chain terminator). Protease inhibitors block the viral protease that must cut the virus's long polyprotein into working pieces; HIV protease inhibitors and the SARS-CoV-2 drug nirmatrelvir work this way. Vaccines show the immune system a harmless piece of the virus ahead of time: a killed or weakened virus, a recombinant viral protein (hepatitis B, HPV), or, in mRNA vaccines, an mRNA wrapped in lipid nanoparticles that your own ribosomes translate into one viral protein for a few days.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Several drug classes you will give or monitor act on the chemistry of this chapter. Chemotherapy agents that stop DNA synthesis hit rapidly dividing cells hardest: 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) is a uracil look-alike with F in place of H; cells convert it to a nucleotide that jams the enzyme making thymine nucleotides, so the cancer cell cannot replicate its DNA. Methotrexate blocks dihydrofolate reductase, the enzyme that keeps folate in its active form; without active folate the cell cannot make purines or thymine. Both drugs also hit the gut lining, bone marrow, and hair follicles, which explains mucositis, low blood counts, and hair loss. The same folate chemistry is why folic acid in early pregnancy lowers the risk of neural tube defects such as spina bifida: the neural tube closes in the first month, when cells divide fastest and need folate to make DNA. For a patient with HIV, antiretroviral therapy combines drugs that block reverse transcriptase with a protease inhibitor or integrase inhibitor, each targeting a viral enzyme human cells do not have; adherence matters because a virus with no proofreading quickly evolves resistance to a single drug. And an mRNA vaccine is simply a fragile message: it needs cold storage because RNA hydrolyzes easily, it never enters the nucleus, and it is degraded within days, after ribosomes have made the viral protein that trains the immune response.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Viruses are part of every day in the operatory. Human papillomavirus type 16 is a DNA virus whose proteins disable two of the cell's growth-control proteins, and it now causes most oropharyngeal cancers (base of tongue, tonsils); oral cancer screening at each recall visit and the HPV vaccine, a recombinant viral coat protein, are the defenses. Hepatitis B is a bloodborne DNA virus that survives for days on surfaces, so clinicians are vaccinated (again a recombinant protein made in yeast) and follow standard precautions. Herpes simplex virus 1 hides in the trigeminal nerve and reactivates as herpes labialis (cold sores); during an active lesion elective treatment is deferred because the virus spreads by contact and aerosol, and acyclovir, a chain-terminating guanosine analog, shortens outbreaks. Two inherited conditions you will see on radiographs are mutations in the genes for matrix proteins: amelogenesis imperfecta (defective enamel proteins such as amelogenin; thin, pitted, discolored enamel) and dentinogenesis imperfecta (defective dentin sialophosphoprotein; opalescent teeth with obliterated pulp chambers). Saliva carries DNA from shed epithelial cells and microbes, so a saliva sample serves genetic tests and PCR tests for oral HPV or SARS-CoV-2. Finally, dental radiographs use ionizing radiation, which can break DNA, so you follow ALARA (as low as reasonably achievable): digital sensors, collimation, thyroid collars, and radiographs only when they change the treatment plan.

Health Career Connection

  • Dental hygiene – HPV and oral cancer screening: High-risk human papillomavirus (HPV-16) is now a leading cause of oropharyngeal cancers. Viral DNA disrupts the host cell's growth controls, which is part of why oral cancer screenings matter at every recall visit.
  • Dental hygiene – cold sores: Herpes labialis (HSV-1) outbreaks are shortened by acyclovir or valacyclovir. These are nucleoside look-alikes that viral DNA polymerase builds into DNA, stopping chain growth because they lack a 3′-OH. Elective dental treatment is usually postponed during an active outbreak because the lesions are contagious.
  • Nursing – mRNA vaccines: These deliver an mRNA that your ribosomes translate into a harmless viral protein, so your immune system learns to recognize it. The mRNA is broken down within days and never enters the nucleus.
  • Nursing – sickle cell disease: One DNA base substitution changes codon GAG (Glu) to GUG (Val) in the mRNA for the β chain of hemoglobin (see Chapter 10).
  • Nursing – genetic testing: PCR and DNA sequencing of a blood or saliva sample can identify carriers of sickle cell trait, cystic fibrosis, and many other conditions, and can show which patients will metabolize a drug slowly or quickly (pharmacogenomics).

Worked Examples

Example 1: Complementary DNA

Problem: Write the complementary strand of 5′-GACCTA-3′.

Solution

Pair base by base (G–C, A–T): 3′-CTGGAT-5′. Rewritten 5′→3′: 5′-TAGGTC-3′.

Check: Line the two up antiparallel: 5′-GACCTA-3′ over 3′-CTGGAT-5′. Every column is A/T or G/C, and the answer has the same number of bases (6) as the original.

Example 2: Transcription and translation

Problem: A DNA template strand reads 3′-TACGGAACT-5′. Write the mRNA and the peptide.

Solution

mRNA is complementary to the template, with U in place of T: 5′-AUG CCU UGA-3′.

Codons: AUG = Met (start), CCU = Pro, UGA = stop. Peptide: Met-Pro.

Check: The mRNA begins with AUG, as a message must, and ends with a stop codon, so the peptide has (3 − 1) = 2 amino acids.

Example 3: Mutation effect

Problem: In the mRNA above, the second codon changes from CCU to CCC. What happens to the peptide?

Solution

CCC also codes for Pro, so the peptide is unchanged. This is a silent mutation. The genetic code has more than one codon for most amino acids.

Check: Look at the CC_ block of the codon table: all four third bases give Pro. Changes in the third base are the ones most likely to be silent.

Example 4: Chargaff's rules

Problem: A sample of double-stranded DNA from a bacterium is 24% adenine. Predict the percentages of T, G, and C.

Solution

Because A pairs only with T, %T = %A = 24%. Together A and T account for 48%, which leaves 100% − 48% = 52% for G plus C. Because G pairs only with C, they split that evenly: %G = %C = 26%.

Check: 24 + 24 + 26 + 26 = 100%, and the purines (A + G = 50%) equal the pyrimidines (T + C = 50%), as they must in any double helix. (These rules do not apply to a single-stranded RNA, where nothing forces A to equal U.)

Example 5: Name the nucleotide

Problem: A molecule contains guanine, a sugar with no OH on C2′, and three phosphate groups on C5′. Name it, give its abbreviation, and say what it is used for.

Solution

Guanine on a sugar is guanosine; no 2′-OH means the sugar is deoxyribose, so the nucleoside is deoxyguanosine. Three phosphates make it a triphosphate: deoxyguanosine triphosphate, dGTP. It is one of the four building blocks DNA polymerase uses during replication; the two outer phosphates leave as pyrophosphate when the nucleotide is added to a growing strand.

Check: Base, sugar, phosphate count: all three are in the name. If the sugar had a 2′-OH, the answer would be GTP, an RNA building block (and an energy carrier like ATP).

Example 6: Coding strand to protein

Problem: The coding strand of a short gene reads 5′-ATGAAGTGGCACTAA-3′. Write the template strand, the mRNA, and the peptide.

Solution

Template strand: the complement, antiparallel: 3′-TACTTCACCGTGATT-5′.

mRNA: the coding strand with U for T (equivalently, the complement of the template): 5′-AUG AAG UGG CAC UAA-3′.

Peptide: AUG = Met, AAG = Lys, UGG = Trp, CAC = His, UAA = stop: Met-Lys-Trp-His.

Check: The mRNA and the coding strand read the same (ATG/AUG, AAG/AAG...). Fifteen bases make five codons; one is the stop, so four amino acids.

Example 7: The sickle-cell mutation

Problem: In the normal β-globin gene, the coding strand for codons 5–7 reads 5′-CCT GAG GAG-3′. In sickle cell disease the middle codon is CCT GTG GAG. Write both mRNAs, name the amino acids, classify the mutation, and explain why one amino acid matters so much.

Solution

Normal mRNA: 5′-CCU GAG GAG-3′ = Pro-Glu-Glu. Mutant mRNA: 5′-CCU GUG GAG-3′ = Pro-Val-Glu. One base (A → T in DNA, A → U in mRNA) replaced another, and the new codon codes for a different amino acid, so this is a substitution that is missense. Glutamate has a charged, polar side chain (–CH2CH2COO−); valine has a nonpolar isopropyl side chain. The nonpolar patch on the surface of hemoglobin S sticks to a pocket on neighboring molecules when oxygen is unloaded, and the molecules stack into fibers that deform the red cell.

Check: The frame is unchanged (still three-base codons), so only one amino acid differs, which matches a single substitution. A frameshift would have scrambled every codon after it.

Example 8: Classify the mutations

Problem: The mRNA 5′-AUG UGG CAU AAA UAG-3′ (Met-Trp-His-Lys) undergoes three different mutations. Classify each: (a) UGG → UAG; (b) CAU → CAC; (c) a G is inserted after AUG, giving AUG GUG GCA UAA AUA G.

Solution

(a) UGG (Trp) becomes UAG, a stop codon: a nonsense mutation; the peptide is just Met. (b) CAU and CAC both code for His: silent. (c) Reading in threes after the insertion: AUG GUG GCA UAA = Met-Val-Ala-stop. Every codon after the insertion changed and a new stop appeared early: a frameshift.

Check: Substitutions keep the number of bases (15) and change at most one amino acid; the insertion makes 16 bases, which is not a multiple of three, and changes everything downstream.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Putting T in RNA or U in DNA.
  • Forgetting to reverse direction when writing a complementary strand.
  • Reading codons in overlapping or shifted groups. Start at AUG and read in non-overlapping triplets.
  • Mixing up purines and pyrimidines. "Pure As Gold": purines are A and G.
  • Confusing nucleoside with nucleotide. Nucleoside = base + sugar; nucleotide adds the phosphate.
  • Writing the mRNA as a copy of the template strand. The mRNA is the complement of the template (and a copy of the coding strand with U for T).
  • Calling every insertion or deletion a frameshift. Adding or removing three bases (or six, or nine) keeps the reading frame; ΔF508 in cystic fibrosis removes exactly one amino acid.
  • Applying Chargaff's rules to single-stranded RNA or to one strand of DNA. %A = %T only when every A is paired with a T.
  • Assuming the ribosome "reads" the amino acids. It reads codons; the amino acid is already attached to the tRNA by the synthetase enzyme, and only base pairing happens on the ribosome.

Practice

Exercise 1

Which bases are pyrimidines? Which base is found only in RNA?

Answer
C, T, and U are pyrimidines. Uracil is only in RNA.

Exercise 2

A DNA segment is 30% adenine. What percent is guanine?

Answer
A = T = 30%, so G + C = 40%. G = C = 20%.

Exercise 3

Translate the mRNA 5′-AUG UUU GGC UAG-3′.

Answer
Met-Phe-Gly (UAG = stop)

Exercise 4

Which enzyme seals the gaps between DNA fragments during replication?

Answer
DNA ligase

Exercise 5

Name the nucleotide that contains uracil, ribose, and two phosphates. Could it be part of DNA?

Answer
Uridine diphosphate (UDP). No: it has ribose and uracil, both found only in RNA. (UDP is also the carrier that activates glucose for glycogen synthesis, Chapter 13.)

Exercise 6

The coding strand of a gene reads 5′-ATGGGTTGCTGA-3′. Write the mRNA and the peptide. Then say what happens if the C in the third codon is deleted.

Answer
mRNA 5′-AUG GGU UGC UGA-3′: Met-Gly-Cys (UGA = stop). Deleting the C gives AUG GGU UGU GA: Met-Gly-Cys and then no stop codon in frame. This is a frameshift; the ribosome would keep reading into whatever follows until it hits a stop in the new frame.
