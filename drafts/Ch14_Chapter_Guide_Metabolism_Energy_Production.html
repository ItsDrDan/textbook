Learning Objectives

  • Describe how pyruvate becomes acetyl CoA, and name the coenzymes (and their vitamins) that the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex needs.
  • Describe what enters and what leaves one turn of the citric acid cycle.
  • For each of the eight cycle steps, identify the reaction type, the enzyme class, and the coenzyme used, and trace the carbon balance.
  • Explain how electron transport and ATP synthase use NADH and FADH2 to make ATP, and predict what an inhibitor or uncoupler does to the process.
  • Calculate the ATP yield from acetyl CoA and from one glucose.
  • Calculate the energy content of food from grams of carbohydrate, protein, and fat.

Key Ideas

1. The citric acid cycle (Krebs cycle)

This happens in the mitochondrial matrix. Acetyl CoA (2 C) joins oxaloacetate (4 C) to make citrate (6 C). Over 8 steps, two carbons leave as CO2 and oxaloacetate is regenerated.

Per turn (per acetyl CoA): 2 CO2, 3 NADH, 1 FADH2, 1 GTP (= 1 ATP). One glucose makes 2 acetyl CoA, so the cycle turns twice.

Getting in: pyruvate → acetyl CoA. Pyruvate (3 C) from glycolysis is made in the cytosol, but the cycle runs in the matrix. Pyruvate crosses both mitochondrial membranes, and then the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (a cluster of three enzymes working as one unit) carries out an oxidative decarboxylation: one carbon leaves as CO2 (decarboxylation), the remaining two-carbon piece is oxidized (NAD+ → NADH), and the two-carbon acetyl group is attached to coenzyme A through a high-energy thioester bond. The complex needs five coenzymes, four of them made from B vitamins:

  • Thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP) – from thiamine (vitamin B1); it holds the carbon chain while CO2 leaves.
  • Lipoate (lipoic acid) – a disulfide-containing arm that carries the acetyl group; the body makes it, so it is not a vitamin.
  • Coenzyme A (CoA–SH) – from pantothenic acid (vitamin B5); it accepts the acetyl group as a thioester.
  • FAD – from riboflavin (vitamin B2); it re-oxidizes the lipoate arm.
  • NAD+ – from niacin (vitamin B3); it carries the electrons away as NADH.

Net: pyruvate + CoA–SH + NAD+ → acetyl CoA + CO2 + NADH. Because one glucose gives two pyruvates, this step runs twice per glucose (2 NADH, 2 CO2). It is not reversible, so once a carbon has become acetyl CoA it cannot go back to glucose; this is why fat (which is broken down to acetyl CoA) cannot be turned into glucose.

Three patterns are worth noticing. Every oxidation uses NAD+ except step 6, which uses FAD because turning a C–C single bond into a C=C double bond does not release enough energy to reduce NAD+. Steps 6–8 (alkane-like → alkene → alcohol → ketone) are exactly the alkene hydration and alcohol oxidation you learned in Chapters 3 and 4, now run by enzymes. And the only step that makes an energy carrier directly is step 5; all the rest of the cycle's energy is stored in NADH and FADH2 for electron transport.

Energy in, energy out. No ATP is spent in the cycle. Step 1 is driven by breaking the thioester bond of acetyl CoA; step 5 captures energy as GTP; the four oxidations (steps 3, 4, 6, 8) store energy in 3 NADH and 1 FADH2. Water enters in steps 1 and 7 (step 2 removes a water and then puts one back), which is where the extra hydrogens and oxygens come from.

2. Electron transport and oxidative phosphorylation

  • NADH and FADH2 hand their electrons to a chain of protein complexes in the inner mitochondrial membrane. The final electron acceptor is O2, which becomes H2O.
  • The energy released pumps H+ across the membrane. H+ flows back through ATP synthase, which makes ATP.
  • Yields used in this course: NADH → 2.5 ATP, FADH2 → 1.5 ATP. FADH2 enters the chain later, so it pumps fewer protons.

Redox bookkeeping. Oxidation and reduction always happen together: one substance loses electrons while another gains them. When NAD+ oxidizes a substrate it accepts a hydride ion (H−: one proton plus two electrons) and becomes NADH; the second hydrogen from the substrate is released as H+. That is why the half-reaction is written NAD+ + 2 H → NADH + H+. FAD accepts both hydrogens (2 H+ + 2 e−) and becomes FADH2. In electron transport the carriers run backward: NADH is oxidized to NAD+, giving the electrons to the chain, and at the end O2 is reduced to water. The NAD+ and FAD regenerated this way go back to the cycle; without O2 they are not regenerated and the cycle stops.

Professor Tip

"LEO the lion says GER." Loss of Electrons is Oxidation, Gain of Electrons is Reduction. NADH → NAD+ is oxidation (it gives its electrons to the chain). O2 → H2O is reduction. For organic molecules a quick shortcut also works: gaining O or losing H is oxidation; gaining H or losing O is reduction.

4. Energy in food

Carbohydrate and protein: 4 kcal/g. Fat: 9 kcal/g. (1 food Calorie = 1 kcal.) Fat yields more because its carbons are more reduced, so there is more to oxidize.

5. A closer look at electron transport

  • Complex I accepts electrons from NADH. Complex II accepts electrons from FADH2 (made by succinate dehydrogenase in the citric acid cycle).
  • Coenzyme Q (CoQ, ubiquinone) is a mobile carrier that moves electrons from I and II to Complex III. Cytochrome c, another mobile carrier, moves them to Complex IV (cytochrome oxidase), which reduces O2 to H2O.
  • Complexes I, III, and IV pump H+; Complex II does not. That is why FADH2 yields less ATP (1.5) than NADH (2.5).

The chain sits in the inner mitochondrial membrane, which is folded into cristae to give it more surface area. Think of it as four stations and two shuttles:

  • Complex I (NADH dehydrogenase): takes 2 e− from NADH, passes them to CoQ, and pumps H+ out of the matrix.
  • Complex II (succinate dehydrogenase): the same enzyme as step 6 of the cycle. Its FADH2 never leaves the complex; the electrons go straight to CoQ. No protons are pumped.
  • Coenzyme Q (ubiquinone): a small, fat-soluble molecule with a long hydrocarbon tail, so it dissolves in the lipid bilayer and diffuses between complexes. It is not a protein.
  • Complex III: takes electrons from reduced CoQ, passes them to cytochrome c, and pumps H+.
  • Cytochrome c: a small water-soluble protein with an iron-containing heme group. It sits on the outer face of the inner membrane and carries one electron at a time from III to IV.
  • Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase): collects four electrons, hands them to one O2 molecule along with 4 H+, and makes 2 H2O. It pumps H+ too. This is the only place in the body where you actually "use" the oxygen you breathe.

Chemiosmosis and ATP synthase. Pumping H+ into the intermembrane space creates a gradient: more positive charge and a lower pH outside the matrix than inside. The membrane is otherwise closed to H+, so the only way back is through ATP synthase (Complex V), a rotary motor. As H+ flow down their gradient, the enzyme spins and presses ADP and Pi together into ATP. This coupling of electron transport (oxidation) to ATP formation (phosphorylation) by a proton gradient is called chemiosmosis, and the whole process is oxidative phosphorylation.

Where 2.5 and 1.5 come from. About 10 H+ are pumped per NADH (by I, III and IV) and about 6 per FADH2 (by III and IV only). ATP synthase and the transporters that bring in ADP and Pi use roughly 4 H+ per ATP. So 10/4 = 2.5 ATP per NADH and 6/4 = 1.5 ATP per FADH2. These are called P/O ratios (phosphates added per oxygen atom reduced).

Inhibitors and uncouplers. Because the chain is one line, blocking any station stops everything after it, and the carriers before the block pile up in their reduced form. Uncouplers do something different: they let H+ leak back across the membrane without going through ATP synthase, so the chain keeps running (fast, even) but the energy leaves as heat instead of ATP. 6. Regulation and carbon balance

  • The citric acid cycle is slowed by high ATP and NADH (the cell has enough energy) and sped up by high ADP. Electron transport and ATP synthase also run only as fast as ADP is available ("respiratory control").
  • Carbon balance: each turn takes in 2 carbons (acetyl) and releases 2 carbons as CO2, so oxaloacetate is regenerated unchanged.

Three control points. The cycle has no hormone switch; it answers directly to the cell's energy state through allosteric control (Chapter 11):

  • Citrate synthase (step 1) is inhibited by ATP, NADH, and its own product citrate.
  • Isocitrate dehydrogenase (step 3) is the main pacemaker: inhibited by ATP and NADH, activated by ADP and by Ca2+.
  • α-Ketoglutarate dehydrogenase (step 4) is inhibited by NADH and succinyl CoA and activated by Ca2+.

The Ca2+ signal is clever: a contracting muscle releases Ca2+ to trigger contraction, and the same ion speeds up the cycle that will pay for it. The pyruvate dehydrogenase complex is regulated the same way (inhibited by acetyl CoA, NADH and ATP; activated by ADP, Ca2+ and pyruvate).

Respiratory control. Electron transport and ATP synthesis are tightly coupled, so the chain can only run as fast as H+ can flow back through ATP synthase, and that only happens when ADP is available. A resting cell with lots of ATP and little ADP breathes slowly; a working cell that is burning ATP to ADP breathes fast. This is the molecular reason your oxygen consumption rises when you exercise.

Carbon balance, extended. Oxaloacetate is a catalyst for the cycle: one molecule can process acetyl CoA after acetyl CoA. The two carbons released as CO2 in a given turn are not, strictly, the two that just came in as acetyl (they come off the oxaloacetate end of citrate), but the count is what matters: 2 C in, 2 C out, 4-carbon oxaloacetate back. For a whole glucose: 6 C → 2 CO2 (pyruvate dehydrogenase) + 4 CO2 (two turns) = 6 CO2.

7. Getting glycolysis NADH into the mitochondria

NADH made in the cytoplasm cannot cross the inner mitochondrial membrane. The malate–aspartate shuttle (liver, heart) passes its electrons inside as NADH, worth 2.5 ATP each; this is the value used in the 32 ATP total above. The glycerol-3-phosphate shuttle (muscle, brain) delivers them as FADH2 instead (1.5 ATP each), giving about 30 ATP per glucose. Section 14.5 shows how both shuttles work and does the full accounting.

Health Career Connection

  • Respiratory therapy and nursing – why oxygen matters: O2 is the final electron acceptor. Without it, electron transport stops, NADH cannot be recycled, and cells fall back to glycolysis alone (2 ATP instead of 32 per glucose). This is the biochemistry behind hypoxia.
  • Nursing – carbon monoxide and cyanide: CO binds hemoglobin so oxygen cannot be delivered. CO and cyanide also block cytochrome oxidase, the last complex of the chain. Oxygen can displace CO from hemoglobin, but a blocked Complex IV needs an antidote that removes the cyanide (see the Nursing box).
  • Nursing – newborn thermoregulation: Brown fat in infants contains an "uncoupling" protein that lets H+ leak back without making ATP. The energy is released as heat, which helps keep newborns warm.
  • Nutrition counseling: The 4-4-9 rule is the basis of nutrition labels and diet counseling for patients with diabetes, obesity, or malnutrition.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Poisons that block Complex IV. A patient pulled from a house fire can have both carbon monoxide and cyanide (from burning plastics and foam) in the blood. CO occupies hemoglobin so O2 is not delivered; cyanide binds the iron of cytochrome c oxidase so the O2 that does arrive cannot be used. In both cases the tissues switch to anaerobic glycolysis and blood lactate rises, causing metabolic acidosis. High-flow oxygen helps displace CO from hemoglobin; for cyanide the antidote hydroxocobalamin (a form of vitamin B12) binds the cyanide ion so it can be excreted as cyanocobalamin.

Sepsis. In severe infection, inflammatory signals appear to impair mitochondria so that cells use oxygen poorly even when it is delivered. Lactate can rise despite a normal oxygen saturation, which is one reason serial lactate levels are tracked in sepsis.

Thiamine and pyruvate dehydrogenase. Without TPP, pyruvate cannot become acetyl CoA; it backs up and is converted to lactate. In thiamine deficiency (seen with alcohol use disorder, bariatric surgery, or prolonged vomiting) the brain, which depends on glucose oxidation, is hit first (Wernicke encephalopathy). This is the chemistry behind the rule that thiamine is given with, or before, a glucose load in at-risk patients.

Temperature and metabolic rate. Every rise of 1 °C in body temperature raises oxygen demand by roughly 10%, so a fever is costly for a patient with poor lungs or heart; therapeutic hypothermia after cardiac arrest does the opposite, lowering the brain’s ATP demand. Inherited mitochondrial diseases, caused by mutations in electron transport proteins, show up first in high-demand tissues such as muscle and brain, often with a high resting lactate.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Tissues need a constant oxygen supply. Cells store almost no ATP, only seconds' worth, so any tissue whose blood flow stops loses oxidative phosphorylation at once. This is why ischemia (loss of blood supply) leads to necrosis (tissue death): glycolysis alone cannot meet the demand. Osteonecrosis of the jaw, a feared complication of some bone-active drugs and of radiation to the head and neck, involves a compromised blood supply to bone that cannot keep up with the oxygen demand of healing. The vasoconstrictor epinephrine in a local anesthetic cartridge deliberately reduces blood flow (and oxygen delivery) in a small area for a short time, which is useful for a cleaner field but is one reason the dose and the site are chosen carefully.

Plaque bacteria run on fermentation. The streptococci that start dental caries (tooth decay) lack a full electron transport chain; they make ATP by glycolysis and dump the electrons onto pyruvate to form lactic acid, the acid that demineralizes enamel below pH 5.5. The obligate anaerobes deep in periodontal pockets cannot tolerate oxygen at all. Chlorhexidine rinses work by disrupting bacterial membranes, which is where a bacterium's energy-producing machinery sits. Finally, a patient with a fever or an active infection has a higher metabolic rate and oxygen demand, something to note before a long appointment.

Worked Examples

Example 1: ATP per acetyl CoA

How many ATP are made from one acetyl CoA going through the citric acid cycle and electron transport?

Solution

3 NADH × 2.5 = 7.5; 1 FADH2 × 1.5 = 1.5; 1 GTP = 1. Total = 10 ATP.

Check: Two acetyl CoA per glucose give 20, which is the citric-acid-cycle line of the 32-ATP tally in Key Idea 3.

Example 2: Food energy

A snack bar has 20 g carbohydrate, 5 g protein, and 10 g fat. How many kcal does it contain?

Solution

20(4) + 5(4) + 10(9) = 80 + 20 + 90 = 190 kcal. About half the energy comes from the fat.

Check: 35 g of food at 4–9 kcal/g must land between 140 and 315 kcal; 190 does.

Example 3: Oxidized or reduced?

In each change, is the first species oxidized or reduced? (a) FADH2 → FAD; (b) NAD+ → NADH.

Solution

(a) Loses H and electrons: oxidized. (b) Gains H and electrons: reduced.

Check: LEO says GER: the carrier that loses hydrogen (FADH2) is oxidized; the one that gains it (NAD+) is reduced.

Example 4: Classify a cycle step

Problem. For each conversion, give the reaction type, the enzyme class, and the coenzyme (if any): (a) succinate → fumarate; (b) fumarate → malate; (c) α-ketoglutarate → succinyl CoA; (d) succinyl CoA → succinate.

Solution. Look at what changed in the structure. (a) A C–C single bond became a C=C: an oxidation (2 H removed), so the enzyme is an oxidoreductase (succinate dehydrogenase) and the coenzyme is FAD → FADH2. (b) H and OH were added across the C=C: a hydration, no oxidation state change, so a lyase (fumarase) with no coenzyme. (c) A carbon was lost as CO2 and the remaining acid became a thioester: an oxidative decarboxylation by an oxidoreductase (α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase) using NAD+ → NADH and CoA–SH. (d) The thioester is broken and the energy is used to make GTP from GDP + Pi: a ligase (succinyl CoA synthetase), substrate-level phosphorylation.

Check. Count the carbons: (a), (b) and (d) keep 4 C; (c) goes 5 C → 4 C, matching the one CO2 released. Only (a) and (c) make a reduced coenzyme, and only (d) makes a nucleoside triphosphate directly.

Example 5: Predict the effect of cyanide

Problem. Cyanide binds Complex IV. Predict what happens to (a) the ratio NADH/NAD+ in the matrix, (b) the citric acid cycle, (c) ATP production, and (d) oxygen consumption, and (e) whether giving more oxygen alone would fix the problem.

Solution. (a) Electrons can no longer reach O2, so every carrier upstream stays reduced: NADH rises and NAD+ falls. (b) The three NAD+-dependent dehydrogenases run out of NAD+ (and NADH inhibits them allosterically), so the cycle stops. (c) No electron flow means no proton pumping, no gradient, and no ATP from ATP synthase; only glycolysis (2 ATP per glucose) remains. (d) Oxygen consumption falls to nearly zero even though blood oxygen is normal; venous blood stays bright red. (e) No. The problem is not oxygen delivery but the enzyme that uses it, so the antidote must remove the cyanide.

Check. Compare with an uncoupler, which has the opposite effect on oxygen use: oxygen consumption goes up while ATP goes down. A blocker stops flow; an uncoupler wastes it.

Example 6: Carbon balance of one glucose

Problem. Account for all six carbons of glucose as CO2, naming the step that releases each one.

Solution. Glycolysis splits the 6-carbon glucose into two 3-carbon pyruvates and releases no CO2 (6 C still present). Pyruvate dehydrogenase removes one carbon from each pyruvate: 2 CO2, leaving two 2-carbon acetyl groups (4 C). Each turn of the cycle releases 2 CO2 (isocitrate dehydrogenase and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase), and the cycle turns twice: 4 CO2. Total: 2 + 4 = 6 CO2.

Check. The overall equation C6H12O6 + 6 O2 → 6 CO2 + 6 H2O balances. Notice that none of the CO2 is made in electron transport; that stage consumes O2 and makes water.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Counting the cycle once per glucose. It turns twice (2 acetyl CoA).
  • Giving FADH2 the same value as NADH. It is 1.5, not 2.5.
  • Thinking the citric acid cycle uses O2 directly. It does not, but it stops without O2 because NAD+ and FAD are not regenerated.
  • Thinking CO2 is made in electron transport. All 6 CO2 come from pyruvate dehydrogenase and the cycle; electron transport makes H2O.
  • Calling succinate dehydrogenase a lyase because it makes a C=C. It removes two hydrogens, so it is an oxidoreductase (and it is Complex II). The lyase is fumarase, which adds water.
  • Mixing up a blocker and an uncoupler. Cyanide stops electron flow (O2 use falls); DNP lets electrons flow without making ATP (O2 use rises, heat is released).
  • Forgetting that oxaloacetate is regenerated. It is a catalyst, not a reactant that is used up, so the cycle needs only a trace of it to keep turning.
