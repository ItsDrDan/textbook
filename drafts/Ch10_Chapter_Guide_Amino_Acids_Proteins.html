Learning Objectives

  • Draw the general structure of an α-amino acid, identify its chiral center, and tell D from L in a Fischer projection.
  • Classify amino acids by their R groups (nonpolar, aromatic, polar neutral, acidic, basic) and use their three- and one-letter codes.
  • Draw an amino acid as a zwitterion at its isoelectric point and predict its structure and net charge above and below the pI.
  • Form peptide bonds by condensation, break them by hydrolysis, and name peptides from the N-terminus to the C-terminus.
  • Describe the four levels of protein structure and the R-group interactions in tertiary and quaternary structure.
  • Explain how denaturation and hydrolysis differ, and predict which denaturing agents disrupt which interactions.

Key Ideas

1. Amino acids

Proteins are polymers of amino acids. Every one of the 20 common amino acids is built the same way: a central carbon, the α-carbon (alpha carbon), carries four groups: an amino group (–NH2, written –NH3+ at body pH), a carboxyl group (–COOH, written –COO− at body pH), a hydrogen, and a side chain called the R group. Because the amine sits on the carbon right next to the carboxyl carbon, these are α-amino acids. The backbone (N–Cα–C) is identical in all 20; only the R group changes, and the R group decides the amino acid's personality: whether it likes water, whether it carries a charge, and what interactions it can form inside a folded protein.

The table below lists all 20 amino acids sorted by R-group class, with their three-letter and one-letter codes. The nine marked essential cannot be made by the body and must come from the diet (Chapter 15 returns to this). You will not be asked to draw all 20 from memory, but you should be able to look at a side chain and place it in the right class, and you should know the codes.

Two quick ways to sort a side chain you have never seen: if it is only C and H (or C, H and a thioether S), it is nonpolar. If it has an O–H, S–H or amide, it is polar neutral. Tyrosine is the one that straddles classes: its ring is aromatic and hydrophobic, but its phenol O–H hydrogen-bonds, so most GOB books (and this one) file it under polar neutral.

Professor Tip

Learn the one-letter codes that are not the first letter of the name as a set: F (Phe), Y (Tyr), W (Trp), K (Lys), R (Arg), D (Asp), E (Glu), N (Asn), Q (Gln). The rest (G, A, V, L, I, P, M, S, T, C, H) are just the first letter. The Peptide Builder game drills these.

2. Zwitterions and pH

An amino acid contains both an acid (–COOH) and a base (–NH2) in the same molecule, so it reacts with itself: the carboxyl group hands its H+ to the amine. The result is a zwitterion, a molecule that carries a positive charge (–NH3+) and a negative charge (–COO−) at the same time but has a net charge of zero. In solid form and in water near neutral pH, amino acids exist as zwitterions, which is why they behave like salts: they are crystalline solids with high melting points and dissolve in water but not in nonpolar solvents.

  • At physiological pH (about 7.4) an amino acid with a neutral R group is a zwitterion: –NH3+ and –COO−, net charge 0.
  • In strong acid (low pH), the COO− picks up H+ → –COOH. Net charge +1.
  • In strong base (high pH), the NH3+ loses H+ → –NH2. Net charge −1.

Which form you have depends on the pH compared with the amino acid's isoelectric point (pI), the pH at which the zwitterion dominates and the net charge is zero (Key Idea 6 works through this with numbers). Think of the two ends as switches: the carboxyl switch flips from –COOH to –COO− at about pH 2, and the amino switch flips from –NH3+ to –NH2 at about pH 9–10. Between the two flips (which includes blood at pH 7.4) the zwitterion is the main form.

Professor Tip

"Acid adds H+, base takes H+." Low pH: everything that can be protonated is. High pH: everything that can lose an H+ has. Count charges last: +1 for each –NH3+, −1 for each –COO−, and add them up.

3. Peptides

Amino acids link together the same way an amine and a carboxylic acid form an amide (Chapter 7). The carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amino group of the next lose a molecule of water, a condensation reaction, and the C(=O)–NH link that results is an amide bond. Between amino acids it gets a special name: the peptide bond. Two amino acids joined this way make a dipeptide, three a tripeptide, up to about 50 a polypeptide, and longer chains that fold into a working shape are called proteins. Because the peptide bond is an amide, it is planar, it does not rotate freely, and it is stable in water at body temperature; breaking it requires hydrolysis (adding the water back) with strong acid and heat in the lab, or a digestive enzyme such as pepsin or trypsin in the body.

Notice what is left over at the two ends of a peptide. One end still has a free –NH3+: the N-terminus. The other still has a free –COO−: the C-terminus. Everything in between has had both groups used up in peptide bonds, so a peptide with n amino acids contains n − 1 peptide bonds. Each amino acid unit inside a chain is called a residue.

Naming and writing peptides. By convention, peptides are always written and named starting from the N-terminus on the left and ending at the C-terminus on the right. The rules:

  • Every residue except the last (C-terminal) one is named with its ending changed to -yl, the same ending used for acyl groups: glycine → glycyl, alanine → alanyl, serine → seryl, cysteine → cysteinyl, aspartate → aspartyl, glutamine → glutaminyl. Tryptophan becomes tryptophyl; the C-terminal residue keeps its full name.
  • Run the names together as one word: Gly-Ala is glycylalanine; Ala-Gly is alanylglycine, a different compound (different N-terminus, different C-terminus).
  • The three-letter form uses hyphens (Ser-Gly-Val); the one-letter form just strings the letters (SGV). Ser-Gly-Val is seryl-glycyl-valine, written as one word: serylglycylvaline.

Order matters because the two ends are chemically different, so Gly-Ala and Ala-Gly are constitutional isomers. With three different amino acids there are 3 × 2 × 1 = 6 possible tripeptides, and a protein of 100 residues has 20100 possible sequences, which is why primary structure carries so much information.

4. Levels of protein structure

A protein is not a floppy string. It folds into a specific shape, and that shape is what lets hemoglobin carry oxygen or an enzyme catalyze one reaction. Biochemists describe the fold in four levels, each building on the one before. The table summarizes them and the diagram that follows shows how they connect.

LevelWhat it isHeld together by
PrimaryAmino acid sequencePeptide bonds
Secondaryα-helix, β-pleated sheetHydrogen bonds between backbone C=O and N–H
TertiaryOverall 3-D fold of one chainR-group interactions: hydrophobic, hydrogen bonds, salt bridges (+/−), disulfide bonds (Cys–S–S–Cys)
QuaternaryTwo or more chains together (hemoglobin has 4)The same R-group interactions
  • Primary structure is the sequence of residues, written N-terminus to C-terminus, and it is covalent. Change one residue and you may change everything downstream: sickle cell hemoglobin differs from normal hemoglobin at one position out of 146 in the β chain.
  • Secondary structure is the regular local shape the backbone adopts. In an α-helix the chain coils like a spiral staircase, with each backbone C=O hydrogen-bonded to the N–H four residues further along; the R groups point outward. In a β-pleated sheet stretches of chain lie side by side, zig-zagging, with hydrogen bonds between the C=O of one strand and the N–H of the neighbor. Both are held by backbone hydrogen bonds; the R groups are not involved. Keratin in hair is mostly α-helix; silk is mostly β-sheet; most globular proteins contain both. A third type of secondary structure, the triple helix, is found in collagen, where three chains wind around each other.
  • Tertiary structure is the complete three-dimensional fold of one chain, and this is where the R groups take over. Four interactions hold it: (1) hydrophobic interactions: nonpolar side chains (Leu, Val, Ile, Phe) cluster in the interior away from water; (2) hydrogen bonds between polar side chains (Ser, Thr, Asn, Gln, Tyr) or between a side chain and water on the surface; (3) salt bridges, ionic attractions between a − side chain (Asp, Glu) and a + side chain (Lys, Arg); and (4) disulfide bonds, covalent –S–S– links formed when two cysteine –SH groups are oxidized (Chapter 4). The disulfide bond is the only covalent one of the four, so it is the strongest and the hardest to undo.
  • Quaternary structure exists only in proteins made of two or more separate chains (subunits) held together by the same R-group interactions. Hemoglobin is the classic case: two α and two β subunits, each cradling a heme group. Insulin, with two chains linked by disulfide bonds, is another. A single-chain protein such as myoglobin has no quaternary structure.

Proteins are also sorted by overall shape. Fibrous proteins are long, tough and water-insoluble; they are mostly one kind of secondary structure repeated, and they build things: collagen (triple helix; skin, tendon, bone, dentin, gingiva), keratin (α-helix; hair, nails, outer skin), and fibroin (β-sheet; silk). Globular proteins fold into compact, roughly spherical shapes with the hydrophobic residues inside and polar ones outside, so they dissolve in water and do chemistry: enzymes, hemoglobin, myoglobin, albumin, antibodies, and most hormones such as insulin.

ClassShape and solubilityMain structureExamplesJob
FibrousLong strands or sheets; insoluble in waterOne repeated secondary structureCollagen, keratin, fibroin, elastinStructure and protection
GlobularCompact, spherical; soluble in waterMixed helix and sheet folded into a tertiary (often quaternary) shapeEnzymes, hemoglobin, myoglobin, albumin, antibodies, insulinCatalysis, transport, defense, signaling

Denaturation (heat, strong acid or base, alcohol, heavy-metal ions, agitation) disrupts secondary, tertiary, and quaternary structure, and function is lost. Peptide bonds survive. Only hydrolysis breaks the primary structure. Key Idea 7 looks at each denaturing agent in turn.

5. Abbreviations and stereochemistry

  • Amino acids have 3-letter and 1-letter codes. Most are obvious (Gly G, Ala A, Val V, Leu L, Ser S), but learn the tricky ones: Phe F, Tyr Y, Trp W, Lys K, Arg R, Asp D, Glu E, Asn N, Gln Q.
  • Every amino acid except glycine has a chiral α-carbon: it carries four different groups (–NH3+, –COO−, –H and R). Glycine's R group is a second H, so it is achiral and has no D or L form.
  • Draw the Fischer projection the same way you did for sugars in Chapter 8: the carbon chain runs vertically with the most oxidized carbon (the carboxylate) on top, so the R group ends up at the bottom and the horizontal bonds (H and NH3+) point toward you. Then read the amino group: on the left means L; on the right means D. The amino acids in human proteins are L, and the ribosome only uses L amino acids. D-amino acids occur mainly in bacterial cell walls.

6. Isoelectric point (pI)

The pI is the pH at which an amino acid's net charge is zero (pure zwitterion). It sits halfway between the pH at which the carboxyl group gives up its H+ (about 2) and the pH at which the ammonium group gives up its H+ (about 9–10), which is why neutral amino acids have pI values near 6. Three rules cover every exam question about pI:

  • pH below pI → the solution is more acidic than the zwitterion "wants," so an extra H+ sits on the carboxyl group: –COOH and –NH3+, net positive (+1 for a neutral R group).
  • pH at pI → zwitterion, –COO− and –NH3+, net zero.
  • pH above pI → H+ has been removed from the ammonium group: –COO− and –NH2, net negative (−1 for a neutral R group).

A charged side chain moves the pI. Aspartate and glutamate carry a second carboxyl group that is also deprotonated at neutral pH, so they reach net zero only in quite acidic solution: pI ≈ 2.8 for Asp and 3.2 for Glu. At pH 7.4 both are well above their pI and carry a net charge of −1 (two –COO− and one –NH3+). Lysine and arginine carry a second basic nitrogen that stays protonated until the pH is high, so their pI values are about 9.7 and 10.8; at pH 7.4 they are below their pI and net +1. Histidine's imidazole ring is a weaker base, giving pI ≈ 7.6, so histidine is close to neutral in blood and is the side chain that does most of a protein's buffering.

Electrophoresis. Because charge depends on pH, a mixture of amino acids (or proteins) can be separated by placing it in a buffer of known pH on a gel between two electrodes. Anything with a net negative charge migrates toward the positive electrode, anything positive toward the negative electrode, and a molecule sitting at its pI does not move. At pH 6.0, for example, glutamate (net −) moves toward the positive electrode, lysine (net +) toward the negative electrode, and alanine (at its pI) stays put. Clinical labs use the same principle to separate hemoglobin variants such as sickle hemoglobin (HbS), which has lost a negative charge (Glu → Val) and therefore migrates more slowly toward the positive electrode than normal HbA, and to separate serum proteins into albumin and globulin fractions. A protein is least soluble at its pI, because with no net charge the molecules no longer repel one another and clump; that is how casein is precipitated from milk in the lab by adding acid to pH 4.6.

7. Denaturation and hydrolysis

Protein function depends on shape, and shape depends on a web of weak interactions. Denaturation is the loss of secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure, and therefore of biological activity, without breaking peptide bonds. The chain unfolds, buried hydrophobic groups come to the surface, and unfolded chains tangle and precipitate: a cooked egg white turns from a clear solution of albumin into a white solid. Denaturation can be reversible for small proteins if the agent is removed gently, but for most proteins (and for anything cooked) it is permanent. The table below pairs each denaturing agent with the interactions it attacks; knowing which lets you predict what it will do.

Hydrolysis is a different process. It is the reverse of peptide-bond formation: water is added across the amide link, with strong acid and hours of heating in the lab or with protease enzymes in the body (pepsin in the stomach, trypsin and chymotrypsin from the pancreas). Hydrolysis destroys the primary structure and releases free amino acids, which is exactly what digestion is for: dietary protein is hydrolyzed to amino acids, absorbed, and reassembled into the body's own proteins. It is also why a protein drug taken by mouth stops being a drug.

Misfolding. A protein can also lose function by folding into the wrong stable shape. Prion diseases such as Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease are caused by a misfolded protein that converts normal copies into the same misfolded, β-sheet-rich, aggregated form, and these aggregates survive the heat of ordinary sterilization.

Professor Tip

To predict what a denaturing agent does, ask one question: which interactions does it touch? Then list the levels those interactions hold. Reducing agents touch only disulfide bonds, so they unfold keratin (lots of Cys) but barely affect a protein with no cysteine. Nothing on the denaturation list breaks peptide bonds, so the amino acid sequence is always intact afterward.

Health Career Connection

  • Dental hygiene – collagen and gum health: Collagen, a triple-helix protein, makes up the periodontal ligament, gingival connective tissue, and the organic matrix of dentin and bone. Vitamin C is needed by the enzyme that converts proline residues in collagen to hydroxyproline so the three chains can hydrogen-bond into a stable triple helix. Deficiency (scurvy) shows up as bleeding, swollen gums and loose teeth.
  • Nursing – sickle cell disease: A single change in hemoglobin's primary structure (Glu → Val at position 6 of the β chain) puts a hydrophobic R group on the surface. The hemoglobin molecules stick together, and red cells sickle.
  • Nursing – disinfection and sterilization: Alcohol wipes, autoclaves (heat), and heavy-metal antiseptics kill microbes by denaturing their proteins.
  • Nursing – insulin: Insulin is a small two-chain protein held together by disulfide bonds. It is injected, not swallowed, because stomach enzymes would hydrolyze its peptide bonds.
  • Dental hygiene – the pellicle: Within minutes of cleaning, salivary proteins adsorb onto enamel to form a thin protein film, the acquired pellicle. It protects against acid, but it is also the surface plaque bacteria attach to. Tea and coffee stains bind to it, and whitening pastes are rated by how well they remove it.

Further reading: M. Epple and J. Enax, "The Chemistry of Dental Care," ChemistryViews (Part 1, Part 2).

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Albumin, the most abundant globular protein in plasma (normal about 3.5–5.0 g/dL), is too large to leave the capillaries, so it holds water in the blood by oncotic pressure (the osmotic pull of plasma proteins). In liver disease, malnutrition or kidney loss, hypoalbuminemia (low albumin) lets fluid leak into tissues: edema. The urine dipstick protein pad detects mainly albumin; a positive result, proteinuria, means the kidney filter is leaking protein that should have stayed in the blood. Insulin is a 51-residue peptide hormone: an A chain (21 residues) and a B chain (30) linked by two disulfide bonds, with a third inside the A chain. Swallowed insulin is just protein to the stomach; pepsin and trypsin hydrolyze it to amino acids, so it must be injected. In phenylketonuria (PKU) the enzyme that converts phenylalanine to tyrosine is missing, phenylalanine builds up and damages the developing brain, so newborns are screened and the diet (and aspartame, a phenylalanine-containing dipeptide ester) is restricted. Denaturation is why an autoclave or an alcohol swab kills microbes and why a high fever is dangerous (enzymes unfold above about 41 °C); Chapter 11 shows how pH and temperature change enzyme activity. Chapter 15 picks up nitrogen balance: protein intake versus the nitrogen lost as urea, negative after surgery, burns or starvation.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Most of what a hygienist touches is protein. Type I collagen is the main protein of gingival connective tissue, the periodontal ligament (the fibers anchoring the tooth to bone) and the organic matrix of dentin and cementum. Enamel is about 96% hydroxyapatite mineral with almost no protein left after it matures, which is why it cannot repair itself; dentin is about 70% mineral and 20% collagen by weight, so it is tougher but softer, and in periodontitis (inflammatory loss of the tooth's supporting tissues) bacterial and host enzymes hydrolyze the collagen of the ligament and gum. Saliva is a protein solution: mucins (glycoproteins) lubricate and coat the mucosa; salivary amylase starts starch digestion; lysozyme, an enzyme, hydrolyzes the polysaccharide of bacterial cell walls; lactoferrin binds iron that bacteria need; and statherin keeps saliva's calcium and phosphate from precipitating, so the fluid stays supersaturated and ready to remineralize enamel. Within minutes of a prophylaxis, these proteins adsorb to the enamel as the acquired pellicle, the film that plaque biofilm attaches to. Chlorhexidine, a positively charged antiseptic, binds to the negatively charged proteins of the pellicle and mucosa and is released over hours, a property called substantivity. Finally, hair and nails get their toughness from keratin's many disulfide bonds; enamel has none, because its strength is mineral, not protein.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Alanine at three pH values

Problem. Draw alanine (R = CH3) at pH 1, pH 7.4, and pH 12.

Solution.

  • pH 1: +H3N–CH(CH3)–COOH (net +1)
  • pH 7.4: +H3N–CH(CH3)–COO− (zwitterion, net 0)
  • pH 12: H2N–CH(CH3)–COO− (net −1)

Check. The carboxyl group loses its H+ first (around pH 2) and the ammonium group last (around pH 9–10), so the charge steps down +1 → 0 → −1 as the pH rises.

Example 2: Identify the tertiary interaction

Problem. What interaction forms between the side chains of (a) Glu and Arg, (b) Phe and Val, (c) Ser and Asn?

Solution. (a) Glu is − and Arg is +: salt bridge. (b) Both nonpolar: hydrophobic interaction. (c) Both polar with O–H or N–H: hydrogen bond.

Check. Classify each side chain first (acidic, basic, nonpolar, polar neutral); the pair of classes names the interaction. Only two Cys residues would give the fourth type, a disulfide bond.

Example 3: Reading a peptide

Problem. For the tripeptide Ser-Gly-Val, which amino acid is the N-terminus? How many peptide bonds are there?

Solution. Serine is written first, so it is the N-terminus. Valine is the C-terminus. Three amino acids are joined by 2 peptide bonds.

Check. A peptide of n residues has n − 1 peptide bonds; 3 − 1 = 2.

Example 4: Inside or outside?

Problem. Would the segment Ile-Phe-Leu more likely be found on the surface or in the interior of a protein in water?

Solution. All three have nonpolar R groups, so the segment is likely buried in the interior, away from water.

Check. A segment such as Asp-Lys-Ser, with charged and polar side chains, would be the opposite: on the surface, hydrogen-bonded to water.

Example 5: Charge of alanine relative to its pI

Problem. Alanine has pI ≈ 6.0. Draw its predominant form and give its net charge at pH 2, pH 6 and pH 10. In an electrophoresis gel buffered at each pH, which electrode would it move toward?

Solution. Compare each pH with the pI.

  • pH 2 (below pI): the solution is acidic enough to keep H+ on the carboxyl group. Form: +H3N–CH(CH3)–COOH. Charges: +1 and 0, net +1. It migrates toward the negative electrode.
  • pH 6 (at pI): zwitterion, +H3N–CH(CH3)–COO−. Charges +1 and −1, net 0. It does not move.
  • pH 10 (above pI): base has removed the H+ from the ammonium group. Form: H2N–CH(CH3)–COO−. Charges 0 and −1, net −1. It migrates toward the positive electrode.

Check. Charge should fall as pH rises (+1 → 0 → −1), and the only form with both charges is the one at the pI. If you had put glutamate (pI 3.2) in the pH 6 gel instead, pH 6 would be above its pI, so it would carry a net negative charge and move toward the positive electrode while alanine stayed put. That difference is how electrophoresis separates them.

Example 6: Name a tripeptide and find its hydrolysis products

Problem. A tripeptide has the sequence Ala-Cys-Gly. (a) Which residue is the N-terminus and which is the C-terminus? (b) Give its full name and its one-letter form. (c) What products form when it is completely hydrolyzed?

Solution.

(a) Peptides are written from the N-terminus, so alanine (free –NH3+) is the N-terminus and glycine (free –COO−) is the C-terminus. Cysteine in the middle has used both of its groups in the two peptide bonds.

(b) Change the ending of every residue except the last to -yl: alanine → alanyl, cysteine → cysteinyl, glycine stays glycine. Run them together: alanylcysteinylglycine. One-letter form: A, C, G → ACG.

(c) Complete hydrolysis adds one water across each of the two peptide bonds and returns the three free amino acids as zwitterions: alanine + cysteine + glycine. No new compounds form; the residues simply get their –NH3+ and –COO− groups back.

Check. Three residues, two peptide bonds, two waters consumed in hydrolysis. Reversing the letters (GCA, glycylcysteinylalanine) would be a different tripeptide with glycine at the N-terminus, so the order in the name must match the order in the sequence.

Example 7: What holds this fold together?

Problem. A small water-soluble enzyme has Cys at positions 14 and 72, Asp at 30, Lys at 95, Thr at 41, Gln at 58, and a cluster of Leu, Ile and Val at positions 20–25 and 80–85. Identify the interaction each group of residues is most likely to form and the level of structure it stabilizes.

Solution. Classify each side chain, then pair up partners.

  • Cys 14 and Cys 72: two thiols can be oxidized to a disulfide bond, a covalent cross-link that pulls two distant parts of the chain together.
  • Asp 30 (−) and Lys 95 (+): a salt bridge (ionic attraction).
  • Thr 41 (–OH) and Gln 58 (amide): a hydrogen bond between polar side chains.
  • The Leu/Ile/Val clusters: hydrophobic interactions; these nonpolar stretches pack together in the interior, away from water.

All four are side-chain (R-group) interactions, so they stabilize tertiary structure. The backbone hydrogen bonds that make the helices and sheets are secondary structure and are not listed here.

Check. Partners that are far apart in the sequence (14 and 72; 30 and 95) can still touch once the chain folds, which is the whole point of tertiary structure. Only the disulfide bond is covalent; it will survive heat and alcohol but not a reducing agent.

Example 8: Predict the effect of a denaturing agent

Problem. A 70% isopropyl alcohol swab is wiped across skin carrying bacteria. (a) Which interactions in the bacterial enzymes does the alcohol disrupt, and which levels of structure are lost? (b) Are the enzymes' amino acid sequences changed? (c) Would the swab have the same effect on a protein with no cysteine as a hair-relaxer reducing agent would?

Solution.

(a) Alcohol is a small polar molecule that hydrogen-bonds and also dissolves nonpolar groups. It disrupts the hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions that hold the fold, so secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure are lost. The enzymes unfold and stop working, and the cell dies.

(b) No. Alcohol does not hydrolyze amide bonds, so the primary structure is intact; the protein is denatured, not digested.

(c) Not at all. A reducing agent such as thioglycolate attacks only disulfide bonds. On a cysteine-free protein it would do almost nothing, whereas alcohol attacks hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions that every protein has.

Check. Match agent to interaction: heat, alcohol and agitation hit hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic packing; acids, bases and heavy metals hit salt bridges (and metals hit disulfides); reducing agents hit disulfides only. Nothing on that list touches peptide bonds.

Example 9: Classify from the structure

Problem. Three amino acids have the side chains (a) –CH2CH2COO−, (b) –CH2OH and (c) –CH2CH(CH3)2. Name each, give its three- and one-letter codes, classify its R group, and state its net charge at pH 7.4.

Solution.

  • (a) A second carboxylate two carbons from the α-carbon: glutamate, Glu, E; acidic. At pH 7.4: –NH3+ (+1) and two –COO− (−2), net −1.
  • (b) A hydroxyl group: serine, Ser, S; polar neutral. Net 0 (zwitterion; the OH is uncharged).
  • (c) A branched hydrocarbon: leucine, Leu, L; nonpolar (and essential). Net 0.

Check. Only acidic and basic side chains change the net charge at pH 7.4. If (a) had been one carbon shorter (–CH2COO−) it would be aspartate, Asp, D, with the same classification and charge.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Drawing amino acids in the body as neutral –NH2/–COOH. At pH 7.4 they are zwitterions.
  • Writing peptides from the C-terminus. Always start with the N-terminus.
  • Saying denaturation breaks peptide bonds. It does not; hydrolysis does.
  • Mixing up secondary (backbone hydrogen bonds) and tertiary (R-group interactions) structure.
  • Assuming the pI is 7. For most amino acids it is near 6, and a charged side chain pushes it to about 3 (acidic) or 10 (basic). Always compare the pH with the actual pI.
  • Reading "L" as "levorotatory" or as "left-handed." L and D come from the Fischer projection (amino group on the left = L) and say nothing about which way the compound rotates light.
  • Calling a disulfide bond a hydrogen bond. It is a covalent S–S bond formed by oxidizing two cysteine thiols, and it is the only tertiary interaction a reducing agent can break.
  • Treating "essential" as "most important." An essential amino acid is one the body cannot synthesize and must get from food.

Practice

Exercise 1

Classify Lys, Leu, Asp, and Thr.

Answer
Lys: basic. Leu: nonpolar. Asp: acidic. Thr: polar neutral.

Exercise 2

What is the net charge of glycine at pH 1?

Answer
+1 (–NH3+ and –COOH)

Exercise 3

Which level of structure is lost when a protein is hydrolyzed but not when it is merely denatured?

Answer
Primary structure (the peptide bonds)

Exercise 4

A hair "perm" breaks and then re-forms one type of bond to reshape hair protein (keratin). Which one?

Answer
Disulfide bonds between cysteine side chains

Exercise 5

The pI of lysine is about 9.7. Is lysine positive, negative, or neutral overall at pH 7.4?

Answer
Positive. The pH is below its pI, so it carries a net positive charge.

Exercise 6

Name the tripeptide Val-Ser-Lys and write it in one-letter form.

Answer
Valylseryllysine (valyl-seryl-lysine); VSK. Valine is the N-terminus and lysine the C-terminus.

Exercise 7

In a Fischer projection of serine with the COO− at the top, the NH3+ is on the right. Is this the form found in human proteins?

Answer
No. Amino group on the right is D-serine; proteins are built from L-amino acids (amino group on the left).

Exercise 8

Collagen, hemoglobin, keratin, and an enzyme: which are fibrous and which are globular?

Answer
Fibrous: collagen and keratin (structural, insoluble). Globular: hemoglobin and the enzyme (compact, water-soluble, functional).
