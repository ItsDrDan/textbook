Learning Objectives

  • Explain how enzymes speed reactions by lowering activation energy without changing ΔG, using the lock-and-key and induced-fit models.
  • Read an enzyme's name (substrate + reaction + -ase) and classify it into one of the six main classes from the reaction it catalyzes, including reactions you will meet in metabolism.
  • Read and sketch graphs of enzyme activity vs. temperature, pH, substrate concentration, and enzyme concentration.
  • Distinguish competitive, noncompetitive, and irreversible inhibition and give a drug example of each.
  • Describe the four ways cells regulate enzymes: allosteric control, feedback inhibition, zymogen activation and covalent modification, and genetic control.
  • Connect water-soluble vitamins to their coenzymes, know the main roles of vitamins A, D, E, and K, and match each deficiency disease to its vitamin.

Key Ideas

1. What enzymes do

Review from CHEM 3A: a catalyst speeds up a reaction without being used up, and it does so by providing a different route with a lower activation energy (Ea), the energy hill between reactants and products. An enzyme is a biological catalyst, almost always a protein. At body temperature most reactions of metabolism would take years without one; with one they take milliseconds.

  • Enzymes lower Ea by binding the reactant, called the substrate, in a pocket called the active site and holding it in exactly the right position and shape for the reaction. The enzyme–substrate complex (ES) then releases product and the enzyme is ready to go again.
  • Enzymes do not change the overall energy of the reaction (ΔG or ΔH) and do not change the position of equilibrium. They change only how fast equilibrium is reached. A reaction that is uphill in energy stays uphill; the enzyme just makes it happen faster in both directions.
  • Enzymes are not consumed. One molecule of carbonic anhydrase can hydrate about a million CO2 molecules per second, over and over.

Specificity. A platinum catalyst in a car's exhaust system will speed up almost any oxidation. An enzyme is choosy. Its active site is a three-dimensional pocket lined with specific R groups, so only a substrate with the right shape, size, and pattern of charges and hydrogen-bonding groups can bind. Degrees of specificity you should recognize:

  • Absolute specificity: one substrate only. Urease hydrolyzes urea and nothing else.
  • Group specificity: one type of functional group or bond. Hexokinase phosphorylates several hexoses (glucose, fructose, mannose); trypsin cuts a peptide bond only after lysine or arginine.
  • Stereospecificity: enzymes distinguish enantiomers. Your enzymes digest D-glucose and build proteins from L-amino acids; the mirror images are ignored. (This is why chirality, Chapter 5, matters in biochemistry and in drug design.)

Two models of how the substrate binds. In the lock-and-key model (Emil Fischer, 1894) the active site is a rigid shape and the substrate fits it like a key in a lock. In the induced-fit model (Daniel Koshland, 1958) the active site is a close but imperfect match, and binding the substrate makes the enzyme shift its shape to wrap around it. The induced-fit model is the better description: the shape change is what squeezes the substrate into its transition-state geometry, and it explains how one enzyme can accept several related substrates.

Naming enzymes. Almost every enzyme name ends in -ase, and the rest of the name tells you the substrate, the reaction, or both:

  • Substrate + -ase: lactase acts on lactose, lipase on lipids, urease on urea, sucrase on sucrose, protease on proteins.
  • Substrate + reaction + -ase: lactate dehydrogenase removes hydrogen from lactate; pyruvate decarboxylase removes CO2 from pyruvate; glucose-6-phosphate isomerase rearranges glucose-6-phosphate.
  • A few old names do not follow the rule: pepsin, trypsin, chymotrypsin (digestive proteases) and lysozyme. Learn these as exceptions.

Professor Tip

Read the enzyme's name like a sentence. "Glucose-6-phosphate isomerase" rearranges glucose-6-phosphate. "Lactate dehydrogenase" removes hydrogen from lactate. The name tells you the class and the substrate, which makes the metabolism chapters much easier. When you have only the equation, ask three questions in order: Is NAD+/FAD involved (oxidoreductase)? Is water a reactant that splits a bond (hydrolase) or ATP a reactant that joins two molecules (ligase)? Does a group move between two molecules (transferase), does one molecule split or gain CO2/H2O at a double bond (lyase), or does one molecule simply rearrange (isomerase)?

3. What affects enzyme activity

Enzyme activity means the rate of the reaction it catalyzes, usually measured as product formed per minute. Four variables control it, and you will test all four in Experiment 13.

  • Temperature: as temperature rises, molecules collide more often and harder, so the rate rises, reaching a maximum at the optimum temperature (about 37 °C for human enzymes). Above the optimum, heat breaks the hydrogen bonds and other weak interactions that hold the tertiary structure, the active site loses its shape, and the rate falls steeply: the enzyme is denatured (Chapter 10). Cooling does not denature; it just slows the enzyme, and activity returns on warming.
  • pH: each enzyme has an optimum pH at which the acidic and basic R groups in and around the active site carry the charges needed to bind the substrate. Pepsin (stomach) works best near pH 2; trypsin (small intestine) near pH 8; most enzymes inside cells near pH 7.4. Far from the optimum, the charges change (–COO− becomes –COOH, –NH3+ becomes –NH2), the substrate no longer fits, and large pH changes denature the protein.
  • Substrate concentration: at fixed enzyme concentration, the rate rises quickly as substrate is added, then levels off at a maximum rate when every active site is occupied, a condition called saturation. Beyond that, extra substrate has nowhere to bind.
  • Enzyme concentration: with substrate in excess, the rate is directly proportional to the amount of enzyme: twice the enzyme, twice the rate. This is the basis of clinical enzyme assays, where the measured rate in a blood sample tells you how much enzyme leaked out of damaged cells.

4. Inhibition

An inhibitor is a molecule that lowers an enzyme's activity. Many drugs, poisons, and the body's own regulators are enzyme inhibitors, so the three types are worth knowing cold.

  • Competitive: the inhibitor resembles the substrate and binds the active site, so enzyme molecules that are holding inhibitor cannot hold substrate. It is reversible, and it can be overcome by adding more substrate, which out-competes the inhibitor; the maximum rate is eventually reached, it just takes more substrate to get there.
  • Noncompetitive: the inhibitor binds somewhere other than the active site and changes the enzyme's shape so the active site no longer works well. More substrate does not help, because the substrate and inhibitor are not competing for the same spot; the maximum rate is lowered. Many heavy-metal ions (Pb2+, Hg2+) act this way by binding to –SH groups of cysteine.
  • Irreversible: the inhibitor forms a covalent bond with the enzyme, usually with an R group in the active site, and permanently inactivates it. The cell must make new enzyme to recover.

5. Cofactors and vitamins

Many enzymes are not complete as a bare protein. They need a non-protein helper called a cofactor. If the cofactor is an organic molecule it is called a coenzyme. Protein + cofactor = the active enzyme.

  • Metal-ion cofactors are bound by R groups (His, Cys, Asp, Glu) and help hold the substrate or carry charge. Zn2+ sits in carbonic anhydrase, alcohol dehydrogenase, and the matrix metalloproteinases of gum tissue; Mg2+ pairs with ATP in every kinase; Fe2+/Fe3+ in heme carries electrons in catalase and the cytochromes of the electron transport chain; Cu2+ in cytochrome oxidase; Mn2+, Se, and Mo in a few others.
  • Coenzymes are made from water-soluble vitamins, mostly the B group. A vitamin is an organic molecule you need in small amounts and cannot make, so it must come from the diet. The coenzyme is usually a slightly built-up version of the vitamin, and it does the chemistry the protein's twenty amino acids cannot: carrying hydrogen, electrons, acyl groups, CO2, amino groups, or one-carbon units from one reaction to the next.
  • Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) are not coenzymes. They are nonpolar lipids (Chapter 9) that are absorbed with dietary fat, carried in lipoproteins, and stored in the liver and adipose tissue, which is why an overdose can build up to toxic levels. Water-soluble vitamins are not stored much; any excess leaves in the urine, so they must be eaten regularly.

6. Specificity and regulation

A cell cannot let every enzyme run at full speed all the time. It needs fast switches (seconds) and slow ones (hours), and it uses four strategies. Sections 11.6 and 11.7 of the book treat these in more depth; here is the map.

  • Specificity: some enzymes act on one substrate only (absolute), others on a type of bond or group (for example, any peptide bond next to a particular amino acid), and most can tell D from L (see Key Idea 1).
  • Allosteric control: an allosteric enzyme has, besides its active site, one or more regulatory sites (allos = other). A regulator molecule binds there and changes the enzyme's shape. A positive regulator (activator) shifts the enzyme into its active shape; a negative regulator (inhibitor) shifts it into the inactive shape, the same idea as noncompetitive inhibition. The response is fast and reversible: the regulator leaves and the enzyme snaps back. Example: ATP, the cell's energy currency, is a negative regulator of phosphofructokinase in glycolysis, so a cell with plenty of ATP slows glucose breakdown.
  • Feedback control (feedback inhibition): the end product of a pathway is a negative allosteric regulator of the first enzyme unique to that pathway. When the product piles up, the whole pathway shuts off at its first step, so no intermediates are wasted; when the product is used up, the pathway restarts. This is how bacteria stop making an amino acid once they have enough of it; your own cells use the same logic in nucleotide and amino acid synthesis.
  • Zymogens (proenzymes): an enzyme that would be dangerous inside the cell that makes it is built as an inactive precursor with an extra piece of chain blocking the active site. Cutting that piece off (a hydrolysis) switches it on, permanently. Pepsinogen is made by stomach cells and becomes pepsin only in the acid of the stomach; trypsinogen from the pancreas becomes trypsin in the small intestine, and trypsin then activates chymotrypsinogen and the other pancreatic proenzymes. The clotting cascade is a chain of proenzymes (prothrombin → thrombin, for example) that lets blood stay liquid until a vessel is cut.
  • Covalent modification: a reversible switch in which a small group, nearly always a phosphate, is attached to a serine, threonine, or tyrosine –OH of the enzyme by a protein kinase (using ATP) and removed by a phosphatase (by hydrolysis). The phosphate's 2− charge changes the enzyme's shape and so its activity. Hormones use this switch: epinephrine and glucagon turn on a kinase that phosphorylates glycogen phosphorylase (ON: break glycogen down) and glycogen synthase (OFF: stop building it); insulin reverses both. You will follow this story in Chapter 13 and in Section 11.7.
  • Genetic control: the slowest switch. A cell changes how much of an enzyme it makes by turning the gene on (induction) or off (repression). Lactase in the intestine is an example of an enzyme whose level drops after childhood in most of the world's population (lactose intolerance); the liver's cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes are induced by many drugs and by alcohol, which changes how fast other drugs are cleared. This takes hours to days because new mRNA and protein must be made (Chapter 12).

7. Enzymes in the clinic: diagnosis, drug targets, and infection control

Two ideas from this chapter run through every clinical setting you will work in. First, enzymes stay inside healthy cells, so an enzyme in the blood is a sign of damaged cells, and which enzyme tells you which tissue. Second, because enzymes are so specific, a drug that fits one active site can shut down one pathway without touching the rest of the body. Clinical Connection: Nursing

Temperature and the patient. A fever of 39 °C does not denature human enzymes; above about 41–42 °C (hyperthermia, as in heat stroke) they begin to unfold, which is why rapid cooling is an emergency. The same chemistry works in reverse: a donor kidney or heart is packed in ice-cold solution because near 4 °C its enzymes run so slowly that the organ uses little oxygen and lasts hours instead of minutes.

Vitamins on the ward. Patients with alcohol use disorder are often thiamine-deficient (poor diet, poor absorption), and giving IV glucose without thiamine can precipitate Wernicke encephalopathy (confusion, unsteady gait, eye-movement problems), because pyruvate cannot be converted to acetyl-CoA without TPP; thiamine is given first. Vitamin B12 needs intrinsic factor from the stomach to be absorbed, so pernicious anemia (autoimmune loss of intrinsic factor), gastric surgery, long-term metformin, and a strict vegan diet (no animal foods) all lead to deficiency and are treated with injections or supplements. Folate before and during early pregnancy prevents neural-tube defects such as spina bifida, since the embryo's rapidly dividing cells need THF to make DNA.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Enzymes in the mouth. Saliva carries salivary amylase (starch → maltose), lysozyme (hydrolyzes bacterial cell walls), and peroxidases. Plaque bacteria have their own enzymes: Streptococcus mutans uses glucosyltransferase, a transferase, to build sticky glucan chains from sucrose that glue the plaque biofilm to enamel, which is one reason sucrose is the most cariogenic (cavity-causing) sugar. In periodontitis, bacterial collagenases and proteases, together with the host's own matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs, zinc-dependent hydrolases), break down collagen in the gingiva and periodontal ligament. Sub-antimicrobial-dose doxycycline is prescribed not to kill bacteria but to inhibit MMPs by binding their Zn2+, slowing attachment loss. Fluoride, besides promoting remineralization, inhibits enolase in bacterial glycolysis, so plaque makes less acid.

Chemistry of the operatory. Chlorhexidine rinse denatures bacterial proteins and membranes. The hydrogen peroxide in bleaching gel is decomposed to water and oxygen by salivary peroxidase and tissue catalase, which is why gel must be isolated from saliva and soft tissue. Bleeding gums on probing can signal vitamin C deficiency (weak collagen), and vitamin D is needed for the mineralization of developing teeth. Sterilization works by the same principle as a fever gone too far: the autoclave's pressurized steam at 121 °C denatures every microbial enzyme, which no cold disinfectant can guarantee.

Health Career Connection

  • Dental hygiene – salivary amylase: Starch digestion begins in the mouth, where amylase hydrolyzes α(1→4) links. This is why starchy snacks that linger on teeth can still feed acid-producing bacteria.
  • Dental hygiene – how fluoride works: Fluoride does not act mainly on enzymes. Its main anticavity effect is catalytic: it speeds up the regrowth (remineralization) of calcium phosphate crystals in softened enamel. In sodium monofluorophosphate toothpastes, the F− is released by hydrolysis of a P–F bond, which can be chemical or enzyme-catalyzed.
  • Nursing – diagnostic enzymes: Damaged cells leak enzymes into the blood. High troponin and CK-MB point to heart damage, and ALT/AST to liver damage.
  • Nursing – drugs as inhibitors: ACE inhibitors (lisinopril) are competitive inhibitors that lower blood pressure. Penicillin irreversibly inhibits the bacterial enzyme that builds cell walls. Warfarin blocks vitamin K recycling, which slows clotting, so patients on it need monitoring before dental procedures.
  • Dental hygiene – saliva's enzymes: Saliva contains lysozyme, a hydrolase that breaks the polysaccharide backbone of bacterial cell walls, and lactoferrin, an iron-binding protein that limits bacterial growth. Both are part of the mouth's natural defense against plaque.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Classify the enzyme

Problem. Classify the enzyme for each reaction: (a) lactate + NAD+ → pyruvate + NADH + H+; (b) maltose + H2O → 2 glucose; (c) glucose + ATP → glucose-6-phosphate + ADP.

Solution. (a) H is removed and NAD+ is reduced: oxidoreductase (lactate dehydrogenase). (b) Water splits a bond: hydrolase (maltase). (c) A phosphate moves from ATP to glucose: transferase (hexokinase).

Check. In (c) ATP is used but two molecules do not become one (glucose-6-phosphate and ADP are both products), so it is a transferase, not a ligase.

Example 2: Type of inhibition

Problem. An inhibitor slows an enzyme, but adding a large excess of substrate restores the normal maximum rate. What type of inhibition is this?

Solution. Competitive. Substrate can out-compete the inhibitor for the active site.

Check. If the inhibition were noncompetitive or irreversible, the maximum rate would stay lowered no matter how much substrate was added.

Example 3: Reading the temperature effect

Problem. A human enzyme is assayed at 37 °C and then at 65 °C. Predict the activity at 65 °C and explain.

Solution. Activity drops sharply. Above its optimum the enzyme denatures, losing the tertiary structure that forms the active site.

Check. Contrast with 10 °C: the enzyme would be slow but intact, and activity would return at 37 °C. Heat damage does not reverse.

Example 4: Classifying reactions you will meet in metabolism

Problem. Each reaction below appears in Chapters 13–15. Classify the enzyme and explain the clue you used.

  1. glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate + NAD+ + Pi → 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate + NADH + H+
  2. fructose-1,6-bisphosphate → dihydroxyacetone phosphate + glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate
  3. 3-phosphoglycerate → 2-phosphoglycerate
  4. fumarate + H2O → malate
  5. glutamate + oxaloacetate → α-ketoglutarate + aspartate
  6. fatty acid + CoA-SH + ATP → fatty acyl-CoA + AMP + PPi

Solution.

  1. NAD+ becomes NADH: an oxidoreductase (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase). The aldehyde is oxidized.
  2. One six-carbon molecule splits into two three-carbon molecules with no water and no NAD+: a lyase (aldolase).
  3. One reactant, one product, same formula; the phosphate has simply moved from C3 to C2: an isomerase (phosphoglycerate mutase).
  4. Water is a reactant, but nothing is split in two: it adds across the C=C of fumarate (a hydration, Chapter 3). That makes fumarase a lyase, not a hydrolase.
  5. An amino group moves from glutamate to oxaloacetate: a transferase (aspartate transaminase, the AST of a liver panel).
  6. ATP is spent and two molecules (fatty acid and CoA) become one: a ligase (acyl-CoA synthetase).

Check. The two reactions that use water, (d) and the hydrolase in Example 1(b), look alike until you ask whether the product count went up. Hydrolysis splits one molecule into two; hydration adds water to one molecule and keeps it whole.

Example 5: Name the regulation

Problem. Identify the type of regulation in each case. (a) In a bacterium, the amino acid isoleucine binds a site away from the active site of threonine deaminase, the first enzyme of the pathway that makes isoleucine, and slows it. (b) After a meal, insulin causes a phosphate to be removed from glycogen synthase, and the enzyme becomes active. (c) A patient who starts drinking heavily every day makes several times more of a liver CYP enzyme after two weeks. (d) Trypsinogen entering the small intestine loses a six-amino-acid piece and becomes trypsin.

Solution. (a) Feedback inhibition through an allosteric site: the end product inhibits the first enzyme. (b) Covalent modification (dephosphorylation by a phosphatase). (c) Genetic control (enzyme induction: more enzyme is made). (d) Zymogen activation by hydrolysis of a peptide bond, which is irreversible.

Check. Ask two questions: how fast (seconds for allosteric and feedback, minutes for covalent modification, hours to days for genetic control) and is it reversible (all but zymogen activation).

Example 6: From symptom to vitamin to coenzyme

Problem. A patient with a long history of heavy alcohol use is confused and has trouble walking and moving his eyes. Which vitamin is most likely deficient, which coenzyme is missing, and which step of metabolism stalls?

Solution. The picture is Wernicke encephalopathy, caused by lack of thiamine (B1). Its coenzyme is thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP), needed by pyruvate dehydrogenase, so pyruvate from glycolysis cannot become acetyl-CoA. Brain cells, which depend on glucose, run short of energy.

Check. Thiamine is water-soluble, so stores are small and a few weeks of poor intake can empty them; that fits the rapid onset. Beriberi is the same deficiency with heart and peripheral-nerve symptoms.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Saying enzymes change ΔH, ΔG, or the equilibrium, or are consumed. They only lower activation energy and speed up the approach to equilibrium.
  • Thinking more substrate overcomes noncompetitive or irreversible inhibition.
  • Mixing up "kinase" (moves a phosphate from ATP, transferase) with "phosphatase" (removes a phosphate with water, hydrolase).
  • Calling fumarase or any "hydratase" a hydrolase. Water that adds across a double bond is a lyase reaction; water that splits one molecule into two is hydrolysis. Aconitase (citrate → isocitrate, Chapter 14) is the trap: the overall change looks like an isomerization, but the enzyme works by removing water and adding it back across a C=C, so it is classified as a lyase (a hydratase).
  • Calling every ATP-using enzyme a ligase. Kinases use ATP too, but they transfer the phosphate; a ligase uses ATP to join two molecules into one. The name "synthase" (no ATP) is a lyase; "synthetase" (ATP) is a ligase.
  • Treating "optimum pH" as always 7. Pepsin's optimum is about 2 and alkaline phosphatase's about 9; the optimum is wherever the enzyme normally works.
  • Confusing cold with heat. Refrigeration slows an enzyme reversibly; heat above the optimum denatures it irreversibly.
  • Assuming fat-soluble vitamins are coenzymes. The coenzymes come from the water-soluble B vitamins; A, D, E, and K act as a light sensor, a hormone, an antioxidant, and a clotting cofactor.

Which vitamin is the precursor of NAD+? Is it fat- or water-soluble?

Answer
Niacin (vitamin B3); water-soluble

Exercise 4

Why are digestive proteases made as zymogens?

Answer
So they do not digest the proteins of the cells (pancreas, stomach lining) that make them. They are activated only after reaching the digestive tract.

Exercise 5

In a five-step pathway, high levels of the final product slow down the first enzyme. What kind of regulation is this?

Answer
Feedback control (feedback inhibition), usually acting through an allosteric site

Exercise 6

Classify the enzyme and name the clue: (a) isocitrate + NAD+ → α-ketoglutarate + CO2 + NADH + H+; (b) glucose-1-phosphate → glucose-6-phosphate; (c) acetyl-CoA + CO2 + ATP → malonyl-CoA + ADP + Pi.

Answer
(a) Oxidoreductase (isocitrate dehydrogenase): NAD+ is reduced to NADH, which outranks the loss of CO2 when you classify. (b) Isomerase (phosphoglucomutase): one molecule, same formula, phosphate moved to a different carbon. (c) Ligase (acetyl-CoA carboxylase): ATP is spent to join CO2 to acetyl-CoA; biotin is the coenzyme.

Exercise 7

A patient's blood work shows high ALT and AST with normal CK-MB and troponin. Which organ is most likely damaged, and what class of enzyme are ALT and AST?

Answer
The liver. ALT and AST are transaminases, which are transferases (they move an amino group from an amino acid to an α-keto acid). Normal cardiac markers argue against heart damage.
