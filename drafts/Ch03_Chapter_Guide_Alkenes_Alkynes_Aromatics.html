Learning Objectives

  • Name and draw alkenes, alkynes, cycloalkenes, and substituted benzenes.
  • Decide whether an alkene can have cis/trans isomers, and label them.
  • Predict the products of addition reactions (hydrogenation, halogenation, hydrohalogenation, hydration), using Markovnikov's rule.
  • Recognize elimination reactions as the reverse of addition.
  • Explain how benzene differs from an ordinary alkene.
  • Name polysubstituted benzenes with numbers or ortho/meta/para, and use "phenyl" when the ring is a substituent.
  • Predict the products of halogenation, nitration, and sulfonation of benzene, and explain why they are substitutions rather than additions.
  • Rank alkenes, alkynes, and aromatic compounds by boiling point and water solubility using intermolecular forces.
  • Match common addition polymers to their monomers and to their uses in health care, and recognize polynuclear aromatic hydrocarbons.

Key Ideas

1. Naming unsaturated hydrocarbons

An unsaturated hydrocarbon contains at least one carbon–carbon double or triple bond, so it holds fewer hydrogens than the alkane with the same number of carbons. Alkenes (C=C) have the general formula CnH2n and alkynes (C≡C) have CnH2n−2. The two carbons of a double bond are trigonal planar (120° angles); the two carbons of a triple bond are linear (180°).

  • Pick the longest chain that contains the double or triple bond.
  • Number from the end that gives the multiple bond the lowest number, even if a branch ends up with a higher number.
  • Change the ending: -ene (C=C) or -yne (C≡C). Put the number of the first carbon of the multiple bond in front of the parent: 2-pentene (also written pent-2-ene). Two double bonds: -diene, as in 1,3-butadiene.
  • Rings: cyclohexene. C1 and C2 are the double-bond carbons, and no number is needed for the double bond itself. Number through the double bond toward the first substituent: a methyl group on the carbon next to the double bond gives 3-methylcyclohexene, never 6-methylcyclohexene. A methyl group on a double-bond carbon makes it C1: 1-methylcyclopentene.
  • Alkynes follow exactly the same rules with -yne: HC≡CCH2CH3 is 1-butyne; CH3C≡CCH3 is 2-butyne.

2. Cis/trans isomers

A C=C cannot rotate. If each double-bond carbon has two different groups, two isomers exist:

  • cis: the matching groups (usually the main chain) are on the same side.
  • trans: they are on opposite sides.

If either double-bond carbon carries two identical groups (such as =CH2 or =C(CH3)2), cis/trans isomers are not possible. Alkynes are linear, so they never have cis/trans isomers.

Cis and trans isomers are stereoisomers: the atoms are connected in the same order, but they are arranged differently in space. They are different compounds with different melting and boiling points, and in living systems the difference matters: the double bonds in natural unsaturated fatty acids are almost all cis, which puts a kink in the chain (Chapter 9).

3. Addition reactions: "open the cabinet doors"

Professor Tip

Picture the double bond as two cabinet doors. Addition opens the doors: one piece of the reactant goes on each carbon, and the C=C becomes a C–C single bond.

ReactionReagentAddsProduct type
HydrogenationH2 with a Pt, Pd, or Ni catalystH and HAlkane
HalogenationCl2 or Br2X and XDihaloalkane
HydrohalogenationHCl, HBr, HIH and XHaloalkane
HydrationH2O with an H+ catalystH and OHAlcohol

In the first two reactions the two added pieces are identical (H and H, or X and X), so it does not matter which carbon gets which. In hydrohalogenation and hydration the two pieces are different, so with an unsymmetrical alkene you have to decide where each one goes.

Markovnikov's rule (for HX and H2O added to an unsymmetrical alkene): the H goes to the double-bond carbon that already has more H's. The X or OH goes to the other carbon.

Professor Tip

"Hydrogen wants to make the most friends." The new H joins the carbon that already has the most hydrogens.

A symmetrical alkene, such as 2-butene or cyclohexene, has the same groups on both double-bond carbons, so both ways of adding H–X give the same product and no rule is needed. An alkyne adds twice: 2-butyne + 2 H2 (Pt) gives butane.

Alkenes and alkynes also undergo combustion, just like alkanes, and the products are the same: C2H4 + 3 O2 → 2 CO2 + 2 H2O. Combustion is the one reaction that does not care whether a hydrocarbon is saturated, unsaturated, or aromatic.

4. Elimination: the reverse of addition

Elimination removes two pieces from neighboring carbons and creates a double bond: dehydrogenation (−H2), dehalogenation (−X2), dehydrohalogenation (−HX), and dehydration (−H2O). When more than one alkene is possible, the major product usually has the more substituted double bond (Zaitsev's rule).

5. Aromatic compounds

  • Benzene (C6H6) is drawn with alternating double bonds, but its electrons are spread evenly around the ring. That makes it unusually stable, and it does not undergo the addition reactions above.
  • Common names to know: toluene (methylbenzene), phenol (hydroxybenzene), aniline (aminobenzene), and benzoic acid.
  • For two substituents: ortho (1,2: next-door neighbors), meta (1,3: one carbon in between), para (1,4: directly opposite).

The structure of benzene. Benzene is a flat, regular hexagon of six carbons, each carrying one hydrogen. If you draw it with three C=C bonds you can place them in two equally good ways (the two Kekulé structures in the figure below). Neither drawing is right on its own: every C–C bond in benzene is the same length, partway between a single and a double bond, because the six electrons that the drawing puts into "double bonds" are actually shared around the whole ring. Chemists show this with a circle inside the hexagon. A ring that shares its electrons this way is called aromatic (the name is historical; it has nothing to do with smell), and the shared electron cloud is what makes the ring so much harder to break open than an alkene.

Naming substituted benzenes.

  • One substituent: name the group, then "benzene," with no number: chlorobenzene, nitrobenzene (–NO2), ethylbenzene. A few rings have their own parent names that you must memorize: toluene (–CH3), phenol (–OH), aniline (–NH2), and benzoic acid (–COOH).
  • Two substituents: there are only three possible arrangements, 1,2-, 1,3-, and 1,4-, called ortho (o-), meta (m-), and para (p-). You may use either the numbers or the prefix. If one of the groups gives the ring a special parent name, that group is on carbon 1 and is not named as a substituent: a chlorine straight across from the methyl of toluene is 4-chlorotoluene or p-chlorotoluene.
  • Three or more substituents: numbers only (no o/m/p). Number to give the lowest set of numbers, start from the special parent group if there is one, and list the substituents alphabetically: 2,4-dichlorotoluene; 1-bromo-3-chloro-5-nitrobenzene.

6. Polymers

Many alkenes (monomers) can add to one another over and over to make long addition polymers: polyethylene, polypropylene, PVC (from chloroethene), and Teflon (from tetrafluoroethene).

A polymer is a giant molecule built from thousands of small repeating units; the small molecule you start with is the monomer. In addition polymerization the double bond of one monomer opens and bonds to the next, which opens and bonds to the next, so nothing is lost: the polymer contains every atom of the monomers. For ethene, n CH2=CH2 → –(CH2–CH2)n–. The product has no double bonds left, which is why these plastics are as unreactive as alkanes and can be sterilized, stored, and implanted. The table below lists the monomer, the repeating unit, and a health-care use for the polymers you should know. To find the monomer from a polymer, pick out the two-carbon repeating unit and put the double bond back between its carbons.

7. Benzene as a substituent and polynuclear aromatics

  • When a benzene ring is attached to a chain that holds the main functional group, it is named as a substituent: phenyl (C6H5–). Example: CH3CH(C6H5)CH2CH3 is 2-phenylbutane.
  • Polynuclear aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) have two or more fused benzene rings: naphthalene (2 rings, mothballs), anthracene (3 rings). Larger PAHs such as benzo[a]pyrene form when organic matter burns incompletely and are carcinogens in tobacco smoke and charred food.

Use "phenyl" when the chain is the main part of the name, usually because it carries the functional group: 2-phenylbutane (drawn in the naming figure above), 2-phenylethanol. When the ring is clearly the parent, name the chain as an alkyl substituent instead: ethylbenzene, not phenylethane. Both names describe real molecules correctly; the convention just keeps names short.

In a PAH, neighboring rings share an edge (two carbons), so naphthalene is C10H8, not C12. All the rings lie in one flat sheet, which lets PAH molecules stack like plates; that stacking is why even naphthalene is a solid at room temperature. PAHs are nonpolar and dissolve in body fat. When the liver tries to oxidize benzo[a]pyrene to make it water soluble for excretion, some of the intermediate products react with DNA, which is how a chemically "stable" aromatic compound becomes a carcinogen (a cancer-causing agent). Wood smoke, diesel exhaust, grilled meat with char, and above all tobacco smoke are the common sources.

8. Benzene reacts by substitution, not addition

In electrophilic aromatic substitution an H on the ring is replaced, so the stable aromatic ring survives:

  • Halogenation: benzene + Br2 (FeBr3 catalyst) → bromobenzene + HBr
  • Nitration: benzene + HNO3 (H2SO4 catalyst) → nitrobenzene + H2O
  • Sulfonation: benzene + SO3 (H2SO4) → benzenesulfonic acid

Why substitution? If Br2 added across one "double bond" of benzene the way it adds to cyclohexene, the ring would lose its shared electron cloud and most of the stability that goes with it. Replacing a hydrogen keeps the cloud intact, so that is the path benzene takes. The reagent that attaches to the ring is an electrophile ("electron-lover"): an electron-poor species that is attracted to the electron-rich ring. On its own Br2 is not a strong enough electrophile, which is why benzene gives no reaction in the bromine test. The catalyst (FeBr3 for bromine, H2SO4 for nitric acid) makes the reagent a much stronger electrophile, and the substitution proceeds. You do not need the step-by-step mechanism; you need to recognize the three reactions, their reagents, and their products, which are summarized in the table and figure below.

Substituted benzenes react the same way; the new group simply lands on one of the remaining ring hydrogens. Toluene + Br2/FeBr3 gives bromotoluene (mainly the ortho and para isomers). The three products above matter later in the course: nitrobenzene is reduced to aniline, the parent of many amine drugs (Chapter 7), and the sulfonic acid group is the strongly acidic, water-solubilizing group in detergents, while its close relative, the sulfonamide group (–SO2NH2), is the core of the sulfa antibiotics.

9. Physical properties: alkenes, alkynes, and aromatics

All of the hydrocarbons in this chapter are made of only carbon and hydrogen, so they are nonpolar and held together by dispersion forces alone. That gives them the same property profile as alkanes: insoluble in water, less dense than water (they float), and boiling points that rise with size. A double or triple bond removes two or four hydrogens but changes the size and shape of the molecule very little, so an alkene boils within a few degrees of the alkane with the same carbon skeleton. The table below shows how close they are.

To rank boiling points, count carbons first: more carbons means more electrons and more surface contact, so stronger dispersion forces. Among molecules of the same size, flat rings (benzene, naphthalene) stack more closely than floppy chains, so they boil and melt a little higher than an open-chain alkene of the same carbon count. Water solubility is essentially zero for every compound in this chapter; the first hydrocarbon derivatives that dissolve in water are the small alcohols and phenol in Chapter 4, because they add an O–H that can hydrogen bond. One practical consequence: benzene and toluene are good solvents for nonpolar substances and are absorbed through skin, and benzene is itself a carcinogen, so laboratories have replaced it with toluene wherever they can.

Health Career Connection

  • Dental hygiene – tooth-colored fillings and sealants: Composite resins and pit-and-fissure sealants are liquid monomers with C=C double bonds (methacrylates such as Bis-GMA). When the curing light is applied, the double bonds "open" and link the monomers into a hard addition polymer inside the tooth. You are watching addition polymerization chairside.
  • Nursing – medical plastics: IV bags and tubing are often PVC. Syringes are usually polypropylene. Both are addition polymers of simple alkenes. Pure PVC is rigid; it is made flexible with a plasticizer, most often DEHP (di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate, an ester), which can leach into lipid-containing fluids. That is why DEHP-free tubing is specified for neonatal units and for infusions of fat emulsions.
  • Nutrition – trans fats: Partial hydrogenation of vegetable oils converts some natural cis double bonds to trans. Trans fats raise LDL ("bad") cholesterol, which is why they now have to be listed on food labels. More in Chapter 9.
  • Pharmacology: Benzene rings appear in many drugs patients take every day, including aspirin, acetaminophen, and ibuprofen.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

The hydrogenation reaction in Key Idea 3 is run on an industrial scale on vegetable oils. An oil is a triacylglycerol whose fatty-acid chains contain several cis C=C bonds; the kinks keep the chains from packing, so the oil is liquid. Bubbling H2 over a nickel catalyst adds H to some of those double bonds, straightens the chains, and turns the oil into a spreadable solid, which is how margarine and shortening were made. The problem is that the catalyst also lets some of the remaining double bonds flip from cis to trans. These trans fatty acids behave like saturated fat in the body: they raise LDL ("bad") cholesterol and lower HDL ("good") cholesterol on a lipid panel, worsening hyperlipidemia (excess blood lipids) and the risk of atherosclerosis, the fatty plaque that narrows arteries. Partially hydrogenated oils were removed from the U.S. food supply for this reason. When you read a patient's lipid panel, you are looking at the downstream result of cis/trans isomerism. One more polyene to recognize: beta-carotene in carrots is a long chain of alternating C=C bonds that the body splits into two molecules of vitamin A.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Three aromatic stories play out in the operatory. First, benzocaine, the active ingredient in topical anesthetic gel applied before scaling or injections, is a para-disubstituted benzene: an amino group (–NH2) on carbon 1 and an ester group straight across on carbon 4. Spotting that 1,4 pattern is exactly the skill in Key Idea 5. Second, tobacco smoke carries polynuclear aromatic hydrocarbons such as benzo[a]pyrene directly onto oral tissue, which is why smokers have a much higher rate of oral squamous cell carcinoma and of periodontitis (infection and bone loss around the teeth that follows untreated gingivitis). Smoking also narrows gingival blood vessels, so the bleeding that normally signals gum disease may be masked. Third, the resins you work with every day are alkene chemistry: pit-and-fissure sealants and composite restorations are methacrylate monomers (Bis-GMA and its thinner partners) whose C=C bonds open under the curing light and link into a hard addition polymer, and denture bases and orthodontic retainers are poly(methyl methacrylate), the acrylic in the polymer table.

In the Lab

  • Experiment 3 – Reactions of Hydrocarbons
  • Bromine test: the red color of Br2 disappears quickly with an alkene (addition across the C=C). With an alkane the color lingers and fades only slowly in light (substitution, which also releases HBr). Benzene gives no reaction.
  • KMnO4 test: purple permanganate adds to a C=C to make a diol and turns into a muddy brown MnO2 solid. Alkanes and aromatic rings stay purple.
  • You will use these two tests to identify an unknown hydrocarbon as saturated or unsaturated.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Naming an alkene and checking for cis/trans

Name CH3CH=CHCH2CH(CH3)CH3. Can it exist as cis and trans isomers?

Solution

The longest chain containing the C=C has 6 carbons. Numbering from the left puts the double bond at C2 (from the right it would be C4), so number from the left. The methyl branch is on C5.

Name: 5-methyl-2-hexene. C2 has H and CH3, and C3 has H and CH2R. Each double-bond carbon has two different groups, so yes, cis and trans isomers exist.

Example 2: Markovnikov addition of HBr

Predict the major product of propene, CH2=CHCH3, + HBr.

Solution

C1 (=CH2) has 2 H's and C2 (=CH–) has 1 H. The H goes to C1 and the Br goes to C2.

Product: CH3CHBrCH3, 2-bromopropane (not 1-bromopropane).

Example 3: Hydration

Predict the product of 2-methylpropene, (CH3)2C=CH2, + H2O (H+ catalyst).

Solution

H goes to the =CH2 carbon. OH goes to the carbon holding the two methyl groups.

Product: (CH3)3C–OH, 2-methyl-2-propanol.

Example 4: Elimination (Zaitsev)

2-Bromobutane, CH3CHBrCH2CH3, undergoes dehydrohalogenation. What is the major alkene product?

Solution

HBr can be removed toward C1 (giving 1-butene) or toward C3 (giving 2-butene). 2-Butene has more carbon groups on its double bond, so it is the major product. Notice this is simply an addition reaction (Key Idea 3) run backwards.

Example 5: Naming a disubstituted benzene

A benzene ring has Cl atoms on carbons 1 and 3. Give the IUPAC and ortho/meta/para names.

Solution

1,3-dichlorobenzene, also called m-dichlorobenzene (one carbon in between = meta).

Example 6: Naming a cycloalkene and an alkyne

Problem. (a) A cyclohexene ring carries a CH3 group on the carbon next to one of the double-bond carbons. Name it. (b) Name CH3CH2C≡CCH(CH3)CH3.

Solution. (a) In a ring the double bond is always C1–C2, so the only decision is which direction to number. Going from C1 through the double bond to C2 and on toward the methyl puts it on C3; going the other way around the ring would put it on C6. Lowest number wins: 3-methylcyclohexene. (b) The longest chain containing the triple bond has 6 carbons. From the left the triple bond starts at C3; from the right it also starts at C3, so use the branch to break the tie: from the right the methyl is on C2, from the left it would be C5. Name: 2-methyl-3-hexyne.

Check. In (a) the name has no number for the double bond, which is correct for a ring; "6-methylcyclohexene" would be the same molecule numbered the wrong way. In (b) count hydrogens: C7H12 fits CnH2n−2 for an alkyne with 7 carbons (6 in the chain plus the methyl).

Example 7: Which alkenes have cis/trans isomers?

Problem. For each alkene, decide whether cis and trans isomers exist, and if so draw the trans isomer in condensed form: (a) 1-chloropropene, ClCH=CHCH3; (b) 2-methyl-2-butene, (CH3)2C=CHCH3; (c) 1-pentene, CH2=CHCH2CH2CH3.

Solution. Look at each double-bond carbon separately and ask, "Are its two groups different?"

  • (a) C1 has Cl and H (different). C2 has CH3 and H (different). Yes. Trans isomer: Cl and CH3 on opposite sides of the C=C; cis: same side.
  • (b) C2 has CH3 and CH3: two identical groups. No. Swapping the two methyls changes nothing, so there is only one 2-methyl-2-butene. You do not even need to look at C3.
  • (c) C1 has H and H. No. Any 1-alkene fails the test at C1.

Check. The test fails as soon as either carbon carries a matched pair. Both carbons must pass for cis/trans to exist, which is why 2-butene, 2-pentene, and 3-hexene have isomers and 1-butene, 2-methylpropene, and 2-methyl-2-pentene do not.

Example 8: When does Markovnikov's rule decide the product?

Problem. Give the major organic product of HBr addition to (a) 2-butene, (b) 1-butene, and (c) 2-pentene.

Solution.

  • (a) 2-Butene, CH3CH=CHCH3, is symmetrical: both double-bond carbons carry one H and one CH3. Whichever carbon gets the Br, the product is the same: 2-bromobutane. No rule needed.
  • (b) 1-Butene, CH2=CHCH2CH3, is unsymmetrical: C1 has two H's, C2 has one. Markovnikov: H to C1, Br to C2. Major product 2-bromobutane (1-bromobutane is minor).
  • (c) 2-Pentene, CH3CH=CHCH2CH3, is unsymmetrical, but each double-bond carbon has exactly one H. Markovnikov's rule cannot choose, so you get a mixture of 2-bromopentane and 3-bromopentane in roughly equal amounts.

Check. Count the hydrogens on the two C=C carbons. A tie means either both products are identical (symmetrical alkene) or you get a mixture (unsymmetrical alkene with a tie). Only when one carbon clearly has more H's does Markovnikov's rule pick a single major product.

Example 9: Naming polysubstituted benzenes

Problem. Name each compound. (a) A benzene ring with –CH3 and, straight across the ring, –Cl. (b) A benzene ring with –OH and, on the next carbon, –NO2. (c) A benzene ring with –Br on C1 and –CH2CH3 on C3. (d) A benzene ring with –CH3 and two Cl atoms, one next to the methyl and one straight across from it.

Solution.

  • (a) The methyl makes this a toluene, so –CH3 is C1 and the Cl is on C4: 4-chlorotoluene, or p-chlorotoluene.
  • (b) The OH makes it a phenol; OH is C1 and the nitro group is on C2: 2-nitrophenol, or o-nitrophenol.
  • (c) Neither group has a special parent, so the parent is benzene and the substituents are listed alphabetically: 1-bromo-3-ethylbenzene, or m-bromoethylbenzene.
  • (d) Three substituents, so numbers only. Toluene parent: CH3 on C1, the chlorines on C2 and C4: 2,4-dichlorotoluene.

Check. Two groups: o/m/p or numbers are both fine. Three or more: numbers only. A special parent group (toluene, phenol, aniline, benzoic acid) is always on C1 and is never listed as a substituent; in (a) "1-chloro-4-methylbenzene" is not wrong, but 4-chlorotoluene is the name you will see on labels.

Example 10: Addition or substitution? Cyclohexene versus benzene

Problem. Both cyclohexene (C6H10) and benzene (C6H6) are six-membered rings. Write the organic product, its molecular formula, and the reaction type for (a) cyclohexene + Br2 and (b) benzene + Br2 with FeBr3.

Solution. (a) Cyclohexene has an ordinary C=C, so Br2 adds: one Br on each former double-bond carbon. Product 1,2-dibromocyclohexane, C6H10Br2; both bromine atoms end up in the product and nothing else forms. Addition. (b) Benzene keeps its aromatic ring. One Br replaces one ring H: product bromobenzene, C6H5Br, plus HBr. Substitution (electrophilic aromatic substitution).

Check. Addition makes one product and uses up the double bond; the formula grows by the whole reagent (C6H10 + Br2). Substitution makes two products and the ring formula changes by swapping one H for one Br (C6H6 → C6H5Br). Without the FeBr3 catalyst, (b) does not happen at all, which is the basis of the bromine test in the lab.

Example 11: Ranking by boiling point and predicting solubility

Problem. Rank from lowest to highest boiling point: 1-octene, propene, 1-hexene, benzene. Which of them dissolves in water?

Solution. All four are nonpolar hydrocarbons with dispersion forces only, so size decides. Carbon count: propene 3, benzene 6, 1-hexene 6, 1-octene 8. Propene is lowest (it is a gas at room temperature, bp −48 °C) and 1-octene is highest (121 °C). Benzene and 1-hexene tie on carbons; benzene is a flat ring that stacks closely, so it boils a bit higher (80 °C versus 63 °C). Ranking: propene < 1-hexene < benzene < 1-octene. None of them dissolves in water; all four float on it.

Check. The two six-carbon compounds differ by only 17 °C, while adding two carbons (hexene to octene) adds nearly 60 °C. Size is the big lever; shape only breaks ties.

Example 12: Monomer from polymer, polymer from monomer

Problem. (a) A hospital tubing sample is a polymer with the repeating unit –CH2–CHCl–. Name the polymer and write the monomer. (b) Write three repeating units of the addition polymer made from propene.

Solution. (a) Put the double bond back between the two carbons of the repeating unit: CH2=CHCl, chloroethene (vinyl chloride). The polymer is poly(vinyl chloride), PVC. (b) Each propene opens its C=C and bonds to the next: –CH2–CH(CH3)–CH2–CH(CH3)–CH2–CH(CH3)–, polypropylene. The methyl groups hang off every other carbon of the backbone.

Check. The repeating unit always has the same atoms as the monomer (C2H3Cl in both parts of (a)); nothing is gained or lost in addition polymerization.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Numbering to favor a branch instead of the double bond. The C=C always gets the lowest number.
  • Assigning cis/trans when one carbon of the double bond has two identical groups.
  • Using addition reactions on benzene. Aromatic rings resist addition.
  • Forgetting Markovnikov's rule. With symmetrical alkenes (2-butene) it does not matter, but with unsymmetrical alkenes it does.
  • Numbering a cycloalkene the long way around. "6-Methylcyclohexene" is 3-methylcyclohexene numbered backwards. Go C1 → C2 through the double bond, then toward the nearest substituent.
  • Applying Markovnikov's rule to Br2 or H2. The rule only matters when the two added pieces are different (H–X, H–OH). Halogenation and hydrogenation add two identical pieces, so there is nothing to decide.
  • Treating the special parents as substituents. A ring with OH and Cl is a chlorophenol, not a "hydroxychlorobenzene," and the OH carbon is C1. The same goes for toluene, aniline, and benzoic acid.
  • Using ortho/meta/para for three or more groups. The prefixes describe the relationship between exactly two groups; with three, use numbers.
  • Counting fused-ring carbons twice. Naphthalene is C10H8; the two shared carbons belong to both rings and carry no hydrogens.

Answer
Propene, CH2=CHCH3

Exercise 6

Give the organic product of benzene + Cl2 with an FeCl3 catalyst. What type of reaction is it?

Answer
Chlorobenzene (+ HCl). Electrophilic aromatic substitution: one ring H is replaced by Cl and the ring stays aromatic.

Exercise 7

Name a benzene ring that carries an OH group and a Br atom on adjacent carbons, and give the product of benzene + HNO3 with H2SO4.

Answer
2-bromophenol (o-bromophenol); the phenol OH is C1. Benzene + HNO3/H2SO4 gives nitrobenzene + H2O (nitration, a substitution).
