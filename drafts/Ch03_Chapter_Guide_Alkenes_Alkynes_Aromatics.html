Learning Objectives

  • Name and draw alkenes, alkynes, cycloalkenes, and substituted benzenes.
  • Decide whether an alkene can have cis/trans isomers, and label them.
  • Predict the products of addition reactions (hydrogenation, halogenation, hydrohalogenation, hydration), using Markovnikov's rule.
  • Recognize elimination reactions (dehydrogenation, dehalogenation, dehydrohalogenation, dehydration) as the reverse of addition, and use Zaitsev's rule to pick the major alkene.
  • Explain how benzene differs from an ordinary alkene.
  • Name polysubstituted benzenes with numbers or ortho/meta/para, and use "phenyl" when the ring is a substituent.
  • Predict the products of halogenation, nitration, and sulfonation of benzene, and explain why they are substitutions rather than additions.
  • Rank alkenes, alkynes, and aromatic compounds by boiling point and water solubility using intermolecular forces.
  • Match common addition polymers to their monomers and to their uses in health care, and recognize polynuclear aromatic hydrocarbons.

Key Ideas

1. Naming unsaturated hydrocarbons

Alkenes (C=C) are CnH2n; alkynes (C≡C) are CnH2n−2. Pick the longest chain that contains the multiple bond, number so the multiple bond gets the lowest number even if a branch ends up higher, and change the ending to -ene or -yne (2-pentene or pent-2-ene; 1,3-butadiene). In a ring the double bond is C1–C2 and needs no number; number through it toward the first substituent (3-methylcyclohexene). Always write the multiple bond into a condensed formula and draw an alkyne straight. Details, example, and widget: Sections 3.1 and 3.6.

2. Cis/trans isomers

Double bonds cannot rotate, so an alkene is rigid and planar. If each double-bond carbon carries two different groups, two stereoisomers exist: cis (matching groups on the same side) and trans (opposite sides). A =CH2 or =C(CH3)2 carbon kills the possibility; alkynes never have cis/trans isomers. Natural fatty acids are cis (Chapter 9). Figure, examples, and the trans-fat connection: Section 3.2.

3. Addition reactions: "open the double doors"

Two reactants become one product and the C=C becomes C–C. Hydrogenation (H2, Pt/Pd/Ni) gives an alkane; halogenation (Cl2 or Br2) a dihaloalkane; hydrohalogenation (HCl, HBr, HI) a haloalkane; hydration (H2O, H+) an alcohol. For H–X and H–OH on an unsymmetrical alkene, Markovnikov's rule: the H goes to the carbon that already has more H's ("hydrogen wants to make the most friends") and the X or OH to the more substituted carbon. Symmetrical alkenes need no rule; an alkyne adds twice; the bromine test (red color disappears) detects a C=C. Alkenes also burn to CO2 and H2O. Table, tips, examples, and widget: Section 3.4.

4. Elimination: the reverse of addition

One reactant loses two pieces from neighboring carbons and a double bond forms: dehydrogenation (−H2), dehalogenation (−X2), dehydrohalogenation (−HX), dehydration (−H2O; strong acid and heat, Chapter 4). Zaitsev's rule: remove the "lonely" H from the neighboring carbon with the fewest hydrogens, so the more substituted alkene is the major product. Markovnikov (addition) and Zaitsev (elimination) point in opposite directions. Details and examples: Section 3.4.

5. Aromatic compounds

Benzene, C6H6, is a flat hexagon whose six "double-bond" electrons are delocalized around the ring (two Kekulé drawings or a circle in a hexagon). That stability is why it does not add H2, Br2, or H2O. Naming: one group, "name + benzene" with no number, except the special parents toluene (CH3), phenol (OH), aniline (NH2), and benzoic acid (COOH), whose group is C1. Two groups: ortho (1,2), meta (1,3), para (1,4) or numbers. Three or more: numbers only, lowest set, alphabetical. Structure and figures: Section 3.7; naming figures and example: Section 3.8.

6. Polymers

A monomer with a C=C adds to itself over and over to make an addition polymer that contains every atom of its monomers: polyethylene (specimen bottles, joint replacements), polypropylene (syringes, masks), PVC (IV bags and tubing), PTFE/Teflon (grafts, floss), polystyrene (petri dishes), PMMA acrylic (dentures, bone cement). To find the monomer, take the two-carbon repeating unit and put the double bond back. Table and example: Section 3.5.

7. Benzene as a substituent and polynuclear aromatics

When the ring hangs off a chain that carries the main functional group, name it phenyl (C6H5–): 2-phenylbutane. Polynuclear (polycyclic) aromatic hydrocarbons share ring edges: naphthalene C10H8 (two rings, mothballs), anthracene (three); benzo[a]pyrene from tobacco smoke and charred food is a carcinogen. Details and figure: Section 3.8.

8. Benzene reacts by substitution, not addition

In electrophilic aromatic substitution a ring H is replaced and the aromatic ring survives: halogenation (Br2, FeBr3 → bromobenzene + HBr), nitration (HNO3, H2SO4 → nitrobenzene + H2O), sulfonation (SO3, H2SO4 → benzenesulfonic acid). The catalyst makes the reagent reactive enough; without it, benzene gives no reaction in the bromine test. Know the reagents and products, not the mechanism. Table, scheme figure, and the cyclohexene-versus-benzene example: Section 3.7.

9. Physical properties: alkenes, alkynes, and aromatics

All hydrocarbons: nonpolar, dispersion forces only, insoluble in water, float on it, and boil higher as the carbon count grows. An alkene boils within a few degrees of the alkane with the same skeleton; flat aromatic rings stack and boil a little higher than open chains of the same size (benzene 80 °C vs 1-hexene 63 °C). Nothing in this chapter dissolves in water. Data table and ranking example: Section 3.3.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Naming an alkene and checking for cis/trans

Name CH3CH=CHCH2CH(CH3)CH3. Can it exist as cis and trans isomers?

Solution

The longest chain containing the C=C has 6 carbons. Numbering from the left puts the double bond at C2 (from the right it would be C4), so number from the left. The methyl branch is on C5.

Name: 5-methyl-2-hexene. C2 has H and CH3, and C3 has H and CH2R. Each double-bond carbon has two different groups, so yes, cis and trans isomers exist.

Example 2: Markovnikov addition of HBr

Predict the major product of propene, CH2=CHCH3, + HBr.

Solution

C1 (=CH2) has 2 H's and C2 (=CH–) has 1 H. The H goes to C1 and the Br goes to C2.

Product: CH3CHBrCH3, 2-bromopropane (not 1-bromopropane).

Example 3: Hydration

Predict the product of 2-methylpropene, (CH3)2C=CH2, + H2O (H+ catalyst).

Solution

H goes to the =CH2 carbon. OH goes to the carbon holding the two methyl groups.

Product: (CH3)3C–OH, 2-methyl-2-propanol.

Example 4: Elimination (Zaitsev)

2-Bromobutane, CH3CHBrCH2CH3, undergoes dehydrohalogenation. What is the major alkene product?

Solution

HBr can be removed toward C1 (giving 1-butene) or toward C3 (giving 2-butene). 2-Butene has more carbon groups on its double bond, so it is the major product. Notice this is simply an addition reaction (Key Idea 3) run backwards.

Example 5: Naming a disubstituted benzene

A benzene ring has Cl atoms on carbons 1 and 3. Give the IUPAC and ortho/meta/para names.

Solution

1,3-dichlorobenzene, also called m-dichlorobenzene (one carbon in between = meta).

Health Career Connection

  • Dental hygiene – tooth-colored fillings and sealants: Composite resins and pit-and-fissure sealants are liquid monomers with C=C double bonds (methacrylates such as Bis-GMA). When the curing light is applied, the double bonds "open" and link the monomers into a hard addition polymer inside the tooth. You are watching addition polymerization chairside.
  • Nursing – medical plastics: IV bags and tubing are often PVC. Syringes are usually polypropylene. Both are addition polymers of simple alkenes. Pure PVC is rigid; it is made flexible with a plasticizer, most often DEHP (di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate, an ester), which can leach into lipid-containing fluids. That is why DEHP-free tubing is specified for neonatal units and for infusions of fat emulsions.
  • Nutrition – trans fats: Partial hydrogenation of vegetable oils converts some natural cis double bonds to trans. Trans fats raise LDL ("bad") cholesterol, which is why they now have to be listed on food labels. More in Chapter 9.
  • Pharmacology: Benzene rings appear in many drugs patients take every day, including aspirin, acetaminophen, and ibuprofen.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Numbering to favor a branch instead of the double bond. The C=C always gets the lowest number.
  • Assigning cis/trans when one carbon of the double bond has two identical groups.
  • Using addition reactions on benzene. Aromatic rings resist addition.
  • Forgetting Markovnikov's rule. With symmetrical alkenes (2-butene) it does not matter, but with unsymmetrical alkenes it does.
  • Numbering a cycloalkene the long way around. "6-Methylcyclohexene" is 3-methylcyclohexene numbered backwards. Go C1 → C2 through the double bond, then toward the nearest substituent.
  • Applying Markovnikov's rule to Br2 or H2. The rule only matters when the two added pieces are different (H–X, H–OH). Halogenation and hydrogenation add two identical pieces, so there is nothing to decide.
  • Treating the special parents as substituents. A ring with OH and Cl is a chlorophenol, not a "hydroxychlorobenzene," and the OH carbon is C1. The same goes for toluene, aniline, and benzoic acid.
  • Using ortho/meta/para for three or more groups. The prefixes describe the relationship between exactly two groups; with three, use numbers.
  • Counting fused-ring carbons twice. Naphthalene is C10H8; the two shared carbons belong to both rings and carry no hydrogens.
  • Mixing up Markovnikov and Zaitsev. Markovnikov is for addition: H goes to the carbon with the most H's. Zaitsev is for elimination: H is removed from the carbon with the fewest H's. The two rules point in opposite directions and both favor the more substituted carbon or alkene.
  • Leaving the multiple bond out of a condensed formula, or bending an alkyne. Write CH3CH=CHCH3, not CH3CHCHCH3, and draw the C≡C and its two neighbors in a straight line.

Practice

Exercise 1

Name HC≡CCH(CH3)CH3.

Answer
3-methyl-1-butyne

Exercise 2

Which can have cis/trans isomers: (a) 1-pentene, (b) 2-pentene, (c) 2-methyl-2-pentene?

Answer
Only (b). In (a) C1 has two H's. In (c) C2 has two CH3 groups.

Exercise 3

Give the product of each: (a) cyclopentene + H2/Pt; (b) ethene + Cl2; (c) 1-butene + HCl.

Answer
(a) cyclopentane; (b) 1,2-dichloroethane; (c) 2-chlorobutane (Markovnikov)

Exercise 4

Draw (condensed) and name the para isomer of dimethylbenzene.

Answer
1,4-dimethylbenzene (p-dimethylbenzene, common name p-xylene): CH3–C6H4–CH3 with the two methyls directly across the ring.

Exercise 5

What monomer is used to make polypropylene?

Answer
Propene, CH2=CHCH3

Exercise 6

Give the organic product of benzene + Cl2 with an FeCl3 catalyst. What type of reaction is it?

Answer
Chlorobenzene (+ HCl). Electrophilic aromatic substitution: one ring H is replaced by Cl and the ring stays aromatic.

Exercise 7

Name a benzene ring that carries an OH group and a Br atom on adjacent carbons, and give the product of benzene + HNO3 with H2SO4.

Answer
2-bromophenol (o-bromophenol); the phenol OH is C1. Benzene + HNO3/H2SO4 gives nitrobenzene + H2O (nitration, a substitution).
