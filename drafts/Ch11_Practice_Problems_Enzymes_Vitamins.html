Very low. At pH 8 the charges on R groups in and around the active site change, disrupting substrate binding and the enzyme’s shape.

More Practice: Analyze and Compare

Problem 11.52

The graph shows the rate of one enzyme-catalyzed reaction as substrate is added, measured with no inhibitor (curve A), with inhibitor X (curve B), and with inhibitor Y (curve C).

(a) Which inhibitor is competitive and which is noncompetitive? (b) Curve B is still climbing at the right edge of the graph. If you kept adding substrate, would it eventually reach curve A? Would curve C? Explain. (c) If inhibitor Y bound covalently instead of reversibly, which curve would look the same and why?

Answer
(a) X is competitive and Y is noncompetitive. (b) Curve B rises more slowly because X competes with substrate for the active site, but at very high substrate the substrate wins almost every time, so curve B eventually reaches the same maximum rate as A. Y binds somewhere else and disables a fraction of the enzyme molecules no matter how much substrate is present, so the maximum rate itself is lowered and C never catches up. (c) Curve C. An irreversible inhibitor also removes a fraction of the enzyme permanently; on this kind of plot it looks like noncompetitive inhibition, with a lowered maximum rate that substrate cannot restore. The difference is that washing the inhibitor away would restore Y's enzyme but not a covalently modified one.

Problem 11.53

A student measured the activity of an unknown digestive enzyme at 37 °C in a series of buffers and recorded the data in the table. The relative rate is the product formed per minute compared with the fastest trial.

pH1.02.03.04.56.07.59.0
Relative rate (%)551007015300

(a) What is the optimum pH? (b) Which of the enzymes in this chapter is this most likely to be, and in what organ does it work? (c) Would you expect the activity at pH 7.5 to return if the sample were brought back to pH 2.0? Explain.

Answer
(a) About pH 2, where the rate is highest. (b) Pepsin, the protease of the stomach, whose optimum is near 2. Trypsin (optimum near 8) would show the opposite pattern. (c) Probably not. Small moves away from the optimum change the charges on active-site R groups reversibly, but a change from pH 2 to 7.5 is large enough to denature pepsin, and denaturation of a protein by pH is usually permanent. (In the body this is how pepsin is inactivated once it reaches the small intestine.)

Problem 11.54

Compare and contrast a competitive inhibitor and a negative allosteric regulator. Give one similarity and two differences, and name a drug or metabolite that is an example of each.

Answer
Similarity: both bind the enzyme reversibly and lower its activity; both let go, so the inhibition ends when their concentration falls. Differences: (1) A competitive inhibitor binds the active site and resembles the substrate; an allosteric regulator binds a separate regulatory site and need not look like the substrate. (2) Competitive inhibition is overcome by more substrate; allosteric inhibition is not, because the regulator is not competing for the same site. Examples: a statin (competitive at HMG-CoA reductase) and ATP as a negative allosteric regulator of phosphofructokinase, or the end product of any feedback-controlled pathway.

Problem 11.55

Two enzyme preparations are stored for a week: one in a refrigerator at 4 °C and one in a hot car that reached 60 °C. Both are then assayed at 37 °C. Predict the activity of each relative to a fresh sample and explain the difference in terms of protein structure.

Answer
The refrigerated sample should show nearly full activity: cold slows reactions but does not break the hydrogen bonds and other weak interactions that hold the tertiary structure, so the active site is intact once rewarmed. The sample from the hot car will have little or no activity: 60 °C is well above the optimum of a human enzyme, the weak interactions were disrupted, the chain unfolded (denatured), and the active site no longer exists. Heat denaturation is not reversed by cooling.

More Practice: Clinical and Integrative

Problem 11.56

Which statement about enzymes and energy is correct?

  1. An enzyme lowers ΔG for the reaction so that more product forms at equilibrium.
  2. An enzyme lowers the activation energy and leaves ΔG and the equilibrium position unchanged.
  3. An enzyme raises the energy of the products so the reaction stops sooner.
  4. An enzyme supplies the activation energy from ATP.
Answer
B. A catalyst changes only the path between reactants and products (the height of the hill), not the energies of the reactants or products themselves, so ΔG and the equilibrium constant are the same with or without the enzyme; equilibrium is simply reached sooner. Choice D confuses catalysis with the ATP-driven reactions of ligases and kinases, where ATP is a reactant, not a source of activation energy.

Problem 11.57

In the citric acid cycle, malate + NAD+ → oxaloacetate + NADH + H+. The enzyme is a(n)

  1. hydrolase
  2. lyase
  3. oxidoreductase
  4. ligase
Answer
C. NAD+ is reduced to NADH while malate's secondary alcohol is oxidized to a ketone; any reaction with NAD+/NADH or FAD/FADH2 in the equation is catalyzed by an oxidoreductase (here malate dehydrogenase). No water splits a bond (not a hydrolase) and no ATP joins two molecules (not a ligase).

Problem 11.58

Pyruvate carboxylase catalyzes pyruvate + CO2 + ATP → oxaloacetate + ADP + Pi, and pyruvate decarboxylase catalyzes pyruvate → acetaldehyde + CO2. These enzymes belong, respectively, to the classes

  1. ligase and lyase
  2. lyase and ligase
  3. transferase and hydrolase
  4. ligase and oxidoreductase
Answer
A. The carboxylase uses ATP to join two molecules (pyruvate and CO2) into one, the definition of a ligase; it also needs biotin as its coenzyme. The decarboxylase splits one molecule into two with no water, no ATP, and no redox, the definition of a lyase. Notice that the reactions are almost mirror images, and the names ("carboxylase" vs. "decarboxylase") tell you which way each goes.

Problem 11.59

A patient is prescribed methotrexate. Its effect comes from

  1. irreversible acetylation of cyclooxygenase
  2. competitive inhibition of dihydrofolate reductase, because it resembles folate
  3. denaturation of bacterial proteins
  4. induction of liver CYP enzymes
Answer
B. Methotrexate is a structural look-alike of folate and occupies the active site of dihydrofolate reductase, so the cell cannot regenerate tetrahydrofolate (THF). Without THF to carry one-carbon units, rapidly dividing cells cannot make the nucleotides needed for DNA. Choice A describes aspirin, C describes chlorhexidine or heat, and D describes drugs such as rifampin.

Problem 11.60

A farm worker arrives in the emergency department after a pesticide spill, drooling, sweating, with twitching muscles and difficulty breathing. The pesticide is an organophosphate. (a) Which enzyme has been inhibited and what type of inhibition is it? (b) Why does the level of acetylcholine at the synapse rise? (c) Why will the patient not recover simply by waiting for the pesticide to wash out of the blood?

Answer
(a) Acetylcholinesterase, the hydrolase that breaks down the neurotransmitter acetylcholine (an ester, Chapter 7). Organophosphates phosphorylate the serine –OH in its active site, a covalent and therefore irreversible inhibition. (b) Acetylcholine is released normally but is no longer hydrolyzed, so it accumulates and keeps stimulating glands (saliva, sweat, tears) and muscles (twitching, then exhaustion and paralysis of the breathing muscles). (c) The enzyme is covalently modified; removing free pesticide from the blood does not restore it. Recovery requires the body to make new enzyme (or, in the first hours, a drug that can pull the phosphate off before the bond "ages"), which is why organophosphate poisoning is an emergency.

Problem 11.61

A dental hygienist notes that a patient's gingiva bleed easily on probing, the patient reports fatigue, and the diet history shows almost no fruit or vegetables for several months. (a) Which vitamin deficiency fits, and what is the disease called? (b) Which protein is affected, and what does the vitamin do in making it? (c) Why does the deficiency develop within months rather than years?

Answer
(a) Vitamin C (ascorbic acid); the disease is scurvy. (b) Collagen, the main protein of gingiva, periodontal ligament, and blood-vessel walls. Vitamin C keeps the iron cofactor of prolyl hydroxylase in its reduced (Fe2+) state so the enzyme can add –OH groups to proline residues; without those hydroxyl groups the collagen triple helix is unstable and the tissue is weak, so capillaries leak and gums bleed. (c) Vitamin C is water-soluble and is not stored in fat; the body holds only a small reserve and excretes the excess, so a diet without it empties the supply in a few months. A fat-soluble vitamin such as A or D, stored in the liver and adipose tissue, would take much longer to run out.

Problem 11.62

Sucrose is more cariogenic (cavity-causing) than glucose even though both are fermented to acid by plaque bacteria. Using the enzyme glucosyltransferase of Streptococcus mutans, which builds sticky glucan polymers from sucrose, explain why. Classify the enzyme and name the bond type it forms.

Answer
Glucosyltransferase moves the glucose unit of sucrose onto a growing glucan chain and releases fructose; the glucans are sticky polysaccharides that glue bacteria to the enamel surface and thicken the plaque biofilm, so acid is produced right against the tooth and saliva cannot buffer it away. Glucose alone can be fermented but cannot be used to build these glucans. The enzyme is a transferase (it moves a glucosyl group from one molecule to another), and the bonds it forms are glycosidic linkages (acetals, Chapter 8). The energy stored in sucrose's glycosidic bond is what pays for the transfer; the bacterium needs no ATP.

Problem 11.63

A hospitalized patient with a long history of heavy drinking is to receive an IV infusion of D5W (5% dextrose in water). The nurse checks that thiamine has been given first. Explain the chemistry: which coenzyme is made from thiamine, which reaction needs it, and why giving glucose before thiamine can make the patient worse.

Answer
Thiamine (vitamin B1) is the precursor of thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP), the coenzyme of pyruvate dehydrogenase, which converts pyruvate from glycolysis into acetyl-CoA for the citric acid cycle. Alcohol use disorder depletes thiamine (poor diet, poor absorption). A glucose load drives glycolysis and produces pyruvate that cannot be processed without TPP, so the small remaining thiamine is consumed and the brain, which depends on glucose, is starved of energy; this can precipitate or worsen Wernicke encephalopathy. Giving thiamine first restores the coenzyme before the glucose arrives.

Problem 11.64

Integrative. Aspirin (Chapter 6) is an ester that transfers its acetyl group to a serine –OH in the active site of cyclooxygenase. (a) What functional group is formed on the enzyme, and what small molecule is released? (b) Why is this inhibition irreversible while the binding of a statin to HMG-CoA reductase is not? (c) Aspirin's effect on platelets lasts about a week even though the drug is gone from the blood in hours. Explain using the idea of genetic control.

Answer
(a) The serine –OH becomes an ester (an acetyl ester, O–C(=O)CH3), and salicylic acid, the other half of aspirin, is released. (b) Aspirin forms a covalent bond to the enzyme; a statin binds the active site by shape and weak interactions only, so it comes and goes, and more substrate can displace it. (c) A platelet has no nucleus and cannot transcribe genes, so it cannot make new cyclooxygenase to replace the acetylated enzyme. The effect lasts until the body makes new platelets, about 7–10 days. Nucleated cells recover within a day because they can induce fresh enzyme.

Problem 11.65

Integrative. Insulin is a peptide hormone, yet its main effect on liver enzymes is to remove phosphate groups. Connect the chapters: (a) why can insulin not be taken as a pill (Chapter 10)? (b) What class of enzyme does insulin ultimately activate to dephosphorylate glycogen synthase, and what bond does that enzyme break? (c) What happens to blood glucose in a patient who misses an insulin dose, and which glycogen enzyme is left in the "on" state?

Answer
(a) Insulin is a protein; the proteases of the stomach and intestine (pepsin, trypsin, chymotrypsin) would hydrolyze its peptide bonds, and the fragments are inactive, so it must be injected. (b) A protein phosphatase, a hydrolase. It hydrolyzes the phosphate ester bond between a serine –OH of the enzyme and the phosphate, releasing HPO42−. (c) Without insulin the phosphatases stay quiet while glucagon's kinase pathway continues, so glycogen phosphorylase stays phosphorylated and "on," the liver keeps releasing glucose, and glucose from food is not stored: blood glucose rises (hyperglycemia), and in type 1 diabetes this can progress to diabetic ketoacidosis.
