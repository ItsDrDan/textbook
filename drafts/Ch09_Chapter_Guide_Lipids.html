Learning Objectives

  • Sort a lipid into the hydrolyzable or non-hydrolyzable family and name its class (wax, triacylglycerol, glycerophospholipid, sphingolipid, steroid, fat-soluble vitamin, eicosanoid).
  • Relate fatty acid structure (chain length, saturation, cis double bonds) to melting point, and read the shorthand notation (for example 18:2, omega-6).
  • Locate α, β, and omega (ω) positions on a fatty acid and name the essential fatty acids.
  • Draw a triacylglycerol and predict the products of hydrogenation, hydrolysis, and saponification; explain oxidation (rancidity) and trans fats.
  • Distinguish glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids, steroids, fat-soluble vitamins, and eicosanoids, and give one function of each.
  • Describe the fluid-mosaic lipid bilayer and classify how a molecule crosses it (simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, active transport).

Lipids are the one family of biomolecules defined by a physical property rather than a functional group: a lipid is a biological compound that does not dissolve in water but does dissolve in nonpolar solvents. That single fact explains most of this chapter. It is why fat floats on soup, why cell membranes form, why some vitamins can build up to toxic levels, and why a lipid panel needs a fasting blood draw. The functional groups you already know (esters, amides, alcohols, alkenes) do all of the chemistry.

Key Ideas

1. Fatty acids

  • Long, unbranched carboxylic acids, usually with an even number of carbons (12–20). The long hydrocarbon tail is nonpolar; only the COOH end is polar.
  • Saturated (no C=C): straight chains pack tightly, giving many dispersion-force contacts and high melting points. Solid fats (butter, lard, coconut oil).
  • Unsaturated (one or more C=C, almost always cis): each cis double bond puts a kink in the chain, so the molecules pack poorly. Lower melting points, so they are liquid oils.
  • Carbon labels: the carbon next to the COOH is α (C2), the next is β (C3), and the last carbon (the CH3) is ω. "Omega-3" means the first C=C starts 3 carbons in from the ω end.

Shorthand notation. A fatty acid is written as carbons : double bonds. Stearic acid is 18:0 (18 carbons, saturated); oleic acid is 18:1; linoleic acid is 18:2; arachidonic acid is 20:4. Double-bond positions are counted from the COOH carbon (C1) when a chemist draws the molecule, but the omega number counts from the CH3 end: it is the position of the first double-bonded carbon you reach starting from the ω carbon as number 1. A quick way to get it: omega number = total carbons − the C1-numbered position of the last double bond. For oleic acid the only C=C is C9=C10, so 18 − 9 = 9: omega-9.

Oleic acid, written as a condensed formula, shows both ends at once:

CH3(CH2)7CH=CH(CH2)7COOH   (oleic acid, 18:1, omega-9)

Essential fatty acids. Human enzymes can add double bonds only within the first nine carbons from the COOH end, so we cannot make a C=C at the omega-6 or omega-3 position. Linoleic acid (18:2, omega-6) and α-linolenic acid (18:3, omega-3) must therefore come from the diet; they are essential fatty acids. From them the body builds the longer ones: arachidonic acid from linoleic acid, and EPA and DHA (slowly and inefficiently) from α-linolenic acid. A C=C in the trans geometry does not kink the chain, so a trans fatty acid behaves like a saturated one and melts much higher than its cis isomer (elaidic acid, the trans isomer of oleic acid, melts at about 45 °C).

Professor Tip

Two rules decide every melting-point ranking in this chapter. (1) Among saturated chains, longer chain = more dispersion forces = higher melting point. (2) Among chains of equal length, each cis double bond lowers the melting point a lot. When the two rules pull in opposite directions, a cis double bond usually wins over a few extra carbons: arachidonic acid (20:4) melts far below lauric acid (12:0).

2. Triacylglycerols (fats and oils)

Glycerol + 3 fatty acids → triacylglycerol + 3 H2O (three ester bonds). This is Fischer esterification three times over, so everything you learned about esters in Chapter 6 applies.

  • Fat vs. oil is a melting-point distinction, not a chemical one. A fat is solid at room temperature because most of its fatty acids are saturated (beef tallow, butter, lard). An oil is liquid because most of its chains are unsaturated (olive, corn, canola, fish oils). Natural triacylglycerols are mixed: the three chains are usually different. Coconut oil is the instructive exception: it is almost all saturated, yet it melts near 24 °C because its chains are short (lauric, 12:0).
  • Hydrogenation (H2, Ni catalyst, heat): adds H across C=C, turning liquid oils into semi-solid fats (margarine, shortening). Each mole of C=C consumes one mole of H2. Partial hydrogenation leaves some double bonds, and the catalyst flips a fraction of the remaining cis bonds to trans. These trans fats raise LDL and lower HDL cholesterol, and partially hydrogenated oils have been phased out of U.S. processed foods.
  • Acid or enzyme hydrolysis: → glycerol + 3 fatty acids (what pancreatic lipase does during digestion).
  • Saponification (NaOH or KOH, heat): → glycerol + 3 fatty acid salts (soap). One mole of base is used per ester, so a triacylglycerol needs 3 mol of base.

Why soap cleans. A soap molecule such as sodium stearate, CH3(CH2)16COO− Na+, is amphipathic: it has a polar, water-loving (hydrophilic) carboxylate head and a long nonpolar, water-fearing (hydrophobic) tail. In water, dozens of soap molecules cluster into a micelle, a ball with the tails inside and the heads facing the water. Grease dissolves into the nonpolar core, and the charged surface lets the whole ball wash away. (In hard water, Ca2+ and Mg2+ trade places with Na+ and give an insoluble "soap scum," the same salt-solubility idea from Chapter 6.) Keep the micelle in mind: bile salts and the phospholipid bilayer use the same head-and-tail logic.

3. Membrane lipids, steroids, and eicosanoids

The table gives the four families you must tell apart by their backbone. The sections that follow add detail on each one.

ClassBackboneKey features
Glycerophospholipidsglycerol2 fatty acids + phosphate + amino alcohol (e.g., choline). Polar head with nonpolar tails (amphipathic).
Sphingolipidssphingosine1 fatty acid as an amide. Sphingomyelin is abundant in the myelin sheath around nerves.

Glycerophospholipids. Start with a triacylglycerol and replace the third fatty acid with a phosphate that is also esterified to a small amino alcohol. With choline, HOCH2CH2N+(CH3)3, the product is phosphatidylcholine, better known as lecithin; with ethanolamine or serine you get phosphatidylethanolamine or phosphatidylserine. The phosphate (negative) and the ammonium nitrogen (positive) make a strongly polar head; the two fatty acid chains are nonpolar tails. That head-and-tail design is what makes these molecules build membranes and act as emulsifiers (lecithin in egg yolk holds oil and vinegar together in mayonnaise).

Sphingolipids. The backbone is sphingosine, an 18-carbon amino alcohol, instead of glycerol. One fatty acid is attached through an amide bond (not an ester), and a polar head sits on the terminal OH. In sphingomyelin the head is phosphate + choline, so it looks and behaves much like lecithin; it is the main lipid of the myelin sheath, the insulating wrap around nerve axons. When the head is a sugar instead of a phosphate, the molecule is a glycolipid: a cerebroside carries one monosaccharide (glucose or galactose) and is abundant in brain and nerve membranes. Glycolipids have no phosphate; that is the quickest way to tell them from sphingomyelin.

Membranes: Phospholipids form a bilayer with polar heads facing water on both sides and nonpolar tails inside. Small nonpolar molecules (O2, CO2) diffuse straight through. Ions and glucose need protein channels or carriers (facilitated transport), and moving them against a gradient costs energy (active transport). Section 8 works through the three transport types.

Professor Tip

Lipids are grouped by solubility (they don't dissolve in water), not by one functional group. To classify one, ask: Is there a glycerol? A phosphate? Four fused rings? An ester or an amide? Then follow the table. A second question sorts the storage and membrane lipids: what is the backbone? Glycerol means a glycerolipid (triacylglycerol or glycerophospholipid); sphingosine means a sphingolipid; and the amide bond is the giveaway for sphingosine.

4. Hydrolyzable vs. non-hydrolyzable lipids

  • Hydrolyzable (contain ester or amide links that water can split): waxes, triacylglycerols, glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids.
  • Non-hydrolyzable: steroids, eicosanoids, and the fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K).

The map below is the whole chapter on one page. Hydrolyzable lipids are built by condensation (esterification or amide formation) and can be taken apart by hydrolysis, which is how digestion and saponification work. Non-hydrolyzable lipids have no such link, so they survive boiling with acid or base; the body changes them by oxidation and other reactions instead.

5. Waxes

A wax is an ester of a long-chain fatty acid and a long-chain alcohol, so it has one ester group and two long nonpolar tails. The main ester in beeswax, for example, joins palmitic acid (16 carbons) to a 30-carbon alcohol: CH3(CH2)14COO(CH2)29CH3. Hydrolysis of a wax gives back the fatty acid and the alcohol, one molecule of each, which is the easy way to tell a wax from a fat. The long nonpolar chains make waxes water-repellent and give them a firm, moldable texture: beeswax (candles, cosmetics), carnauba wax (car polish, candy coatings), and lanolin, the wax secreted by sheep's skin that coats wool. Lanolin is a mixture of long-chain esters, including esters of steroid alcohols, and because it softens and seals skin it is the base of many lip balms, nipple creams, and hand creams. Plants coat leaves and fruit with wax (the bloom on a plum) to hold in water.

6. Oxidation of fats (rancidity)

The C=C double bonds in unsaturated fats react with O2 and break into short-chain aldehydes and carboxylic acids that smell and taste "off." Antioxidants such as vitamin E and BHT slow this down, which is why oils are stored cool, dark, and sealed.

A little more detail, without the mechanism. Oxygen attacks the CH2 that sits between two double bonds most easily, so the more polyunsaturated an oil is, the faster it spoils: fish oil and flaxseed oil go rancid in weeks, olive oil (mostly 18:1) lasts months, and a saturated fat like coconut oil lasts longest. The chain is cut at the double bond, and the fragments are volatile aldehydes (hexanal is the "stale oil" smell), which can be oxidized further to carboxylic acids. This is oxidative rancidity. A second kind, hydrolytic rancidity, is simple ester hydrolysis by moisture and microbial lipases; it releases free fatty acids, and in butter the short one, butyric acid (4:0), is the sour, "spoiled butter" odor. Antioxidants stop oxidative rancidity by giving up a hydrogen atom to the reactive intermediates before they can attack another chain. Vitamin E (α-tocopherol, a phenol) does this job inside cell membranes and in the lipid part of foods; the synthetic phenols BHT and BHA do it in packaged foods. Hydrogenation also slows rancidity, which was the original reason shortening and margarine were partially hydrogenated.

7. More about steroids

Cholesterol is converted to bile salts (which emulsify dietary fat for digestion), sex hormones (estrogen, progesterone, testosterone), and corticosteroids (cortisol controls glucose and inflammation; aldosterone controls Na+ and blood pressure). Prednisone and inhaled steroid medications are synthetic corticosteroids.

The steroid nucleus. Every steroid is built on the same frame: three six-membered rings (A, B, C) and one five-membered ring (D) fused edge to edge, 17 carbons in all. Steroids differ only in what hangs off the frame: an OH or a C=O on ring A, a double bond here or there, methyl groups at the ring junctions, and a side chain on C17. There are no esters or amides in the frame, which is why steroids are non-hydrolyzable.

Cholesterol is the most abundant steroid in the body and the only one the body makes in large amounts (about 1 g per day, much of it in the liver). It has one polar OH and an otherwise nonpolar frame, so it is weakly amphipathic and slips between the phospholipid tails of a membrane. There it acts as a fluidity buffer: it keeps the tails from packing into a rigid solid at low temperature and from flying apart at high temperature. Animal plasma membranes hold up to about one cholesterol for every phospholipid. Cholesterol is also the precursor (starting material) of every other steroid in the body and of vitamin D.

Bile salts (for example glycocholate and taurocholate) are made in the liver from cholesterol, stored in the gallbladder, and released into the small intestine after a fatty meal. The steroid frame is tilted so that its OH groups and the charged carboxylate or sulfonate side chain all face one way, giving a flat molecule with a polar face and a nonpolar face: amphipathic, like a soap. Bile salts emulsify dietary fat, breaking large globules into tiny droplets so that water-soluble lipase can reach the ester bonds. Without bile, most fat (and the fat-soluble vitamins dissolved in it) passes through unabsorbed.

Steroid hormones are made from cholesterol in the adrenal glands, ovaries, and testes. Being nonpolar, they cross cell membranes on their own and bind receptors inside the cell. The table gives one job for each; you do not need to draw them.

8. Cell membranes and transport

A cell membrane is a phospholipid bilayer about 7 nm thick, described by the fluid-mosaic model: "fluid" because the phospholipids and cholesterol drift sideways like molecules in a thin oil film, and "mosaic" because proteins are scattered through the bilayer, some spanning it completely. Glycerophospholipids and sphingomyelin supply the bilayer, cholesterol tunes its fluidity, and glycolipids and glycoproteins on the outer face act as cell-identity tags. The nonpolar core is the key to transport: anything small and nonpolar slips through; anything charged or polar and larger than water needs a protein. The table and figure sort the three cases.

Two details matter clinically. The GLUT family of glucose carriers includes GLUT4 in muscle and fat cells, which only moves to the cell surface when insulin signals; that is the molecular reason insulin lowers blood glucose. The Na+/K+-ATPase is in every cell and uses a large share of a resting cell's ATP to keep Na+ low and K+ high inside; nerve impulses and the heartbeat depend on those gradients, which is why serum K+ is watched so closely.

9. Fat-soluble vitamins

Vitamins A, D, E, and K are nonpolar, so they are absorbed with dietary fat (bile salts and lipase required), carried in lipoproteins, and stored in the liver and fat tissue. Storage cuts both ways: a deficiency takes months to appear, but a large daily excess accumulates instead of being excreted in urine as the water-soluble B vitamins and vitamin C are. Fat-soluble vitamins are therefore the ones with real toxicity (hypervitaminosis). Vitamin D is made from cholesterol: ultraviolet light opens ring B of 7-dehydrocholesterol in the skin, so vitamin D is a modified steroid.

10. Eicosanoids

Eicosanoids ("eicosa" = twenty) are 20-carbon signaling lipids made on demand from arachidonic acid (20:4, omega-6), which is first cut out of a membrane phospholipid by the enzyme phospholipase. Unlike hormones, eicosanoids act within seconds on the cells right around them and are destroyed almost immediately, so they are "local hormones." Two enzyme routes give three families:

  • Prostaglandins (made by the COX, or cyclooxygenase, enzymes; the chain is folded into a five-membered ring): promote inflammation, pain, and fever; also protect the stomach lining by stimulating mucus and bicarbonate, help the kidney keep blood flowing, and trigger uterine contractions in labor.
  • Thromboxanes (also from COX, in platelets): trigger platelet aggregation (the first step of a clot) and constrict blood vessels.
  • Leukotrienes (made by lipoxygenase, no ring): cause bronchoconstriction, mucus secretion, and airway swelling in asthma and allergic reactions; drugs such as montelukast block their receptors.

How NSAIDs work (concept only). Aspirin, ibuprofen, and naproxen are nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). They block the COX enzymes, so less arachidonic acid is turned into prostaglandins (less pain, fever, and swelling) and thromboxanes (less clotting). The same block removes the stomach-protecting prostaglandins, which is why long-term NSAID use can cause gastric ulcers and why they are taken with food. Corticosteroids such as cortisol and prednisone act one step earlier: they suppress the release of arachidonic acid itself, so they lower all three eicosanoid families, including leukotrienes. That is the chemical reason an inhaled corticosteroid controls asthma while an NSAID does not.

Health Career Connection

  • Dental – post-extraction pain: NSAIDs such as ibuprofen and aspirin block the COX enzyme that turns arachidonic acid into prostaglandins. This reduces pain and swelling after extractions and periodontal surgery. Aspirin also blocks thromboxane, which is why patients on aspirin may bleed more during procedures.
  • Respiratory therapy and nursing – lung surfactant: A phospholipid surfactant (mainly dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine) keeps alveoli from collapsing. Premature infants who cannot make enough are given surfactant through the endotracheal tube.
  • Nursing – cholesterol and fats: Diets high in saturated and trans fats raise LDL cholesterol and the risk of arterial plaque. Omega-3 fatty acids (fish oils) are associated with heart health.
  • Nursing – lipid emulsions: IV lipid nutrition and the anesthetic propofol are oil-in-water emulsions stabilized by phospholipids from egg yolk. That is why they look milky white.
  • Dental hygiene – chlorhexidine: Chlorhexidine rinse, the professional "gold standard" for controlling plaque biofilm, kills bacteria by disrupting their lipid cell membranes. Brown staining of teeth is a known side effect of long-term use.

Further reading: M. Epple and J. Enax, "The Chemistry of Dental Care," ChemistryViews (Part 3).

Clinical Connection: Nursing

A lipid panel is drawn after a 9–12 hour fast and reports four numbers in mg/dL. Cholesterol and triacylglycerols travel inside lipoproteins, particles with a phospholipid-and-protein shell. Typical targets: total cholesterol below 200; LDL (the particle that delivers cholesterol to artery walls) below 100; HDL (the particle that returns cholesterol to the liver) 60 or higher is protective, below 40 is low; triglycerides (the lab name for triacylglycerols) below 150. Many labs calculate LDL as total cholesterol − HDL − triglycerides/5. Hyperlipidemia drives atherosclerosis: LDL lodged in an artery wall is oxidized and built into cholesterol-rich plaque. Statins inhibit HMG-CoA reductase, the enzyme that controls the liver's cholesterol synthesis, so the liver pulls more LDL from the blood; prescription omega-3 (EPA/DHA) lowers triglycerides. Low-dose aspirin is antiplatelet because it blocks COX in platelets, which cannot make new enzyme, so thromboxane stays low for the platelet's 7–10 day life. Vitamin K activates clotting factors; warfarin blocks vitamin K recycling, so a sudden change in leafy-green intake changes the INR. Because fat-soluble vitamins are stored, hypervitaminosis A and D are real risks of high-dose supplements. Bile is a cholesterol solution kept dissolved by bile salts and lecithin; when cholesterol outruns them it crystallizes as gallstones. Blocked bile flow or lipase deficiency causes steatorrhea (bulky, pale, floating stools of unabsorbed fat) and loss of vitamins A, D, E, and K.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Plaque biofilm bacteria are wrapped in a phospholipid bilayer; essential-oil rinses (thymol, eucalyptol, menthol, methyl salicylate) dissolve into that bilayer and disrupt it, and chlorhexidine, a positively charged bis-biguanide, binds the negatively charged bacterial surface and makes the membrane leak. Your local anesthetics (lidocaine, articaine, mepivacaine) are amines that exist as a lipid-soluble uncharged base and a water-soluble protonated cation. Only the uncharged base crosses the nerve membrane. An abscess is acidic, which pushes the drug toward the charged form, so anesthesia near inflamed tissue is less reliable. Drug from inhaled corticosteroids deposits in the mouth, suppresses local immunity, and allows oral candidiasis (thrush); teach patients to rinse and spit after each dose. Waxes are tools of the trade: baseplate, bite-registration, and orthodontic relief waxes (paraffin hydrocarbons blended with beeswax esters) soften near mouth temperature, and lanolin lip balm protects patients' lips during long appointments. Dietary omega-3 fatty acids are the raw material for resolvins, signals that help turn inflammation off; evidence that they reduce periodontal inflammation is still developing. Vitamin D and calcium support the alveolar bone that anchors every tooth; low vitamin D is associated with periodontal bone loss and poorer healing after extractions.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Melting points

Problem. Stearic acid (18:0) melts at 69 °C, and oleic acid (18:1, cis) melts at about 13 °C. Explain.

Solution

Both have 18 carbons. The cis double bond in oleic acid kinks the chain, so the molecules cannot line up closely. Fewer dispersion-force contacts mean a lower melting point, so oleic acid is a liquid at room temperature.

Check. Same chain length, so only the double-bond rule applies; one cis C=C should drop the melting point by tens of degrees, and it does (56 °C).

Example 2: Omega numbering

Problem. Linoleic acid is 18 carbons with cis double bonds starting at C9 and C12 (numbered from the COOH carbon). Is it an omega-3 or omega-6?

Solution

The double bond farthest from the COOH starts at C12, i.e., between C12 and C13. Counting from the ω end, C18 is ω1, so C13 is ω6. Omega-6.

Check. Shortcut: 18 − 12 = 6. Try it on α-linolenic acid (18:3; C9, C12, C15): 18 − 15 = 3, omega-3. And on arachidonic acid (20:4; C5, C8, C11, C14): 20 − 14 = 6, omega-6. All three agree with the table in Section 1.

Example 3: Saponification

Problem. What forms when glyceryl tripalmitate (tripalmitin) is heated with excess NaOH?

Solution

All three ester bonds are hydrolyzed in base: glycerol + 3 sodium palmitate (CH3(CH2)14COO−Na+), which is soap.

Check. Three esters need three OH−; the products are a salt (carboxylate), not a free acid, because the solution is basic.

Example 4: Classify the lipid

Problem. A lipid contains glycerol, two fatty acids, a phosphate, and choline. What is it, and where is it found?

Solution

A glycerophospholipid (phosphatidylcholine, also called lecithin). It is a major component of cell membranes, and it is the emulsifier in egg yolk.

Check. Backbone glycerol rules out a sphingolipid; the phosphate rules out a triacylglycerol and a glycolipid; two tails plus a charged head makes it amphipathic, as a membrane lipid should be.

Example 5: Identify a fatty acid from its notation and classify it

Problem. A fish-oil supplement lists a fatty acid as 20:5 with double bonds at C5, C8, C11, C14, and C17. Name it, give its omega class, say whether it is saturated or unsaturated, and predict whether it is a solid or a liquid at room temperature.

Solution

Twenty carbons and five double bonds is EPA (eicosapentaenoic acid). The last double bond begins at C17, so the omega number is 20 − 17 = 3: omega-3. Five C=C makes it polyunsaturated. Five cis kinks in a 20-carbon chain prevent any close packing, so it is a liquid (oil) at room temperature; the table lists a melting point near −54 °C.

Check. The double bonds are spaced three carbons apart (5, 8, 11, 14, 17), the usual pattern for polyunsaturated fatty acids; counting three carbons in from C20 lands on C17, confirming omega-3. Fish that live in cold water need membrane lipids that stay fluid, which fits a very low melting point.

Example 6: Rank by melting point

Problem. Rank these fatty acids from lowest to highest melting point: palmitic acid (16:0), α-linolenic acid (18:3), palmitoleic acid (16:1), lauric acid (12:0).

Solution

Sort by kinks first, then by length. Most kinks: α-linolenic (three cis C=C) is lowest. Next: palmitoleic (one cis C=C). The two saturated acids come last, and the shorter chain melts lower: lauric (12 carbons) below palmitic (16 carbons).

α-linolenic (18:3) < palmitoleic (16:1) < lauric (12:0) < palmitic (16:0)

Check. Actual values: about −11, 0, 44, and 63 °C. Notice that α-linolenic acid has the longest chain yet the lowest melting point: three cis double bonds outweigh six extra carbons.

Example 7: Products of saponifying a mixed triacylglycerol

Problem. A triacylglycerol carries palmitic acid on C1 of glycerol, oleic acid on C2, and stearic acid on C3. It is heated with excess KOH. Write every product, state how many moles of KOH react per mole of fat, and explain why the products dissolve in water when the starting fat did not.

Solution

Each of the three ester bonds is split by one OH−, so 3 mol KOH react per mole of fat. The alcohol part of every ester is the same glycerol, so one glycerol forms. The acid parts leave as potassium salts, one of each:

  • potassium palmitate, CH3(CH2)14COO− K+
  • potassium oleate, CH3(CH2)7CH=CH(CH2)7COO− K+
  • potassium stearate, CH3(CH2)16COO− K+

The fat was a large nonpolar molecule. Glycerol has three OH groups and hydrogen-bonds with water, and each carboxylate salt is ionic at one end: they dissolve (the salts form micelles). Base does not touch the C=C of oleate, so it survives as a cis double bond.

Check. Count: 1 fat + 3 KOH → 1 glycerol + 3 salts, four product molecules. Potassium soaps are softer and more soluble than sodium soaps, which is why liquid soaps use KOH.

Example 8: Classify the transport

Problem. For each case, name the transport type and justify it in one sentence. (a) After a meal, glucose moves from the blood into a muscle cell through a GLUT4 carrier that insulin has brought to the surface. (b) A steroid hormone such as cortisol enters a liver cell. (c) A kidney tubule cell keeps its internal Na+ low even though Na+ is high in the fluid around it.

Solution

(a) Facilitated diffusion. Glucose is polar and needs a carrier, but it is moving from high (blood) to low (cell), so no ATP is used; insulin only supplies the door. (b) Simple diffusion. Cortisol is a nonpolar steroid and dissolves through the bilayer on its own, down its gradient. (c) Active transport. Keeping Na+ low inside against a steady inward leak means pumping it out against its gradient, which only the ATP-driven Na+/K+-ATPase can do.

Check. Ask two questions in order: does it need a protein (polar or charged = yes), and does it go against the gradient (yes = ATP)? Case (a) answers yes/no, (b) no/no, (c) yes/yes, which are the three rows of the table.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Assuming more carbons always beats double bonds. Compare chain lengths and the number of cis double bonds.
  • Counting omega positions from the COOH end. Omega is counted from the CH3 end.
  • Forgetting that saponification gives fatty acid salts, not fatty acids.
  • Thinking cholesterol is only harmful. It is required for membranes and to make hormones, bile salts, and vitamin D.
  • Treating a trans fatty acid like a cis one. A trans C=C does not bend the chain, so a trans fat packs and melts like a saturated fat; "unsaturated" does not automatically mean "low melting."
  • Confusing a wax with a fat. Both are esters, but a wax has one ester (fatty acid + long-chain alcohol) and a fat has three (fatty acids + glycerol). Hydrolyzing a wax gives one acid and one alcohol; hydrolyzing a fat gives three acids and glycerol.
  • Calling facilitated diffusion "active" because it uses a protein. Energy, not the protein, is the test: if the solute moves down its gradient, no ATP is spent, and it is facilitated diffusion.
  • Mixing up the sphingolipid bond. The fatty acid in sphingomyelin is attached by an amide bond, not an ester, because sphingosine has an amine; it is still hydrolyzable.

Practice

Exercise 1

Which has the higher melting point: a triacylglycerol made from three stearic acids or one made from three linoleic acids?

Answer
Tristearin (all saturated, straight chains pack tightly). Trilinolein is an oil.

Exercise 2

What is the product of completely hydrogenating triolein?

Answer
Tristearin (the oleic chains, 18:1, become stearic chains, 18:0)

Exercise 3

Which can diffuse directly through a lipid bilayer: CO2, Na+, glucose?

Answer
Only CO2. Na+ and glucose are polar or charged and need protein transporters.

Exercise 4

Which lipid class do prostaglandins belong to, and what drug class blocks their synthesis?

Answer
Eicosanoids; NSAIDs (aspirin, ibuprofen, naproxen) block the COX enzyme.

Exercise 5

Classify as hydrolyzable or non-hydrolyzable: beeswax, cholesterol, sphingomyelin, prostaglandin E2.

Answer
Hydrolyzable: beeswax, sphingomyelin. Non-hydrolyzable: cholesterol, prostaglandin E2.

Exercise 6

A fatty acid is listed as 22:1 with its double bond at C13. Give its omega class and predict whether it melts above or below oleic acid (18:1, 13 °C).

Answer
22 − 13 = 9, so omega-9. One cis kink in both, but the 22-carbon chain has more dispersion-force contact, so it melts higher than oleic acid (erucic acid, about 33 °C).

Exercise 7

Which fat-soluble vitamin is (a) a modified steroid made in the skin, (b) a membrane antioxidant, (c) required for clotting factors, (d) the source of the light-absorbing retinal in the eye?

Answer
(a) vitamin D; (b) vitamin E; (c) vitamin K; (d) vitamin A.

Exercise 8

A patient takes a corticosteroid inhaler and a daily low-dose aspirin. Which eicosanoid family does each drug lower, and by acting on what step?

Answer
Aspirin blocks COX, lowering prostaglandins and thromboxanes (the antiplatelet effect comes from thromboxane). The corticosteroid acts earlier, suppressing release of arachidonic acid, so it lowers prostaglandins, thromboxanes, and leukotrienes; the leukotriene effect is what helps asthma.
