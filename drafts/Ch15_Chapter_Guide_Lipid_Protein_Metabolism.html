Learning Objectives

  • Describe how dietary fats and proteins are digested and absorbed, naming the enzymes and their classes.
  • Trace glycerol into glycolysis or gluconeogenesis and explain how stored fat is mobilized.
  • Describe fatty acid activation, the carnitine shuttle, and the four reactions of β-oxidation (reaction type, enzyme class, coenzyme).
  • Calculate the number of β-oxidation cycles, acetyl CoA, NADH, FADH2, and ATP from a saturated or unsaturated fatty acid.
  • Explain when and why ketone bodies form, and the difference between ketosis and ketoacidosis.
  • Contrast fatty acid synthesis (lipogenesis) with β-oxidation.
  • Describe transamination, oxidative deamination, and the urea cycle, including enzyme classes and coenzymes.
  • Classify amino acid carbon skeletons as glucogenic or ketogenic, list the essential amino acids, and explain nitrogen balance.
  • Draw the "hub" molecules where carbohydrate, lipid, and protein metabolism meet.

Key Ideas

1. Digesting fats

Dietary fat arrives in the small intestine as large, water-insoluble droplets of triacylglycerol (TAG), the triester of glycerol and three fatty acids you met in Chapter 9. Digesting it is a two-part problem: the droplets must be broken into small ones so that a water-soluble enzyme can reach them, and then the ester bonds must be hydrolyzed.

  • Emulsification. Bile salts, made in the liver from cholesterol and stored in the gallbladder, are detergents: a steroid ring that dissolves in fat plus an ionic tail that dissolves in water. They coat the fat and break it into tiny droplets with a huge total surface area. No bonds are broken yet; this is a physical change.
  • Hydrolysis. Pancreatic lipase (a hydrolase) cuts the two outer ester bonds of each TAG, releasing 2 fatty acids + 1 monoacylglycerol (a 2-monoacylglycerol, with the middle fatty acid still attached). Compare this with saponification in Chapter 9: same ester hydrolysis, but at body pH with an enzyme instead of hot NaOH.
  • Micelles and absorption. The fatty acids and monoacylglycerols cluster with bile salts into micelles, small enough to deliver their cargo to the intestinal cells. Inside the cells the TAGs are rebuilt and packaged with protein into chylomicrons, the largest lipoproteins, which enter the lymph and then the blood. (Short- and medium-chain fatty acids, with fewer than about 12 carbons, skip this step and go straight to the portal blood.)
  • Delivery. At capillaries in muscle and adipose tissue, lipoprotein lipase (LPL), another hydrolase, hydrolyzes the chylomicron TAG again so that fatty acids can enter the cells to be burned or stored.

2. β-Oxidation (in the mitochondria)

β-Oxidation is the spiral of reactions that chops a fatty acid into two-carbon acetyl CoA units. Before it can start, the fatty acid must be activated (attached to CoA, Key Idea 6) and carried into the mitochondrial matrix by the carnitine shuttle (also Key Idea 6). The pathway gets its name from the β carbon: the second carbon from the carbonyl (the α carbon is the first). Each cycle oxidizes that carbon from CH2 to C=O and then breaks the bond between the α and β carbons.

  • Activation: the fatty acid is attached to CoA. This costs 2 ATP (one time per fatty acid).
  • Each cycle removes 2 carbons as acetyl CoA and produces 1 FADH2 + 1 NADH.
  • For a fatty acid with n carbons (even): cycles = n/2 − 1, acetyl CoA = n/2. The last cycle splits a 4-carbon piece into two acetyl CoA, which is why there is one fewer cycle than acetyl CoA.
  • ATP total = (acetyl CoA × 10) + (NADH × 2.5) + (FADH2 × 1.5) − 2

The four reactions of one cycle follow a pattern you have seen in the citric acid cycle (succinate → fumarate → malate → oxaloacetate): oxidize to a C=C, add water across it, oxidize the new alcohol to a ketone, then a fourth step that is unique to β-oxidation, cleave with CoA. 3. Ketone bodies

When carbohydrate is scarce (fasting, very low-carb diets) or cannot be used (uncontrolled diabetes), the liver makes more acetyl CoA than the citric acid cycle can handle. It converts the extra into ketone bodies: acetoacetate, β-hydroxybutyrate, and acetone. The heart, muscles, and (after a few days) the brain can burn them, but acetoacetate and β-hydroxybutyrate are acids. In large amounts they lower blood pH: ketoacidosis.

Here is the chemistry behind that paragraph. Acetyl CoA can enter the citric acid cycle only by condensing with oxaloacetate. During fasting or insulin deficiency the liver is busy making glucose, and gluconeogenesis pulls oxaloacetate out of the cycle. Meanwhile, hormone-sensitive lipase is flooding the liver with fatty acids, and β-oxidation turns them into acetyl CoA faster than the oxaloacetate-starved cycle can accept it. In the liver mitochondria, two acetyl CoA join (thiolase in reverse) and a third is added to form HMG-CoA, which a lyase splits into acetoacetate. Acetoacetate is then either reduced (NADH) to β-hydroxybutyrate or slowly loses CO2 to give acetone, which is volatile and leaves in the breath. This whole process is ketogenesis.

  • Who uses them? The liver makes ketone bodies but lacks the enzyme to activate them, so it exports them. Heart and skeletal muscle use them readily; after two to three days of fasting the brain, which cannot burn fatty acids, gets most of its energy from ketone bodies, sparing muscle protein that would otherwise be broken down for gluconeogenesis.
  • Ketosis vs. ketoacidosis. Ketosis is a modest, controlled rise in blood ketone bodies (fasting, ketogenic diets, prolonged exercise); the blood buffers handle it. Ketoacidosis is the runaway version: in diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) there is no insulin to stop lipolysis or ketogenesis, so acids accumulate faster than bicarbonate can buffer them and blood pH falls below 7.35 (acidosis).

4. Amino acid metabolism

  • Transamination: the amino group moves from an amino acid to α-ketoglutarate, making glutamate and a new α-keto acid (alanine → pyruvate). The enzymes involved are ALT and AST.
  • Oxidative deamination: glutamate releases its nitrogen as NH4+ (toxic) and regenerates α-ketoglutarate.
  • Urea cycle (liver): converts NH4+ and CO2 into urea, which the kidneys excrete.
  • Leftover carbon skeletons are glucogenic (can become glucose via pyruvate or cycle intermediates) or ketogenic (become acetyl CoA or ketone bodies).

Unlike glucose and fat, amino acids are not stored. Whatever the body does not need for protein synthesis is broken down in two parts: the nitrogen (the amino group) is removed and disposed of as urea, and the carbon skeleton is burned or converted to glucose or fat. Let's follow each.

Step 1: Transamination. An aminotransferase (also called a transaminase; enzyme class transferase) swaps the amino group of an amino acid with the keto oxygen of an α-keto acid. The usual acceptor is α-ketoglutarate, the citric acid cycle intermediate, which becomes glutamate. The coenzyme is pyridoxal phosphate (PLP), the active form of vitamin B6, which holds the amino group between the two halves of the reaction. The reaction is reversible, which is why the same enzymes also make nonessential amino acids (Key Idea 9).

The two aminotransferases you will see on lab reports are ALT (alanine aminotransferase: alanine + α-ketoglutarate → pyruvate + glutamate) and AST (aspartate aminotransferase: aspartate + α-ketoglutarate → oxaloacetate + glutamate). Transamination funnels the nitrogen of almost every amino acid into one molecule, glutamate.

Step 2: Oxidative deamination. In the mitochondria, glutamate dehydrogenase (an oxidoreductase, using NAD+ or NADP+) oxidizes glutamate and releases its nitrogen as free ammonium ion, NH4+, regenerating α-ketoglutarate for another round of transamination:

glutamate + NAD+ + H2O → α-ketoglutarate + NH4+ + NADH + H+

Ammonia is toxic, especially to the brain, so it cannot be allowed to build up in the blood. The liver converts it to urea.

Step 3: The urea cycle. The cycle runs only in the liver, starting in the mitochondria and finishing in the cytosol. It takes in two nitrogen atoms, one from NH4+ and one from aspartate, plus one carbon from CO2 (as bicarbonate), and releases urea, H2N–CO–NH2, a small, neutral, very water-soluble molecule that the kidneys filter into urine. The carrier molecule ornithine is regenerated each turn, like oxaloacetate in the citric acid cycle. The cost is 4 ATP-equivalents per urea (2 ATP → 2 ADP, and 1 ATP → AMP + PPi). "L" for ketogenic: Leucine and Lysine are the only two amino acids that are purely ketogenic. Everything else can make at least some glucose.

5. Fat mobilization and glycerol

When energy is needed, glucagon and epinephrine activate hormone-sensitive lipase in fat tissue, which releases fatty acids and glycerol into the blood. Glycerol goes to the liver, where it becomes dihydroxyacetone phosphate, an intermediate that can enter glycolysis or gluconeogenesis.

The signal travels the same way glycogen breakdown is switched on in Chapter 13: the hormone binds a receptor on the fat cell, cAMP rises, protein kinase A phosphorylates hormone-sensitive lipase, and the phosphorylated enzyme is the active form (covalent modification, Chapter 11). Insulin does the opposite. The fatty acids are not water-soluble, so they travel in the blood bound to the protein albumin; the glycerol, a small triol, simply dissolves. Section 15.6 covers storage and mobilization in detail.

Glycerol is worth a closer look because it is the only part of a fat that can become glucose. In the liver (fat cells lack the first enzyme):

  1. Glycerol kinase (a transferase; ATP → ADP) phosphorylates the end carbon to make glycerol 3-phosphate.
  2. Glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (an oxidoreductase; NAD+ → NADH) oxidizes the middle 2° alcohol to a ketone, giving dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP).

DHAP is a glycolysis intermediate, so it can go "downhill" to pyruvate when energy is needed or "uphill" to glucose during fasting. Two glycerols make one glucose. The amount is small (glycerol is about one-tenth of a fat by mass), but during a long fast it is a steady contribution to blood glucose.

6. Getting fatty acids into the mitochondria

After activation (fatty acyl CoA), the fatty acid cannot cross the inner mitochondrial membrane directly. The carnitine shuttle carries it across, and it is reattached to CoA inside.

Activation. A carboxylic acid is unreactive, so the first job is to convert it into a thioester. Acyl CoA synthetase (a ligase) on the outer mitochondrial membrane joins the fatty acid to coenzyme A:

R–COO− + CoA–SH + ATP → R–CO–S–CoA + AMP + PPi

ATP is split to AMP, not ADP, and the pyrophosphate (PPi) is then hydrolyzed to two phosphates. Two high-energy bonds are spent, so we count the cost as 2 ATP-equivalents per fatty acid, no matter how long the chain.

The carnitine shuttle. Long-chain acyl CoA cannot pass the inner mitochondrial membrane. Carnitine palmitoyltransferase I (CPT-I) on the outer membrane swaps the CoA for carnitine, a small molecule made from lysine; a translocase carries acyl-carnitine across; and CPT-II on the matrix side gives the acyl group back to CoA inside. CPT-I is the control point: malonyl CoA, the first intermediate of fatty acid synthesis, inhibits it, so a cell that is building fat does not burn it at the same time. Medium-chain fatty acids (6 to 12 carbons) do not need the shuttle, a fact with clinical uses (see the Nursing box).

7. Unsaturated fatty acids

A double bond that is already present means one FAD oxidation step is skipped, so each C=C gives one fewer FADH2 (1.5 fewer ATP).

There is a wrinkle: natural double bonds are cis and often fall on the wrong carbon, whereas β-oxidation needs a trans double bond between the α and β carbons. An enoyl CoA isomerase (an isomerase) moves and flips the bond into position, and for polyunsaturated acids such as linoleic acid a reductase (using NADPH) is also needed. At the level of this course, count one fewer FADH2 per pre-existing double bond; oleic acid (18 C, one C=C) gives 120 − 1.5 = 118.5 ATP. Odd-chain fatty acids (rare in the diet) end with a three-carbon propionyl CoA instead of acetyl CoA, which a vitamin B12-dependent pathway converts to succinyl CoA, a citric acid cycle intermediate.

8. Lipogenesis vs. β-oxidation

When you eat more carbohydrate than you can burn or store as glycogen, the liver turns the extra acetyl CoA into fatty acids (lipogenesis, or fatty acid synthesis) and ships them to adipose tissue as VLDL. The pathway is not simply β-oxidation in reverse. It runs in a different compartment, uses a different carrier and a different coenzyme, and is controlled by the opposite hormones, which lets the cell run one direction without the other.

9. Protein digestion, nitrogen balance, and essential amino acids

  • Pepsin (stomach) and trypsin and chymotrypsin (small intestine) hydrolyze proteins into amino acids, which join the body's amino acid pool along with amino acids from normal protein turnover.
  • Positive nitrogen balance (more N taken in than lost): growth, pregnancy, wound healing. Negative: starvation, severe burns, serious illness.
  • Nine amino acids are essential (must come from food): His, Ile, Leu, Lys, Met, Phe, Thr, Trp, Val. The rest can be made, largely by transamination.
  • Everything connects: acetyl CoA, pyruvate, and citric acid cycle intermediates link carbohydrate, fat, and protein metabolism.

Digestion, step by step. Protein digestion is peptide-bond hydrolysis (Chapter 10), done by proteases (all hydrolases) in stages:

  • Stomach. Hydrochloric acid (pH about 2) denatures the protein, unfolding it so the enzymes can reach the backbone, and converts the inactive pepsinogen into pepsin, which works best at that low pH and cuts proteins into large fragments.
  • Small intestine. The pancreas secretes trypsin, chymotrypsin, and carboxypeptidase as inactive zymogens (trypsinogen, chymotrypsinogen, procarboxypeptidase) so that they do not digest the pancreas itself; an intestinal enzyme activates trypsin, and trypsin activates the rest. Bicarbonate raises the pH to about 8, where these enzymes work. Trypsin and chymotrypsin cut inside the chain at specific amino acids; carboxypeptidase trims from the C-terminus.
  • Brush border and absorption. Peptidases on the surface of the intestinal cells finish the job, producing free amino acids and a few di- and tripeptides, which are absorbed by active transport (Chapter 9) and carried in the portal vein to the liver.

The amino acid pool and turnover. The free amino acids in blood and cells, about 100 g in an adult, make up the amino acid pool. It is fed by diet, by the breakdown of body protein (protein turnover, roughly 300 g per day, since every protein is eventually replaced), and by synthesis of nonessential amino acids. It is drained by protein synthesis, by the making of nitrogen-containing molecules such as heme, nucleotides, and neurotransmitters, and by degradation to urea plus carbon skeletons.

Nitrogen balance. Because the body does not store protein, nitrogen in (dietary protein) and nitrogen out (urea, plus smaller losses in feces, skin, and hair) are normally equal: nitrogen balance. Positive balance means the body is building more protein than it breaks down: growth, pregnancy, recovery from illness or surgery, muscle building. Negative balance means net loss of body protein: starvation or inadequate dietary protein, a diet missing even one essential amino acid, severe burns, sepsis (a body-wide infection), major trauma or surgery, immobilization (bed rest, casting), and treatment with corticosteroid drugs, all of which drive muscle breakdown.

Essential vs. nonessential. The body can make the carbon skeleton of eleven amino acids from glycolysis and citric acid cycle intermediates, then add nitrogen by transamination; these are nonessential. The nine it cannot make are essential and must come from food. The mnemonic PVT TIM HaLL lists them: Phenylalanine, Valine, Threonine, Tryptophan, Isoleucine, Methionine, Histidine, Leucine, Lysine (the lower-case "a" is a reminder that arginine is conditionally essential: the urea cycle makes it, but not fast enough in infants or during severe illness). Tyrosine and cysteine are also conditionally essential, because tyrosine is made from phenylalanine and cysteine's sulfur comes from methionine.

10. One body, one metabolism: where the pathways overlap

You have now met three chapters of pathways. Step back and notice that they share a handful of molecules. Pyruvate connects glucose, alanine (and other glucogenic amino acids), and glycerol (through DHAP). Acetyl CoA is where glucose, every fatty acid, and the ketogenic amino acids all end up, and it is the starting material for fatty acids, ketone bodies, and cholesterol. Oxaloacetate and α-ketoglutarate are both citric acid cycle intermediates and the keto-acid partners of aspartate and glutamate, so the cycle doubles as the on-ramp for amino acid carbon and the source of carbon for nonessential amino acids. One arrow is missing on purpose: there is no pathway from acetyl CoA back to pyruvate, so fatty acids cannot be turned into glucose. That single fact explains why the brain needs ketone bodies in a fast and why gluconeogenesis must run on glycerol, lactate, and amino acids.

Health Career Connection

  • Nursing – diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA): Without insulin, cells cannot use glucose and fat breakdown runs unchecked. Signs include fruity (acetone) breath, deep rapid Kussmaul breathing (the body blowing off CO2 to raise pH), frequent urination, and dehydration. DKA is a medical emergency, and patients in a dental chair can show these signs too.
  • Nursing – lab values: ALT and AST are the transamination enzymes, so high blood levels suggest liver damage. Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) reflects the urea cycle and kidney clearance. High ammonia in liver failure can cause confusion (hepatic encephalopathy).
  • Dental hygiene – PKU and sugar-free products: People with phenylketonuria (PKU) cannot convert phenylalanine to tyrosine. The sweetener aspartame contains phenylalanine, which is why sugar-free gums and some mouth products carry a "Phenylketonurics: contains phenylalanine" warning. Xylitol-sweetened products are an alternative.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Ketones. Urine ketone strips detect acetoacetate, but in DKA most of the acid is β-hydroxybutyrate, so a blood β-hydroxybutyrate level is the better measure; as a patient recovers and β-hydroxybutyrate is oxidized back to acetoacetate, the urine strip can briefly look worse while the patient is getting better. Starvation ketosis gives modest ketones with normal glucose; DKA gives high ketones and hyperglycemia (high blood glucose) with a low bicarbonate. Carnitine. Patients with carnitine deficiency cannot move long-chain fatty acids into the mitochondria and become hypoglycemic and weak when fasting; medium-chain triglyceride (MCT) oil helps because 8- and 10-carbon fatty acids enter without the shuttle. Liver and kidney markers. ALT and AST are intracellular enzymes; they appear in blood only when liver cells (or, for AST, heart and muscle cells) are damaged and leak. Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) rises when the kidneys cannot clear urea. In liver failure the urea cycle fails and NH4+ rises (hyperammonemia), causing hepatic encephalopathy; inherited urea cycle disorders produce the same chemistry in infants, which is why both are managed partly by limiting dietary protein. Protein needs. Burns, sepsis, and ICU stays put patients in negative nitrogen balance; dietitians raise protein intake and, when the gut cannot be used, supply amino acids by total parenteral nutrition (TPN), an IV infusion of amino acids, glucose, and lipid.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Breath tells a story. A sweet, fruity, nail-polish-remover odor is acetone: ketone bodies are being made in quantity. In a patient who is also thirsty, urinating often, breathing deeply, or confused, it may signal DKA and needs urgent referral; in a fasting or low-carb patient with no other signs, it is simple ketosis. A urine-like or ammonia odor (uremic fetor) suggests kidney failure: urea accumulates in saliva, and bacterial urease converts it to ammonia. Diabetes and the periodontium. Poorly controlled diabetes (high HbA1c) worsens periodontitis and slows healing after scaling and root planing, and periodontal inflammation in turn raises blood glucose, so your care is part of diabetes care. Liver disease. The liver makes the clotting proteins and metabolizes amide local anesthetics, so patients with hepatic disease may bleed more after instrumentation and clear lidocaine or articaine more slowly; the dentist may limit the dose. Nutrition and the oral tissues. Protein malnutrition (negative nitrogen balance) means poor collagen synthesis and poor healing of the gingiva after periodontal therapy. Deficiencies of the transamination coenzyme vitamin B6, or of B12 or folate, show up early in fast-dividing oral tissues as glossitis (smooth, sore, red tongue) and angular cheilitis (cracks at the corners of the mouth), so the mouth can be the first place a nutritional problem is seen.

Worked Examples

Example 1: β-Oxidation of lauric acid (12 C)

Find the number of β-oxidation cycles, acetyl CoA, NADH, FADH2, and the total ATP.

Solution

  • Cycles = 12/2 − 1 = 5, so 5 NADH and 5 FADH2.
  • Acetyl CoA = 12/2 = 6.
  • ATP = 6(10) + 5(2.5) + 5(1.5) − 2 = 60 + 12.5 + 7.5 − 2 = 78 ATP.

Check: Each extra pair of carbons on a saturated chain is worth 14 ATP, so 78 + 14 + 14 = 106 for palmitate (Example 4). The numbers agree.

Example 2: Transamination

Write the products when alanine transfers its amino group to α-ketoglutarate.

Solution

Alanine + α-ketoglutarate → pyruvate + glutamate (catalyzed by ALT). Pyruvate can become glucose, so alanine is glucogenic.

Check: The amino acid lost its NH2 and gained a C=O; the keto acid did the reverse. Nothing else changed, and no nitrogen was released.

Example 3: Comparing fuels

A 6-carbon fatty acid (hexanoic acid) and glucose both have 6 carbons. Which yields more ATP?

Solution

Hexanoic acid: 2 cycles, 3 acetyl CoA → 3(10) + 2(2.5) + 2(1.5) − 2 = 36 ATP. Glucose = 32 ATP. The fatty acid wins because its carbons are more reduced (fewer oxygens), so there is more to oxidize. This is the same reason fat gives 9 kcal/g.

Check: C6H12O2 versus C6H12O6: four fewer oxygens means four more C–H bonds to hand to NAD+ and FAD.

Example 5: Classify the reactions of the urea cycle

Problem. For each urea-cycle step, name the enzyme class and say which of the two nitrogen atoms of urea enters at that step, if any.

Solution. (1) Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I joins NH4+ and bicarbonate at the cost of 2 ATP: a ligase; the first nitrogen enters here. (2) Ornithine transcarbamoylase moves the carbamoyl group onto ornithine: a transferase; no new nitrogen. (3) Argininosuccinate synthetase joins citrulline to aspartate using ATP: a ligase; the second nitrogen (aspartate's amino group) enters here. (4) Argininosuccinase splits off fumarate without water or oxidation: a lyase. (5) Arginase uses water to split arginine into urea and ornithine: a hydrolase.

Check. Two ligases spend ATP (2 ATP at step 1, and ATP → AMP at step 3, which counts double), giving the 4 ATP-equivalents quoted for the cycle. Two nitrogens in, one urea out, and ornithine returned, so the cycle balances.

Example 6: Predict the nitrogen balance

Problem. A 24-year-old with a 30% body-surface burn is in the ICU, on a high-protein feeding plan. A 7-year-old is healthy and growing. A 60-year-old is on bed rest after a hip fracture and is taking a corticosteroid. For each, predict the nitrogen balance and name the metabolic process that explains it.

Solution. Burn patient: negative. Stress hormones (cortisol, glucagon, epinephrine) drive muscle protein breakdown to supply amino acids for gluconeogenesis and tissue repair; transamination and the urea cycle run hard, so urinary urea exceeds dietary nitrogen even with extra protein. Growing child: positive; new tissue is being built, so more nitrogen is retained than excreted. Bed-rest patient on a corticosteroid: negative. Immobilized muscle is not maintained, and corticosteroids stimulate protein breakdown and gluconeogenesis.

Check. Ask "is net body protein going up or down?" Up means positive; down means negative. The direction of the urea cycle output follows the opposite way: more urea, more negative.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Setting the number of cycles equal to the number of acetyl CoA. Cycles = acetyl CoA − 1.
  • Forgetting to subtract the 2 ATP activation cost.
  • Thinking ketone bodies are always harmful. Mild ketosis is a normal response to fasting; ketoacidosis is the dangerous extreme.
  • Calling thiolase a hydrolase. Step 4 of β-oxidation uses coenzyme A, not water, to break the carbon–carbon bond; it is a transferase. (Pancreatic lipase and arginase, which do use water, are the hydrolases in this chapter.)
  • Saying that fatty acids can be made into glucose. Acetyl CoA cannot go back to pyruvate; only the glycerol part of a fat is glucogenic.
  • Mixing up the two aminotransferase reactions. ALT: alanine ↔ pyruvate. AST: aspartate ↔ oxaloacetate. In both, the partner is α-ketoglutarate ↔ glutamate.
  • Thinking both urea nitrogens come from NH4+. One comes from ammonium (via carbamoyl phosphate); the other comes from aspartate.
  • Confusing ketosis with ketoacidosis on a lab report. Ketones alone are not a diagnosis; look at blood glucose, pH, and bicarbonate.

Practice

Exercise 1

For stearic acid (18 C), find the cycles, acetyl CoA, and total ATP.

Answer
8 cycles, 9 acetyl CoA. ATP = 90 + 8(2.5) + 8(1.5) − 2 = 90 + 20 + 12 − 2 = 120 ATP.

Exercise 2

For myristic acid (14 C), how many FADH2 are produced?

Answer
6 (one per cycle; 14/2 − 1 = 6 cycles)

Exercise 3

Name the three ketone bodies. Which one is exhaled?

Answer
Acetoacetate, β-hydroxybutyrate, and acetone. Acetone is volatile and exhaled.

Exercise 4

Why does the body convert ammonium ions to urea instead of excreting NH4+ directly?

Answer
Ammonia/ammonium is toxic, especially to the brain. Urea is non-toxic, water soluble, and safely excreted by the kidneys.

Exercise 5

How many ATP does oleic acid (18 carbons, one C=C) yield? Compare with stearic acid (120 ATP).

Answer
118.5 ATP: the same as stearic acid minus one FADH2 (1.5 ATP) for the existing double bond.

Exercise 6

A patient recovering from major burns is losing more nitrogen than they take in. What is this called, and why does it matter?

Answer
Negative nitrogen balance. Body protein is being broken down faster than it is replaced, which slows healing, so extra dietary protein is needed.

Exercise 7

Classify each enzyme by class: pancreatic lipase, acyl CoA synthetase, enoyl CoA hydratase, glutamate dehydrogenase, acetyl CoA carboxylase.

Answer
Pancreatic lipase: hydrolase (ester hydrolysis). Acyl CoA synthetase: ligase (joins fatty acid to CoA using ATP). Enoyl CoA hydratase: lyase (adds water across a C=C without ATP or oxidation). Glutamate dehydrogenase: oxidoreductase (NAD+). Acetyl CoA carboxylase: ligase (adds CO2 using ATP; biotin coenzyme).

Exercise 8

Which hub molecule do these all pass through on their way to the citric acid cycle: glucose, palmitic acid, and leucine? Which of the three could instead be used to make glucose?

Answer
All three become acetyl CoA. Only glucose itself (through pyruvate and oxaloacetate) can be made into glucose; palmitate and leucine (purely ketogenic) cannot, because acetyl CoA has no path back to pyruvate.
