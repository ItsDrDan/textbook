Multiple Choice

Problem 12.1

Which base is a purine?

  1. cytosine
  2. thymine
  3. guanine
  4. uracil
Answer
C. Guanine is a purine, with a fused double ring; cytosine, thymine, and uracil are pyrimidines, with a single ring.

Problem 12.2

DNA differs from RNA because DNA contains

  1. ribose and uracil
  2. deoxyribose and thymine
  3. ribose and thymine
  4. deoxyribose and uracil
Answer
B. DNA uses deoxyribose (no OH on the 2′ carbon) and thymine; RNA uses ribose and uracil instead.

Problem 12.3

Nucleotides in a nucleic acid are joined by

  1. peptide bonds
  2. glycosidic bonds
  3. phosphodiester bonds
  4. hydrogen bonds
Answer
C. (Hydrogen bonds hold the two strands together.)

Problem 12.4

How many hydrogen bonds form between G and C?

  1. 1
  2. 2
  3. 3
  4. 4
Answer
C. G and C pair through three hydrogen bonds, while A and T (or A and U) pair through only two.

Problem 12.5

Which enzyme unwinds the DNA double helix during replication?

  1. helicase
  2. ligase
  3. primase
  4. RNA polymerase
Answer
A. Helicase breaks the hydrogen bonds between base pairs to unzip the helix; primase, DNA polymerase, and ligase work after the strands separate.

Problem 12.6

Which RNA carries an anticodon?

  1. mRNA
  2. tRNA
  3. rRNA
  4. all of them
Answer
B. tRNA has a three-base anticodon that pairs with the matching mRNA codon, which lets it deliver the correct amino acid to the ribosome.

Problem 12.7

Which codon is a start codon?

  1. UAA
  2. AUG
  3. UGA
  4. UAG
Answer
B. AUG starts translation and also codes for methionine; UAA, UGA, and UAG are stop codons.

True or False? (If false, correct it.)

Problem 12.8

Transcription produces mRNA from a DNA template in the nucleus.

Answer
True. RNA polymerase reads the DNA template strand in the nucleus and builds a complementary mRNA, which then leaves for the ribosomes in the cytoplasm.

Problem 12.9

A frameshift mutation changes only one amino acid in the protein.

Answer
False. A frameshift (insertion or deletion) shifts every codon after it, usually changing the whole rest of the protein.

More Practice: Analyze and Compare

Problem 12.50

Which statement correctly describes the bond that links one nucleotide to the next in a DNA strand?

  1. A hydrogen bond between the bases of neighboring nucleotides
  2. A phosphate that is esterified to the 3′-OH of one sugar and the 5′-OH of the next
  3. An N-glycosidic bond between the base of one nucleotide and the sugar of the next
  4. A phosphate anhydride bond between two phosphate groups
Answer
B. The backbone is a chain of phosphodiester bonds: each phosphate forms two ester links, one to C3′ of the sugar above and one to C5′ of the sugar below. Hydrogen bonds (A) hold the two strands together, not the nucleotides in one strand. The N-glycosidic bond (C) joins a base to its own sugar. Anhydride bonds (D) are found in ADP and ATP, not in the backbone.

Problem 12.51

A nucleotide contains cytosine, a sugar with a 2′-OH, and three phosphate groups. It is

  1. dCTP, a building block for DNA replication
  2. CTP, a building block for transcription
  3. CMP, a product of RNA hydrolysis
  4. cytidine, a nucleoside
Answer
B. The 2′-OH means the sugar is ribose, so this is an RNA nucleotide; three phosphates make it the triphosphate, CTP, which RNA polymerase uses during transcription. dCTP (A) would have deoxyribose; CMP (C) has one phosphate; cytidine (D) has no phosphate at all.

Problem 12.52

Which single-base change in an mRNA is most likely to have no effect on the protein?

  1. GCA → GCG
  2. GCA → ACA
  3. UGG → UGA
  4. AUG → AUA at the start of the message
Answer
A. GCA and GCG both code for Ala, so the change is silent; third-base changes are the ones most often silent because of degeneracy. (B) is missense (Ala → Thr), (C) is nonsense (Trp → stop), and (D) destroys the start codon, so translation would not begin at that point.

Problem 12.53

Which enzyme is carried by HIV and has no counterpart in a healthy human cell, which makes it a safe drug target?

  1. DNA polymerase
  2. RNA polymerase
  3. reverse transcriptase
  4. DNA ligase
Answer
C. Reverse transcriptase copies the viral RNA genome into DNA, a step human cells never perform; drugs that inhibit it stop the virus without blocking a human enzyme. The other three enzymes are essential to the patient's own cells, so drugs against them would be toxic (that is exactly how some chemotherapy works).

Problem 12.54

Three purified nucleic acid samples were analyzed for base composition. Use the table to answer the questions.

Problem 12.55

Use the genetic code table in the Chapter Guide to decode this mRNA: 5′-CCAUGGCUCACAAGUGAUC-3′. (a) Where does translation start? (b) Write the codons and the peptide. (c) Which bases are never translated, and why?

Answer
(a) At the first AUG, which is bases 3–5. (b) Reading in threes from there: AUG GCU CAC AAG UGA → Met-Ala-His-Lys; UGA is a stop codon. (c) The two bases before AUG (CC) are upstream of the start codon, and UC after the stop codon are downstream of it; the ribosome only reads from the start codon to the first in-frame stop codon.

Problem 12.56

Compare and contrast DNA polymerase and RNA polymerase in a short table: template used, product, building blocks, direction of synthesis, need for a primer, and proofreading. Then predict which one makes more errors per base and explain one consequence.

Answer
Both read a DNA template strand 3′→5′ and build the new strand 5′→3′. DNA polymerase makes DNA from dATP, dGTP, dCTP, and dTTP; it needs an RNA primer to supply the first 3′-OH, and it proofreads (removes a wrong base before moving on). RNA polymerase makes RNA from ATP, GTP, CTP, and UTP; it starts at a promoter without a primer and does not proofread. RNA polymerase makes more errors per base. For a cell this rarely matters because an mRNA is short-lived and made in many copies; for an RNA virus, whose genome is copied by a non-proofreading polymerase, it means rapid mutation and drug or vaccine escape.

Problem 12.57

Two DNA fragments of the same length are heated slowly. Fragment 1 is 5′-ATATTAAATTAT-3′ (paired with its complement); fragment 2 is 5′-GCGGCCGCCGGC-3′ (paired with its complement). Predict which fragment's strands separate at the lower temperature and justify your prediction with the number of hydrogen bonds in each.

Answer
Fragment 1 is all A–T pairs: 12 pairs × 2 H-bonds = 24 hydrogen bonds. Fragment 2 is all G–C pairs: 12 × 3 = 36 hydrogen bonds. Fragment 1 separates at the lower temperature; it has fewer hydrogen bonds holding the strands together (and A–T-rich DNA also stacks less strongly). This is why PCR primers and probes are designed with their G + C content in mind.

Problem 12.58

The mRNA 5′-AUG CUU AAG GAU UGG UAA-3′ codes for Met-Leu-Lys-Asp-Trp. Copy the table and classify each mutation (silent, missense, nonsense, frameshift, or in-frame insertion/deletion), then give the new peptide.

More Practice: Clinical and Integrative

Problem 12.59

A nursing student learns that sickle cell hemoglobin (HbS) can be identified by hemoglobin electrophoresis, a common laboratory test in which HbS moves more slowly toward the positive electrode than normal HbA at pH 8.6. Using the mutation (Glu → Val at position 6 of the β chain) and what you know about amino acid side chains at that pH (Chapter 10), explain the difference in mobility. Then state what kind of mutation this is.

Answer
At pH 8.6 the side chain of glutamate is a carboxylate, –COO−, so each Glu contributes one negative charge. Valine's side chain is a nonpolar isopropyl group with no charge. HbS has lost two negative charges per molecule (one on each β chain), so it is less negative than HbA and is pulled toward the positive electrode more weakly. The mutation is a single-base substitution that is missense: the codon GAG (Glu) becomes GUG (Val) in the mRNA.

Problem 12.60

A pediatric nurse explains newborn screening to a parent. The heel-stick blood spot is tested for phenylketonuria (PKU), in which a mutation in the gene for phenylalanine hydroxylase leaves the enzyme non-functional. (a) Trace the path from the mutation to the missing enzyme activity using the words replication, transcription and translation. (b) The most common PKU mutation changes one base so that an arginine codon becomes a tryptophan codon. What type of change in the DNA is this, and what is its effect on the protein? (c) Why does a low-phenylalanine diet work even though the gene is still mutated?

Answer
(a) The mutated gene is copied into every cell by replication; in liver cells it is read into mRNA by transcription, so the mRNA carries the altered codon; translation then builds the enzyme with the wrong amino acid, and the altered protein cannot catalyze phenylalanine → tyrosine. (b) A point mutation (one base replaced, same number of bases). Its effect is one changed amino acid (Arg → Trp): a basic, charged side chain becomes a large nonpolar one, which disrupts the fold and the active site (a missense change). (c) The diet treats the chemistry, not the gene: with little phenylalanine coming in, little accumulates, so the brain is protected even though every cell still carries the mutation. (Aspartame, a phenylalanine-containing dipeptide ester, carries a PKU warning for the same reason.)

Problem 12.61

A dental hygienist is asked why a saliva sample can be used for a PCR test for an oral virus. (a) What two kinds of nucleic acid might the sample contain, and from where? (b) For an RNA virus, which extra enzyme must the laboratory add before PCR can copy the viral genes, and what does it do? (c) The test reports the virus as "detected" after the DNA has been doubled 30 times. Explain in one sentence why so few starting copies can be found.

Answer
(a) Human DNA from shed cheek and gum cells, plus the DNA or RNA of any viruses or bacteria present in the saliva. (b) Reverse transcriptase, the retroviral enzyme that copies RNA into DNA; PCR's DNA polymerase can only copy DNA, so the viral RNA must first be reverse-transcribed. (c) Each PCR cycle doubles the DNA, so 30 cycles multiply even a handful of copies by about 230 (about a billion), enough to detect.

Problem 12.62

A patient asks a nurse whether the mRNA in a vaccine could "get into my DNA." Answer in terms of the chemistry of this chapter: (a) where in the cell the mRNA goes and what the cell does with it; (b) which enzyme would be needed to copy RNA into DNA and why human cells cannot do it; (c) why the mRNA does not last.

Answer
(a) The mRNA enters the cytoplasm, where ribosomes translate it into one viral protein for a few days; it does not enter the nucleus, where the DNA is kept. (b) Copying RNA into DNA requires reverse transcriptase, an enzyme carried by retroviruses; human cells do not make it, so the information cannot flow backward from the mRNA into the chromosomes. (c) RNA is single-stranded and its 2′-OH makes the backbone easy to hydrolyze, and cells have enzymes (nucleases, a class of hydrolase) that break mRNA down; the message is degraded within days.

Problem 12.63

ATP is a nucleotide you will meet again in every metabolism chapter. Identify, by name and chapter, the type of bond or functional group at each of these places: (a) the link between adenine and C1′ of ribose; (b) the link between C5′ of ribose and the first phosphate; (c) the two links between phosphate groups; (d) the ring nitrogens of adenine. Which of these bonds is hydrolyzed when ATP supplies energy to a reaction?

Answer
(a) An N-glycosidic bond, the nitrogen analog of the glycosidic bond in disaccharides (Chapter 8). (b) A phosphate ester, an ester of phosphoric acid (Chapter 6). An mRNA reads 5′-AUG UCU GAU-3′. (a) Write the amino acid sequence. (b) Draw the tripeptide as it exists at physiological pH and give its net charge.

Problem 12.72

The normal coding strand of a gene reads 5′-ATG GCT TGT AAA TAG-3′. Identify the type of change in the DNA (point mutation, deletion mutation or insertion mutation) in each mutant and describe the effect on the protein: (a) 5′-ATG GCT TGT GAA TAG-3′; (b) 5′-ATG GCT TGT AAT AG-3′; (c) 5′-ATG GCT TGA AAA TAG-3′.

Answer
Normal mRNA: AUG GCU UGU AAA UAG = Met-Ala-Cys-Lys. (a) Same number of bases, A → G in the fourth codon: a point mutation; AAA (Lys) becomes GAA (Glu), so one amino acid changes from basic to acidic. (b) One base fewer (the first A of AAA is gone): a deletion mutation causing a frameshift; the mRNA now reads AUG GCU UGU AAU AG = Met-Ala-Cys-Asn-… with no stop in frame, so everything after the deletion is wrong. (c) Same number of bases, U → A in the third codon: a point mutation that creates a stop codon (UGU Cys → UGA stop); the protein is cut off after Met-Ala.

Problem 12.73

True or false? If false, correct it: "A point mutation always changes one amino acid in the protein."

Answer
False. A point mutation keeps the same number of bases, so it can be silent (the new codon codes for the same amino acid, because most amino acids have several codons), it can change one amino acid, or it can create a stop codon that cuts the protein short. Only insertions and deletions shift the reading frame.

Problem 12.74

Explain, in order, the steps by which a retrovirus such as HIV infects a cell, naming the enzyme that makes it a retrovirus and stating what is unusual about the direction of information flow.

Answer
The virus particle, which contains RNA but no DNA, attaches to a host cell and its RNA enters. The viral enzyme reverse transcriptase uses that RNA as a template to make a viral DNA strand; the DNA strand then forms its complementary strand, and the double-stranded viral DNA is inserted into a host chromosome. From there the host's own RNA polymerase, ribosomes, nucleotides and amino acids transcribe and translate the viral genes, and new virus particles assemble and leave to infect other cells. The unusual step is RNA → DNA, the reverse of the usual DNA → RNA flow of transcription.

Problem 12.75

Which bond type holds a codon to its anticodon, and which holds an amino acid to its tRNA? Why does the difference make sense for what each must do?

Answer
Codon and anticodon are held by hydrogen bonds between complementary bases, which are weak enough that the tRNA can leave the ribosome after its amino acid is transferred. The amino acid is held to the tRNA's 3′ end by a covalent aminoacyl ester bond made by the synthetase at the cost of ATP; it must stay attached until peptidyl transferase moves the growing chain onto it, and the energy stored in that ester helps pay for the new peptide bond.
