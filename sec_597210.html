Lecture Connection

The section above covers all three nitrogen-handling stages and the carbon skeletons in one sweep. The lecture splits them: transamination and oxidative deamination are classified in Section 15.2, the urea cycle gets its own table in Section 15.10, and this page is for what the lecture emphasizes here: the body cannot oxidize nitrogen-containing compounds for energy, so the N comes off first, and 20 amino acids give only 7 degradation products, every one of them a molecule you already know. That is also the best place to step back and see how carbohydrate, fat, and protein metabolism overlap.

In this course

The section above counts the cost of disposing of one nitrogen as four ATP-equivalents (one ATP is split to AMP) and then credits two NADH to arrive at a net gain. On exams, use the lecture’s figure: the urea cycle costs the equivalent of 3 ATP per urea (Section 15.10), and you are not asked to net it against NADH. The section’s “oxidative amination” in its learning objective is a slip for oxidative deamination. Reactions 4 and 5 of its first figure (other routes for ammonia) are background.

The carbon skeletons: 20 amino acids, 7 products

With the nitrogen gone, each α-keto acid is a familiar molecule or becomes one. Each amino acid has its own degradation pathway, in one or several steps, but all of them end at one of seven products: pyruvate, acetyl CoA, acetoacetyl CoA, and the citric acid cycle intermediates α-ketoglutarate, succinyl CoA, fumarate, and oxaloacetate. Skeletons that end up as pyruvate or a citric acid cycle intermediate can be made into glucose by gluconeogenesis (Section 13.10): these amino acids are glucogenic. Skeletons that end up as acetyl CoA or acetoacetyl CoA cannot become glucose (there is no path from acetyl CoA back to pyruvate) but can contribute to fatty acids or ketone bodies: these amino acids are ketogenic. A few appear in both groups, either because they degrade by two pathways or because parts of the skeleton go different ways.

Professor Tip

“L” for ketogenic: Leucine and Lysine are the only two amino acids that are purely ketogenic. Everything else can make at least some glucose.

Practice: Transamination removes alanine’s amino group and leaves pyruvate. Transamination of aspartate leaves oxaloacetate. Leucine is degraded only to acetyl CoA and acetoacetyl CoA. Classify each amino acid as glucogenic, ketogenic, or both, and say why.

Answer
Alanine: glucogenic (pyruvate → glucose by gluconeogenesis). Aspartate: glucogenic (oxaloacetate is a citric acid cycle intermediate and a gluconeogenesis starting point). Leucine: ketogenic only (acetyl CoA and acetoacetyl CoA can become ketone bodies or fat but never a net amount of glucose).

One body, one metabolism: where the pathways overlap

You have now met three chapters of pathways. Step back and notice that they share a handful of molecules. Pyruvate connects glucose, alanine (and other glucogenic amino acids), and glycerol (through DHAP). Acetyl CoA is where glucose, every fatty acid, and the ketogenic amino acids all end up, and it is the starting material for fatty acids, ketone bodies, and cholesterol. Oxaloacetate and α-ketoglutarate are both citric acid cycle intermediates and the keto-acid partners of aspartate and glutamate, so the cycle doubles as the on-ramp for amino acid carbon and the source of carbon for nonessential amino acids. One arrow is missing on purpose: there is no pathway from acetyl CoA back to pyruvate, so fatty acids cannot be turned into glucose. That single fact explains why the brain needs ketone bodies in a fast and why gluconeogenesis must run on glycerol, lactate, and amino acids.
