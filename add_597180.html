Lecture Connection

In class you will be asked to identify the type of change in the DNA, a point mutation (one base replaced by another), a deletion mutation (a base removed) or an insertion mutation (a base added), and then to predict what that does to the protein. Count the bases first: same count means a substitution; one more or one fewer means a frameshift.

Classify a mutation by its effect

In this course

The section text calls substitutions, insertions and deletions all "point mutations" because each changes a single nucleotide. In lecture, and on exams, the terms are narrower: a point mutation means one base replaced by another (a substitution); an insertion mutation adds a base and a deletion mutation removes one. Only insertions and deletions shift the reading frame. Use the lecture terms.

What causes mutations

Some are spontaneous copying errors that escape proofreading. Others are caused by mutagens:

  • Ultraviolet (UV) light makes two neighboring thymines bond covalently to each other (a thymine dimer), which the polymerase cannot read. Repair enzymes remove most dimers; the ones that escape cause skin cancer. Every sunburn is DNA damage.
  • Ionizing radiation (X-rays, gamma rays, radioactive particles) breaks the backbone directly or makes reactive radicals from water that attack the bases. Dental radiographs use ionizing radiation, which is why you follow ALARA (as low as reasonably achievable): digital sensors, collimation, thyroid collars, and radiographs only when they change the treatment plan.
  • Chemicals: nitrous acid (HNO2) deaminates the heterocyclic bases, converting an NH2 to C=O, so cytosine becomes uracil and pairs with A instead of G; polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons in tobacco smoke (Chapter 3) and aflatoxin from moldy grain become reactive after the liver oxidizes them and then bond to guanine. A chemical that causes mutations is a mutagen; one that causes cancer is a carcinogen, and most carcinogens are mutagens.

Worked examples

Example 1: The sickle-cell mutation

Problem: In the normal β-globin gene, the coding strand for codons 5–7 reads 5′-CCT GAG GAG-3′. In sickle cell disease the middle codon is CCT GTG GAG. Write both mRNAs, name the amino acids, classify the mutation, and explain why one amino acid matters so much.

Solution

Normal mRNA: 5′-CCU GAG GAG-3′ = Pro-Glu-Glu. Mutant mRNA: 5′-CCU GUG GAG-3′ = Pro-Val-Glu. One base (A → T in DNA, A → U in mRNA) replaced another, and the new codon codes for a different amino acid, so this is a substitution that is missense. Glutamate has a charged, polar side chain (–CH2CH2COO−); valine has a nonpolar isopropyl side chain. The nonpolar patch on the surface of hemoglobin S sticks to a pocket on neighboring molecules when oxygen is unloaded, and the molecules stack into fibers that deform the red cell.

Check: The frame is unchanged (still three-base codons), so only one amino acid differs, which matches a single substitution. A frameshift would have scrambled every codon after it.

Example 2: Classify the mutations

Problem: The mRNA 5′-AUG UGG CAU AAA UAG-3′ (Met-Trp-His-Lys) undergoes three different mutations. Classify each: (a) UGG → UAG; (b) CAU → CAC; (c) a G is inserted after AUG, giving AUG GUG GCA UAA AUA G.

Solution

(a) UGG (Trp) becomes UAG, a stop codon: a nonsense mutation; the peptide is just Met. (b) CAU and CAC both code for His: silent. (c) Reading in threes after the insertion: AUG GUG GCA UAA = Met-Val-Ala-stop. Every codon after the insertion changed and a new stop appeared early: a frameshift.

Check: Substitutions keep the number of bases (15) and change at most one amino acid; the insertion makes 16 bases, which is not a multiple of three, and changes everything downstream.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Many inherited diseases are single mutations that you can now read. Sickle cell disease is one missense substitution: in the gene for the β chain of hemoglobin, codon 6 changes from GAG to GTG (DNA coding strand), so the mRNA codon GAG (Glu) becomes GUG (Val). A polar, charged glutamate on the protein surface becomes a nonpolar valine; deoxygenated hemoglobin molecules stick to one another at that patch and polymerize into rods that bend the red cell into a sickle, causing hemolytic anemia and painful vaso-occlusive crises. Cystic fibrosis is most often ΔF508, a three-base deletion that removes one phenylalanine (residue 508) from the CFTR chloride-channel protein; the reading frame is intact, but the protein misfolds and is destroyed, so thick mucus clogs the airways and pancreatic ducts. Phenylketonuria (PKU) is a mutation in the gene for phenylalanine hydroxylase, the enzyme that converts phenylalanine to tyrosine (Chapter 11), so phenylalanine builds up and damages the developing brain. All three are autosomal recessive and all three are caught by newborn screening of the heel-stick blood spot (abnormal hemoglobin, elevated phenylalanine, and a CF marker confirmed by sweat chloride or DNA testing), so management such as the PKU low-phenylalanine diet can start in the first weeks of life.

Clinical Connection: Dental Hygiene

Two inherited conditions you will see on radiographs are mutations in the genes for tooth matrix proteins: amelogenesis imperfecta (defective enamel proteins; thin, pitted, discolored enamel) and dentinogenesis imperfecta (defective dentin protein; opalescent teeth). Both are examples of a change in primary structure (Chapter 10) that traces back to a change in a gene, and both are diagnosed from the family history and the radiograph rather than from a blood test.
