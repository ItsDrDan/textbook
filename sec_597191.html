Lecture Connection

The section above walks through Stage 1 for all three food classes. In this chapter the lecture cares about the carbohydrate half: where the monosaccharides are released, how they get into the blood, and what goes wrong when one enzyme is missing. The protein and lipid details return when we need them, in Sections 15.1 and 15.9, so do not try to memorize the peptidases yet.

From the brush border to the liver

Maltase, sucrase, and lactase do not float free in the intestinal fluid. They sit on the brush border, the carpet of microscopic projections (microvilli) on the surface of intestinal cells, so each disaccharide is split right where its products are absorbed. Glucose and galactose cross into the intestinal cell by active transport (energy is spent to pump them in against their gradient); fructose crosses by facilitated diffusion through a carrier protein, with no energy cost (Chapter 9). All three leave the intestinal cell, enter the portal vein, and reach the liver first. The liver is where fructose and galactose are converted into glycolysis intermediates (Section 13.7), and where the blood-glucose decisions of Section 13.8 are made.

Cellulose never gets this far. Its glucose units are joined by β(1→4) bonds, and none of the enzymes in the table of Section 13.3 can hydrolyze a β link, so cellulose passes through the gut as dietary fiber. Same monomer as starch, different bond, completely different fate: a one-line illustration of why the α/β distinction of Chapter 8 matters.

Clinical Connection: Nursing

Lactose intolerance and the lactose-free formula. In most of the world’s adults, brush-border lactase declines after early childhood; that is the normal human pattern, not a disease. When undigested lactose reaches the colon, bacteria ferment it to gas and short-chain acids, and water follows the sugar osmotically: bloating, cramping, and diarrhea within an hour or two of dairy. Lactose-free milk is ordinary milk pre-treated with lactase, so the glucose and galactose are already free; a lactose-free infant formula does the same job for a baby who cannot tolerate standard formula after a bout of gastroenteritis has temporarily stripped the brush border. Two look-alikes to keep separate: galactosemia (Section 13.7) is an inherited inability to use galactose after absorption and requires avoiding all milk sugar, lactase-treated or not; and a milk-protein allergy is an immune reaction to casein or whey, not an enzyme problem at all. When a parent says “milk upsets my baby,” the chemistry behind the complaint decides the diet.
