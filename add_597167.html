Lecture Connection

In lecture the enzyme story is E + S ↔ ES ↔ E + P, and the two questions that follow are how specific is the active site and how does the substrate fit into it (lock-and-key versus induced fit). Be ready to say which model is the better description and why.

The enzyme–substrate complex

The enzyme binds the substrate in the active site, holds it in the geometry of the transition state, releases the product, and is unchanged at the end. Both arrows are reversible, which is why an enzyme speeds a reaction in both directions and never moves the equilibrium (Section 11.1).

Specificity

A platinum catalyst in a car's exhaust system will speed up almost any oxidation. An enzyme is choosy. Its active site is a three-dimensional pocket lined with specific R groups, so only a substrate with the right shape, size, and pattern of charges and hydrogen-bonding groups can bind. Three categories of specificity, plus one more:

  • Absolute specificity: one substrate only. Urease hydrolyzes urea and nothing else.
  • Group specificity: one type of substrate, that is, molecules sharing a functional group. Hexokinase phosphorylates several hexoses (glucose, fructose, mannose).
  • Linkage specificity: one type of bond, whatever molecule it is in. Lipases hydrolyze ester bonds; proteases hydrolyze peptide bonds; trypsin cuts a peptide bond only after lysine or arginine.
  • Enzymes are also specific with respect to stereochemistry: they distinguish enantiomers. Your enzymes digest D-glucose and build proteins from L-amino acids; the mirror images are ignored. (This is why chirality, Chapter 5, matters in biochemistry and in drug design.)

In this course

The section text describes the same three levels of specificity (a single substrate, a group of related molecules, a type of bond) without naming them. On exams use the lecture names: absolute, group and linkage specificity, plus stereospecificity for enzymes that tell D from L.

Lock-and-key versus induced fit

In the lock-and-key model (Emil Fischer, 1894) the active site is a rigid cavity, and to react the substrate must exactly match its shape, like a key in a lock. In the induced-fit model (Daniel Koshland, 1958) the active site has a flexible shape that adjusts to fit the substrate: it is a close but imperfect match, and binding the substrate makes the enzyme shift its shape to wrap around it. The induced-fit model is the better description: the shape change is what squeezes the substrate into its transition-state geometry, and it explains how one enzyme can accept several related substrates.

Professor Tip

The active site is built from R groups that are far apart in the sequence but brought together by the fold, so it is tertiary structure (Chapter 10) that makes an enzyme work. That one fact explains the whole next section: anything that disturbs the fold (heat, wrong pH) disturbs the active site, and the rate falls.
