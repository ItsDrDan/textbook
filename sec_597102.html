Lecture Connection

The lecture's first job with carbonyls is reading: tell an aldehyde (C=O at the end of a chain, written –CHO) from a ketone (C=O inside the chain, –CO–), and tell both from the alcohols and ethers of Chapter 4. Then name them, and when a molecule carries two groups, let the higher-priority group pick the suffix.

Reading condensed formulas: carbon needs 4, oxygen needs 2

Carbon needs four bonds and oxygen needs two. CH3CH2CHO can only be an aldehyde: the last carbon has an H, a C and one bond left over, which must be a double bond to O. CH3CH2COCH3 is a ketone, not an ether, because the C between the two carbons has only one bond left for oxygen, and oxygen needs two, so it must be C=O. In a line structure the aldehyde H is sometimes drawn and sometimes not; the C=O at the end of the chain is the clue. The source text's rule that H is written after the atom it is attached to is why an aldehyde is –CHO and never –COH (which would read as an alcohol).

Example: Aldehyde, ketone, alcohol or ether?

Problem. Classify each condensed formula and identify the functional group: (a) CH3CH2CH2CH2CHO; (b) CH3COCH2CH2CH3; (c) CH3CH2OCH2CH3; (d) CH3CH2CH(OH)CH3.

Solution. Count bonds. (a) The final carbon has one C, one H and one O; to reach four bonds the C–O must be a double bond: a C=O with an H on it, an aldehyde (pentanal). (b) "CO" between two carbons: that carbon has two C neighbors and one O, so again the O is double-bonded: a ketone (2-pentanone). (c) The O sits between two carbons with no H; it uses both of its bonds on carbon: an ether (diethyl ether). (d) "OH" on a carbon that also has an H and two carbons: a 2° alcohol (2-butanol).

Check. Formulas (a) and (b) are both C5H10O, so the aldehyde and ketone are functional group isomers; so are the ether and the alcohol (both C4H10O). Only (a) would give a silver mirror with Tollens' reagent (Section 5.4).

Naming aldehydes and ketones

Aldehydes. (1) Find the longest carbon chain containing the CHO group. (2) Change the -e of the parent alkane to -al. (3) The C of the CHO is C1, so it never gets a number. (4) Apply all the other nomenclature rules for substituents. CH3CH(CH3)CH(CH3)CHO is 2,3-dimethylbutanal. Simple aldehydes also have common names ending in -aldehyde: formaldehyde (methanal), acetaldehyde (ethanal). The aldehyde of benzene is benzaldehyde, which is its IUPAC name as well.

Ketones. (1) Find the longest chain containing the C=O. (2) Change the -e to -one. (3) Number the chain to give the C=O the lowest number; include the number unless only one position is possible (propanone, butanone). (4) Apply all other rules. A six-carbon ring with the C=O in it is cyclohexanone; the C=O carbon is C1, and substituents are numbered from there: 3-ethyl-4-methylcyclohexanone. Common names: acetone (propanone), the solvent in nail-polish remover; the source text's "alkyl alkyl ketone" names (ethyl methyl ketone) work like ether common names.

Naming molecules with more than one functional group

Many biological molecules carry two or more functional groups: lactic acid is an alcohol and a carboxylic acid; the ketone bodies of Chapter 15 are ketones with an acid or a second alcohol. IUPAC settles this with one rule: the highest-priority group gets the suffix and decides the numbering (it gets the lowest possible number). Generally, the more oxidized functional group (more C–O bonds) takes priority. Every other, lower-priority group becomes a prefix, listed alphabetically with its own locant alongside the substituent names. In this chapter the decisions you will face most are aldehyde vs. ketone (aldehyde wins; the ketone becomes oxo-), carbonyl vs. alcohol (carbonyl wins; the alcohol becomes hydroxy-), and carbonyl vs. ether or halogen (ethers and halogens are always prefixes). Examples: CH3COCH2CH2OH is 4-hydroxy-2-butanone (ketone outranks alcohol, so the chain is numbered from the end nearer the C=O). CH3CH(OCH3)CH2CHO is 3-methoxybutanal. The Chapter Guide works two more of these in its Worked Examples.

Professor Tip

Two numbering traps. An aldehyde never gets a locant (butanal, not 1-butanal), because its carbon is always C1. A ketone with a lower-priority group is still numbered from the end nearer the C=O: 4-hydroxy-2-pentanone, never 2-hydroxy-4-pentanone. Decide the suffix first, then number.
